Water from a Deep Well

Christian spirituality from early martyrs to modern missionaries
Gerald L. Sittser · foreword by Eugene H. Peterson

A church historian lowers a bucket into two thousand years of Christian life and brings up eleven traditions, each with one word: witness, belonging, struggle, rhythm, holy heroes, windows, union, ordinariness, Word, conversion, risk. Each chapter tells the stories (Polycarp in the arena, Antony in the desert, Benedict's Rule, Julian's visions, Luther's tower, Newton's slave ship, Mary Slessor in Calabar), then asks what we can learn and what went wrong. His conviction runs through it all: there is more, so much more than the shallow, one-generation faith we often settle for.

IVP, 2007 · 11 chapters + conclusion · each chapter ends with Bible readings and a practice · Sittser teaches at Whitworth University and wrote A Grace Disguised

Why history

There is more

Sittser opens with the book he rereads every year, Augustine's Confessions, and its famous cry: late have I loved you, beauty so ancient and so new. Augustine is one saint among hundreds whose voices say to us across the centuries: you are not alone, you don't know it all, you haven't exhausted the faith.

  • Every generation is blind to itself. C. S. Lewis's case for reading old books: only the "clean sea breeze of the centuries" shows us our own blind spots.
  • The story didn't stop at Acts. God's redemption story continues through church history, and it is our family history. Peterson's foreword: we are not sprinters but a relay team.
  • Abusus non tollit usus: abuse doesn't cancel right use. Icons can be gaudy, sacraments formal, Reformation doctrine heady, revivalism shallow. Each still holds a gift. Sittser tells both sides in every chapter.
  • Look along, not only at. Lewis's "Meditation in a Toolshed": you understand a tradition by standing in its beam of light, not only by examining it. Augustine: to understand something, love it first.
  • One truth underneath. However diverse, almost all Christians have confessed that God has revealed himself in Jesus Christ, fully God and fully human, and that God is a communion of love: Father, Son and Spirit.

His students spend a January month with him at Tall Timber, a camp in the Cascades with no phones, following a simplified Benedictine rhythm. At first the saints strike them as strange. By the end the strangeness fascinates them and the saints have become family.

“All things are yours, whether Paul or Apollos or Cephas.” (1 Corinthians 3:21-22)

Chapter 1 · Witness

The early martyrs

Sittser starts in the Roman arena, because you can't understand Christian spirituality without martyrdom. The New Testament speaks of persecution so often it sounds like static on a radio. The Greek martys means witness: the martyrs were not heroes proving something to God but witnesses to the life they had received.

Why Rome persecuted good citizens

Christians committed no crimes; they prayed for the emperor. So why? Sittser gives four reasons, rising in importance:

A strange foreign cult. Pliny called it a depraved, excessive superstition.
A way of life that judged Rome's: no games, no idols, no exposing of babies.
Allegiance to Christ as Lord threatened the emperor's claim.
Jesus as the only Savior. Rome tolerated any god except one who claimed to be the only one.

Polycarp, aged 86

Proconsul: Swear by Caesar's fortune. Revile Christ, and I'll release you.

Polycarp: For eighty-six years I have served him and he has never done me wrong. How can I blaspheme my King who saved me?

Proconsul: I have wild beasts. And if you make light of them, I have fire.

Polycarp: Your fire burns for an hour and goes out. Why wait? Do what you will.

Perpetua, a young noblewoman with a nursing baby, refused her pleading father in court: she could not be called anything but what she was, a Christian. She walked into the arena "as though going to heaven". Sittser, father of a daughter her age, admits her story troubles him. Justin Martyr became a Christian after watching martyrs die, and later died as one himself.

The charges against Polycarp ("teacher of Asia, father of the Christians, destroyer of our gods") sound modern. Rome's pluralism resembles ours. And martyrdom is not past: more Christians die for their faith today than ever.

What it gives us

Faith is witness to Jesus as Lord, costly or not. Chesterton said Francis of Assisi turned martyrdom into a way of life: dying daily to self.

What to avoid

Glorifying or seeking martyrdom for its own sake, or using it to justify violence.

Practice: read Philippians 3:2-16, meditate on your own death, and write your own obituary as you'd want it to read if Jesus is Lord of your life. What does it say about today's choices?

Chapter 2 · Belonging

Early Christian community

In 112, the governor Pliny wrote to Emperor Trajan, puzzled by a "contagion" that spread through cities and villages: people who met before dawn, sang to Christ "as to a god", and swore to commit no crime. The church grew for three hundred years without power, largely because it gave people a place to belong.

  • A peculiar people. The Letter to Diognetus: Christians live in their own countries "but only as aliens". Ordinary on the outside, strangely different within. Even the pagan emperor Julian complained that Christians cared for pagan poor as well as their own.
  • A welcoming community. The critic Celsus sneered that the church recruited slaves, women and children. Women in particular found higher status and protection there; the church refused infanticide, honored marriage and supported widows (Rome's church cared for 1,500 needy people by 250).
  • Stability in unstable cities. Cities like Antioch were crowded, filthy and violent. The church became family for migrants and strangers, feeding and burying the poor.
  • Courage in the plagues. When epidemics swept the empire, pagans fled their sick relatives; Christians stayed, nursed them and washed the dead, and many died doing it. Bishop Dionysius saw their deaths as a share in Christ's sacrifice. Christians even survived at higher rates: basic nursing (broth, cool cloths, clean bedding) saves lives, and survivors became immune caregivers.
  • Pastors from cradle to grave. Long training before baptism (Hippolytus), clear moral teaching (the Didache's "two ways"), worship, sacraments and personal care.

Sittser served as a young associate pastor in a small, unremarkable church in Southern California and found that belonging there. The church's calling hasn't changed: to be a community of belonging for broken people, to respond to catastrophe with compassion, and for leaders to do less and surrender the hunger for influence.

Practice: read John 13:34-35, Acts 2:42-47 and 1 Corinthians 12. Pick one community you belong to (a small group, a class, a team), describe what it is now, name one step to make it a place of real belonging, and pray for it daily.

Chapter 3 · Struggle

The desert saints

His students talk about their "struggles", meaning things that happen to them. The desert saints of fourth-century Egypt, Palestine and Syria chose struggle. When Constantine made Christianity safe and even fashionable, serious believers went into the desert to become "bloodless martyrs".

  • Antony heard "sell all you have" read in church, gave away his inheritance and spent decades in the desert fighting the devil. His friend Athanasius wrote his life, and it became a bestseller. Their eccentricity works like a cartoon: it exaggerates to show what matters.
  • Why the desert? It strips away distraction and makes you face yourself. "Go, sit in your cell, and your cell will teach you everything."
  • The darkness within. Evagrius named eight logismoi, tempting thoughts: gluttony, impurity, avarice, sadness, anger, acedia (restless boredom), vainglory and pride. They later became the seven deadly sins. He treated them as motives, not just acts.
  • Moderation, not heroics. Abba Poemen: eat a little less than you want, every day; that is "the king's highway". The elders said that if you see a young man climbing to heaven by his own will, grab his foot and pull him down.
  • The goal was love. Struggle was the means; apatheia (inner calm), humility and agape were the goal. Abbot Anastasius' precious Bible was stolen; he didn't chase the thief, so the brother wouldn't add perjury to theft. When a buyer brought it to him to ask its value, he only said it was a fine book, worth the price. Hearing that the old man had said nothing more, the thief came back in tears and begged him to take it back. Anastasius tried to give it to him as a gift; the brother stayed with him for the rest of his life.

What it gives us

Modern culture is unfriendly to the soul: busy, noisy, hungry for results (Merton). Solitude and discipline let us see our own darkness and resist it.

What to avoid

Fanaticism and contempt for the body, marriage and sex. Some desert saints went there; the wisest pulled them back.

Practice: start modestly (an hour of silence in an empty church, half a day of prayer). Then name one appetite that rules you and fast from it for a while; in its place memorize Scripture and pray for people who lack what you crave.

Chapter 4 · Rhythm

Monasticism

This chapter is personal. After the tragedy that took his wife, his mother and his young daughter (the story of A Grace Disguised), Sister Florence invited Sittser into her monastery's life. For years he sat in a hermitage on a wooded hillside: in silence, weeping, praying, wrestling with God. "My visits to a monastery saved my life," he writes. It was the rhythm that held him.

  • Rhythm is biblical. Six days and a seventh; Passover and the feasts; the early church's daily prayer and breaking of bread.
  • Roots. Pachomius, a conscripted soldier moved by Christians' kindness, founded the first monasteries in Egypt. Basil the Great grounded Eastern monasticism in community and service: hard work for everyone, and hostels, soup kitchens and hospitals for the poor and the contagious.
  • Benedict's Rule (around 530). Moderate and humane, written for ordinary monks as the Roman world collapsed: prayer (the Divine Office, eight short services a day, from matins at 2 a.m. to compline), work (everyone takes kitchen duty) and study (lectio divina). Three inner works: silence, obedience and humility (in twelve steps). And stability: staying put, which heals our restless self-will.
  • Impact and decline. Monasteries became Europe's schools, libraries, hospitals and farms. Cluny grew rich and corrupt; the Cistercians reformed it. The movement peaked in the twelfth century, but thousands of monasteries still keep the rhythm.
Prayer and work need each other. Without work, prayer becomes empty and irrelevant; without prayer, work becomes an idol. Sittser recently went back to the monastery after years away. He no longer needed healing, but he realized his habit is to work at the expense of prayer.

A monastic rhythm without a monastery: do work (jobs, chores, hospitality) with excellence and humility; rise half an hour earlier to pray over the day; keep regular family meals and devotions; pray with your spouse at bedtime; gather friends for a weekly meal and Bible study.

Practice: read Luke 6:12-19. Map out a typical week and redesign it toward a healthier rhythm of work, prayer and rest.

Chapter 5 · Holy heroes

Icons and saints

Eastern Orthodoxy, the least familiar tradition to most Western readers, centers on deification (theosis): we are being transformed from glory to glory into Christ's likeness (2 Corinthians 3:18). Saints show what that looks like. Sittser uses three windows.

  • An icon: Macrina the Younger, sister of Basil and Gregory of Nyssa. Icons look "unreal" on purpose: they paint a person already transfigured. Their perspective is reversed: the vanishing point lies in front of the picture, so the saint looks out at us. We are no longer the observers; we are the ones being observed and invited in.
  • A biography: Melania the Younger (around 383–438), a Roman heiress married at fourteen. After both her children died, she and her husband Pinian gave away one of the largest fortunes in the empire and founded monasteries in Jerusalem. Her life is meant to show transformation step by step, but she is no typical believer.
  • A voice: John Chrysostom, the "golden-mouthed" preacher of Antioch and Constantinople. He preached tenderly on God's mercy (it is never too late to repent) and fiercely to the rich: filling your wardrobes while the poor freeze is madness, and the images from the theater keep playing in your soul long after the show. His preaching made enemies; he died in exile, forced to march in old age.

C. S. Lewis: God intends to make us "dazzling, radiant, immortal creatures". Peter Kreeft, in a paragraph Sittser memorized: earth is not outside heaven; it is heaven's workshop. Marriage, kids, interruptions, problems and suffering are the tools; God is the sculptor.

Ask daily: what is God trying to do in me through this?

Practice: read Luke 9:28-36 and 2 Corinthians 3. Gaze at an icon and imagine your future glory. Which circumstances is God using to change you, and how can you submit to him in them?

Chapter 6 · Windows

The sacraments

Sittser and his wife visited Chartres on their honeymoon. Gothic cathedrals were built around two things: height that pulls the eye upward, and light (in medieval theology, the image of God's revelation) pouring through stained glass. Everything points to the altar, where the sacraments were celebrated.

  • Why matter matters. Many religions try to leave the material world behind. Christianity can't, because God became flesh: Jesus is "the image of the invisible God". Water, bread, wine and oil can become windows onto grace, though always with the risk of idolatry.
  • Seven sacraments across a life. Baptism at birth (babies were baptized at home immediately), confirmation, penance, the Eucharist, marriage or holy orders, and last rites. From cradle to grave the church surrounded ordinary life with grace.
  • Pilgrimage, relics and the church year. Egeria's fourth-century journal of Holy Week in Jerusalem; the road to Santiago de Compostela; bones of martyrs believed to carry power; a calendar running from Advent through Easter to Pentecost that made time itself tell the gospel.

What it gives us

The gospel is objective: rooted in real events, and grace comes as a real gift, not a feeling we produce. As his Presbyterian congregation files forward for communion, Sittser thinks of the stories he knows behind their faces. And we can become living sacraments to the world.

What to avoid

Superstition, the relic trade, a faith that is purely external, and clergy who control grace.

Practice: before a communion Sunday, fast for twenty-four hours, spend Saturday evening reading all of Mark, rise early to worship alone, receive the bread and cup repeating Romans 8:1, then feast with family or friends.

Chapter 7 · Union

The mystics

By temperament, Sittser says, the mystical way is the most foreign to him. Yet it surprises even the rational: on 6 December 1273, during Mass, Thomas Aquinas, the greatest theologian of the Middle Ages, had an experience he could not describe and stopped writing. "All that I have written seems to me like straw."

  • Roots and a danger. Plato and Plotinus pictured the soul returning to an impersonal One, dissolving into it. Through Pseudo-Dionysius this flowed into Christian mysticism, sometimes leaving even Christ behind as a mere step. Sittser favors the mystics who seek a union of relationship, not absorption.
  • The three ways. Purgation: John Climacus's Ladder of Divine Ascent, thirty rungs of rooting out vice. Illumination: Bonaventure's Soul's Journey into God, seeing God outside us in creation, within us in the image, and above us, all through Christ crucified. Union: a love relationship with God through Jesus.
  • Bernard of Clairvaux's four degrees of love.
1. Loving self for self's sake
2. Loving God for self's sake: for what he gives
3. Loving God for God's sake: for who he is
4. Loving even self for God's sake
  • Julian of Norwich asked for an illness and received sixteen visions of Christ's passion. Asked what it all meant, she heard: "Love was his meaning." God longs for us; even in doubt and sin she felt secure because she knew she was loved. All shall be well.
  • Two kinds of prayer. Petition, modeled on the Lord's Prayer, simply invites God to act. Contemplative prayer is wordless rest in God. John of the Cross: we don't reach it by effort but through darkness, when God strips our feelings. We can't force union, but we can practise silence, which first exposes how trivial our thoughts are.

Practice: one week, pray the Lord's Prayer petition by petition, pausing to pray each one specifically. The next week, sit in silence morning and evening, still the soul, and let the Spirit pray "with sighs too deep for words".

Chapter 8 · Ordinariness

The medieval laity

Most believers are not martyrs, monks or mystics. The medieval system ranked people: monks prayed, knights fought, peasants worked, and laypeople depended on monks for salvation (Cluny believed its prayers carried the whole of society). Then, from 1200, towns, trade and a profit economy created a new class that wanted a faith for the street, the shop and the family.

  • The mendicants. Francis of Assisi, a rich merchant's son, embraced poverty, rebuilt churches and went back into the world to preach. Crowds ran to him. His Canticle of Brother Sun praises God through sun, moon, wind, water, fire and even "Sister Bodily Death". Dominic founded the Order of Preachers to teach.
  • Lay movements. Lay saints raised up by the people themselves; the Beguines, women living devout lives without vows; third-order members attached to the friars while keeping families and jobs; the Brethren of the Common Life, who copied manuscripts and produced Thomas à Kempis's Imitation of Christ: what good is debating the Trinity if you lack humility and displease the Trinity?
  • The Reformers finished the turn. No part of life lies outside God. Luther told husbands to see their wife as "the best little creature of God" in their arms and urged monks to marry. Calvin called every job a "sentry post" where God assigns us to serve.

Three ways to live this today: see ordinary life as God's domain; devote our resources (time, money, home) to his work; and practise "secular" disciplines. Mazhar Mallouhi, a Syrian writer who came to Christ by reading the Gospels after Gandhi pointed him to Jesus, practises hospitality: hundreds of people a week passed through his home in Cairo, from Muslim sheikhs and rabbis to priests, communists and expatriates.

The saint needed today is the secular saint: the parent, teacher, coach, lawyer, neighbor, executive.

Practice: read Matthew 6:25-34, Romans 12:1-13 and Colossians 3:12-17. Each morning, picture God present in your ordinary day (commute, meetings, chores). Each evening, thank him for the good he did in it.

Chapter 9 · Word

The Reformers

Sittser has heard some three thousand sermons and remembers few. In the Reformation, preaching was an event. Wittenberg held three services every Sunday plus weekday sermons; Luther preached thousands of times, as did Calvin, Bucer, Zwingli and Knox.

  • Luther's journey. A terrified monk who confessed for hours and found no peace. Studying Romans 1:17 "night and day", he saw that the righteousness of God is a gift received by faith, and felt "born again… through open gates into paradise." In 1517 he posted ninety-five theses against indulgences. At Worms he would not recant unless convinced by Scripture and plain reason. For him, faith is a wedding ring: Christ takes our sin and death, we receive his righteousness. The gospel takes us "outside ourselves".
  • Calvin's journey. A scholar converted around 1533, he was detoured through Geneva and pressed into service, expelled, spent his happiest three years as a pastor in Strasbourg, and was called back. His insight: God accommodates himself to us, "lisping" like a nurse to a baby; Christ is the perfect accommodation; and the Spirit's inner testimony lets us hear Scripture as God's voice.
  • Preaching is the Word of God. Both Luther and Calvin believed that when the Word is faithfully proclaimed, God himself speaks. So the Reformers prepared hard, studied rhetoric, and spoke to ordinary people. Luther's sermons are full of comfort: there is no greater consolation than that Christ became a baby at his mother's breast.
  • Pastors first. Calvin was loved as a friend and counselor. When his wife Idelette died, he wrote that it was a very cruel thing, and that he tried to master his grief.

For us: learn the whole biblical story by reading it again and again; recover the art of listening to sermons as God's address (and if they are weak, speak to the pastor privately and humbly); and apply the Word to all of life, because it points to the living Word, Jesus.

Practice: for two months, read the big story (Genesis 1–11, 12–24, 37–50, Exodus 1–15, and on through the Gospels and Acts). Then for two months, memorize and meditate on key New Testament passages: Matthew 5:1-12, Romans 5:1-5 and 8:1-8 and others.

Chapter 10 · Conversion

Evangelicals

This is Sittser's own tradition, so he writes as an insider who knows its strengths and weaknesses. The key text is Jesus and Nicodemus: you must be born again.

John Newton: a sailor's son who became a press-ganged sailor and then a slave trader, abused and humiliated on the coast of Africa. On a storm-battered voyage in 1748 he began a slow turn to God (he had been reading The Imitation of Christ). He kept captaining slave ships for years, became a customs officer, then pastor of Olney, then of a London church. There he mentored William Wilberforce, who in turn pushed him to speak publicly against slavery. "Amazing Grace" is his testimony.

Three roots of evangelical conversion

  • The Puritans: conversion as a process. Bunyan's Pilgrim's Progress: Christian loses his burden at the cross, but that is only the start of the long road to the Celestial City.
  • The Pietists: conversion to holiness. Philipp Jakob Spener called a dry Lutheran church to small groups, Bible reading and changed lives.
  • The Moravians: conversion to the world. Count Zinzendorf took in refugees at Herrnhut and, after meeting a former slave in Copenhagen, sent missionaries to the Caribbean and around the globe.

What makes it real?

Jonathan Edwards watched revival sweep Northampton and then fade amid scandal and conflict. His answer in Religious Affections: true religion lies largely in holy affections, love for God's beauty for its own sake, visible over time "like heat and light from a fire". His own church later dismissed him.

After Edwards came the techniques: Whitefield's open-air preaching, John Wesley's heart "strangely warmed" at Aldersgate and his lay-led class meetings, Finney's "new measures" to produce results. Today the movement is global; Sittser saw it in Kenya and among Kurdish believers in Iraq.

What it gives us

Personal faith, energy for mission, Scripture in everyone's hands, and conversion that touches the whole of life.

What to avoid

Reducing conversion to one emotional moment, or to a technique that manufactures decisions.

Practice: read John 3:1-21 and meditate on Romans 12:1-2 every day for a week. Lewis: God doesn't want something from us; he wants us.

Chapter 11 · Risk

Pioneer missionaries

Christianity began as a missionary faith, adapting to each culture (Jew to Gentile, then Germanic tribes, then the colonial age, now a global church). In 1800 about 2% of the world's Christians were non-European; when Sittser wrote, close to 70%.

  • Devotion: Ignatius and the Jesuits. A wounded would-be knight wrote the Spiritual Exercises to free the soul from disordered attachments and form missionaries for life on the move. Jean de Brébeuf served the Huron for years, warned eager recruits that the work would be brutal, urged them to love the Huron as people ransomed by Christ's blood, and was tortured to death by the Iroquois.
  • Sacrifice: C. T. Studd. England's star cricketer joined the "Cambridge Seven" for China, gave away his entire inheritance like his hero Hudson Taylor, served in China and India, then at nearly fifty sailed alone for "the heart of Africa", and preached a "reckless Christianity". Sittser is honest: his drive cost his family and coworkers dearly.
  • Crossing cultures: Mary Slessor. A Dundee mill girl with an alcoholic father, she went to Calabar (Nigeria), lived barefoot among the Okoyong and rescued twins left to die. It took ten years for the first convert and fifteen for the first communion. She became a bridge between worlds, at home in neither.
  • Translation: William Carey. A poor shoemaker with little schooling taught himself languages and sailed for India: "Expect great things from God; attempt great things for God." He studied the culture before trying to win it and translated the Bible into many languages.
  • Single-minded devotion: David Brainerd among Native Americans, and Jim Elliot, killed in Ecuador.

Small risks every day. None of them knew where their first step would lead. Risk can start small: invite an unchurched neighbor to dinner, befriend an international student, volunteer, give sacrificially. God himself took the greatest risk in the incarnation.

Practice: read Matthew 28:16-20. Name a need that has been on your heart. What is one step you can take, and how can your church help?

Conclusion

Where do we go from here?

Sittser deliberately avoids turning the book into techniques. Western culture loves self-help; we treat spirituality like fitness, as if the right method guarantees results. Disciplines matter ("train yourself in godliness"), but they only put us in position to receive.

  • The Source. The eleven traditions are like light refracted into a rainbow; the light itself is Jesus Christ. Dorothy Day, a radical journalist jailed for protest, found in Christ what communism lacked: love for the poor as those nearest to God. Daily Mass and prayer sustained her decades of work with the Catholic Worker.
  • The landscape: ordinary life. Brother Lawrence in the monastery kitchen: holiness doesn't depend on changing our work but on doing for God what we usually do for ourselves. Susanna Wesley, twenty-fifth child of a Puritan family and mother of nineteen (ten survived, among them John and Charles), survived two house fires and a difficult husband, taught her children and led a Bible study in her home.
  • Daily work. Jeremiah Evarts, a Yale-trained lawyer, spent his last fifteen years defending the Cherokees against removal. He lost; Congress passed the act, and he died of exhaustion the next year. Still, he had kept his post.
  • The church. We can't do it alone. Individuals lack the strength to change a culture; a faithful community can move it a little.
  • Hope. Desmond Tutu kept hoping through apartheid because nothing was deader than Jesus on Good Friday. We live between the "already" and the "not yet".

    Takeaways

    Ten things to keep

    1. There is more. Two thousand years of believers are your family. Read old books to see your own blind spots.
    2. Abuse does not cancel use. Every tradition has distortions; take the gift and avoid the excess.
    3. Witness: Jesus is Lord, and that confession costs something. Live it daily, die to self daily.
    4. Belonging: the early church grew because it loved strangers, the poor and the sick. The church's job is still to be a home for broken people.
    5. Struggle and rhythm: fight the darkness within with moderate, steady discipline; weave prayer and work together so neither becomes empty or an idol.
    6. Glory: earth is heaven's workshop. Ask what God is doing in you through this.
    7. Windows: because God became flesh, ordinary things (bread, wine, water, a cathedral's light) can carry grace.
    8. Union: love grows by degrees, from loving God for his gifts to loving him for himself. Love was his meaning.
    9. Ordinariness and Word: your job is a sentry post; listen to Scripture as God speaking.
    10. Conversion and risk: a whole life turned toward God, and willing to take the next small step into the unknown.
