Prologue & introduction

Part 1914, part 1941, part 2022

In the third week of October 2021, a stack of intelligence reached the White House that one official called the "mother lode": intercepts, cyber penetrations and human sources showing that Putin planned to take all of Ukraine. Biden sent CIA director Bill Burns to warn him. Putin, isolated in Sochi, took the call and changed nothing.

Hours before the tanks moved, it was Microsoft that spotted Russian wiper malware in Ukrainian ministries and warned the White House. Then almost nothing went to plan. Kyiv held, and within a year the war mixed trenches, nuclear threats and AI-guided drones.

Sanger argues that for three decades after the Berlin Wall, Washington assumed trade, the internet and self-interest would pull Russia and China into a Western-designed order. Five presidents made that bet, each with less conviction. It failed, and America now faces two rivalries at once, more tangled than the old Cold War.

Part I · Chapters 1–4

Dreaming of one world

Every president from Clinton to Trump believed he could bend Moscow and Beijing westward. Sanger traces the evidence each explained away.

01Russia

The romance on the Neva

Washington spent the 2000s waiting for Putin to join the West. He spent them collecting grievances.

  • A staged friendship. In May 2002 Bush and Putin cruised past the Hermitage, served by Putin's caterer, Yevgeny Prigozhin. Officials talked of Russia joining the WTO, then perhaps the EU, maybe NATO.
  • The grievances piled up. NATO admitted Poland, Hungary and the Czech Republic in March 1999, then bombed Serbia two weeks later without UN approval. Iraq and the "color revolutions" followed, which Putin read as CIA rehearsals for toppling him.
  • He said it out loud. At Munich in 2007 Putin accused America of using force without limits. In 2008 NATO vaguely promised Georgia and Ukraine eventual membership with no path to it, the worst of both options in Sanger's view. Russia invaded Georgia that August and paid almost no price.
  • Reset and rupture. Obama's "reset" produced New START, capping each side at 1,550 deployed warheads. US intelligence later traced Putin's hatred of Hillary Clinton, and the 2016 hacking, to her criticism of Russia's 2011 election.
"Russia was never ours to lose."
Bill Burns, March 2019, on the "who lost Russia" debate
02China

The engagement bet

Trade, the theory went, would make China richer, freer and less dangerous. It made China richer.

  • The deal. Reformer Zhu Rongji toured US cities in 1999 lobbying American CEOs, and the WTO agreement closed that November. China's income per head nearly tripled by 2007 and tripled again by 2017.
  • What was explained away. Systematic theft, from F-35 plans to a jet engine. Around 2010–12 China rolled up about two dozen CIA sources. In 2015 its spy ministry was found to have taken clearance files on 22 million Americans. Xi promised not to militarise new islands in the South China Sea, then built hangars on them.
  • Made in China 2025. The 2015 plan targeted 70% self-sufficiency in key technologies, backed by a $250 billion fund. Obama's officials grasped the threat to chipmakers with months left, and the White House even barred the phrase "great power competition".
  • Three explanations. A master plan all along (Rush Doshi), the collision any rising power brings (Graham Allison's Thucydides Trap), or Xi personally. Avril Haines suggests it was unknowable until Xi acted.
03Crimea

Putin's seven-year itch

Each land grab cost Putin so little that the next one looked affordable.

  • Outbid. In 2013 Yanukovich asked Europe for $160 billion and was offered under $1 billion with anticorruption strings. Russia paid $15 billion. Protests followed, Yanukovich fled, and troops without insignia took Crimea.
  • Tepid responses. Obama refused Javelins, believing Russia would always care more about Ukraine. Two Minsk ceasefires collapsed as the Donbas war killed about 14,000 people over eight years.
  • The reward. In 2015 Western firms agreed with Gazprom on Nord Stream 2, a $10-billion-plus pipeline under the Baltic. Russia already supplied over half of Germany's gas imports, and Ukraine stood to lose up to $3 billion a year in transit fees. Sanger reads Putin's lesson as: invade, wait a year, and business returns.
  • A caution. H. R. McMaster warns against "strategic narcissism", assuming American choices decide everything. Earlier Javelins might not have stopped 2022.

Grab, low price, repeat

A senior British official described the pattern to Sanger: every seven or eight years Putin moves on another piece of the old empire. The yellow bars rank the West's response by severity and are illustrative, not measured.

04Trump

A strategy the president ignored

Trump's aides wrote down the new era of great-power rivalry. Trump himself mostly wanted a trade deal.

  • The 2017 strategy. H. R. McMaster's national security strategy declared that complacency had let rivals advance. Trump launched it while praising partnership with Russia and China.
  • Trade as the whole relationship. Trump judged China by the goods deficit. The Phase One deal's promised $200 billion in extra purchases apparently never happened, and he offered to drop charges against a Huawei executive for a bigger deal.
  • The Rosetta Stone. A secret 2013 Xi speech, published in 2019, foresaw a long struggle socialism would win. Matt Pottinger read it as a plan to displace America. Keith Krach's team won TSMC's $12 billion Arizona commitment and tightened rules on Huawei, groundwork Biden reused.
  • Ukraine as leverage. Trump's July 2019 call tying aid to investigations of Biden led to impeachment. Putin's takeaway, Sanger says, was that Americans didn't care about Ukraine.
Part II · Chapters 5–11

The arc of history

Biden promised competence and repaired alliances. His first year brought a Russian hack, ransomware, Kabul, a Chinese weapons surprise and the realisation that Putin meant to invade.

05Transition

A hack, a riot and no handover

Putin and Xi did not wait for the new president to settle in, and the old one did not help him.

  • SolarWinds. In November 2020 FireEye noticed an impostor had registered a second phone to an employee's account and stolen its hacking tools. Kevin Mandia traced the break-in to SolarWinds network software. Russia's SVR had slipped malicious code into updates of a product used by some 18,000 customers, including federal agencies. Trump suggested it might be China.
  • January 6 as propaganda. Russia broadcast the Capitol riot abroad; China showed it mainly at home, as proof that democracy breeds chaos.
  • No handover. Trump's team blocked transition briefings. Biden's inaugural speech never mentioned China or Russia.
06China policy

The fever dream ends

The president who had championed engagement with China for decades signed up to replace it.

  • Anchorage, March 2021. On camera, China's top diplomat Yang Jiechi lectured Blinken and Sullivan about American democracy.
  • The manifesto. Sullivan and Kurt Campbell had written in 2019 that engagement wrongly assumed it could change China; the aim was coexistence on favourable terms. Transition briefings shocked Biden, and Burns created a China Mission Center, the CIA's only single-country one.
  • The squeeze. Ambassador Qin Gang's reply: if America wanted a cold war, China would not be the loser.
Campbell's five schools on China
  1. Engagement (Kissinger, Scowcroft, Biden for most of his career)
  2. Allies first (Campbell)
  3. China as the enemy, war likely
  4. One issue at a time
  5. Global problems first: climate, pandemics
Biden's three-part squeeze
  • Rebuild alliances: listening tour, Quad, AUKUS
  • Invest at home: chips, minerals, "derisking"
  • Export controls on the technologies that feed China's military
07Russia

Ransomware first, Ukraine second

Biden's only meeting with Putin before the war was about hackers. Nobody in his team imagined a full invasion yet.

  • Colonial Pipeline. In May 2021 the Russian-based gang DarkSide locked Colonial's business systems. The company shut all 5,500 miles of a pipeline carrying 45% of the East Coast's fuel and paid $4.4 million. Biden held Moscow responsible for crimes from its soil.
  • A first buildup. By April 2021 about 100,000 Russian troops sat near Ukraine. Biden's sanctions were deliberately "proportionate". Sanger argues the "stable and predictable" ties Biden offered were what Putin, whose leverage is unpredictability, wanted to avoid.
  • Geneva, June 2021. Biden handed Putin a list of 16 critical-infrastructure sectors that were off-limits. After a follow-up call in July, the REvil gang went dark. Haines admits the worst case they imagined was an incursion into eastern Ukraine.
08Afghanistan

The meltdown in Kabul

Leaving was defensible, Sanger argues. The way the US left told rivals it had little stomach for long fights.

  • The inheritance. Trump's February 2020 Doha deal promised US withdrawal by May 2021 and excluded the Afghan government. Biden overruled his generals and set September 11. Of an Afghan army of 300,000 on paper, only about 22,000 commandos were effective.
  • Collapse. US forces left Bagram overnight on July 2, abandoning 3.5 million items. Ghani promised to stay and fight on August 14 and fled the next day.
  • The cost. A bomber at Abbey Gate killed 13 American troops and more than 150 civilians. A later drone strike killed an aid worker and seven children. Chris Donahue was the last soldier out, on August 30.
09Weapons

A "Sputnik" moment

China jumped ahead in a technology the US invented and then neglected, and began building toward nuclear parity.

  • The missile that went backwards. On July 27, 2021, a Chinese rocket flew west over the United States and released a hypersonic glide vehicle that the Pentagon lost track of. General Hyten counted "hundreds" of Chinese hypersonic tests from 2016 to 2021 against nine American ones.
  • AUKUS. Australia dropped a $60 billion French submarine deal for US and British nuclear propulsion. Pivoting to Asia, Richard Fontaine notes, means doing less elsewhere.
  • Silos. About 230 silos appeared near Yumen and Hami. Mao's few-hundred-warhead "minimum deterrent" was over.

10Intelligence

Publishing the enemy's plan

When Putin couldn't be talked out of the war, Washington tried to take away its surprise.

  • The signals. Putin's July 2021 essay of nearly 7,000 words cast Ukrainians and Russians as one people. By late October the mother lode described 100 to 150 battalions, a puppet government and a 100,000-strong occupation force.
  • Nobody believed it. Apart from the British, allies thought Putin was bluffing, and some recalled Iraq in 2003.
  • Authorised leaks. Sullivan pictured Washington frozen before a slow steamroller. In December the Washington Post showed a 175,000-troop buildup, and false-flag plots were exposed before they ran. Germany blocked Nord Stream 2 two days before the invasion.

How a secret became a headline

Agencies built to hoard secrets were ordered to publish some. Sullivan likened the dilemma to Heisenberg's uncertainty principle: describing the plan changes it.

11Axis

A partnership "without limits"?

The old Cold War's nightmare, Moscow and Beijing together, returned with the roles reversed.

  • February 4, 2022. Xi and Putin issued a 5,000-word statement declaring a friendship with no limits. US intelligence concluded Putin described only a quick, limited operation, and Xi asked him to wait out the Olympics.
  • A long, uneasy history. Mao and Khrushchev despised each other, and by 1969 their armies were firing across the Ussuri River, which opened the door to Nixon. After Senator Biden's 1979 visit, China even hosted US posts listening to Soviet nuclear tests.
  • Intelligence gaps. Chinese officials dismissed the US briefings and passed them to Moscow. Burns predicted Russia would chafe as the junior partner.
Part III · Chapters 12–17

Wars, hot and cold

Ukraine survived. Then came harder questions: how much to give, how fast, how near to nuclear risk, and how to fight a chip war with China at the same time.

12Invasion

Why Kyiv didn't fall

Russia planned an occupation rather than a war, and nobody's war games anticipated how much America would help.

  • On paper. Ukraine spent just over a tenth of Russia's defence budget, with a fifth of its troops. Washington expected Kyiv to fall within days.
  • In practice. Warned by Burns, Ukrainians downed wave after wave of helicopters at Hostomel airport. The 40-mile convoy stalled, conscripts phoning home were located, and Russia failed at the "combined arms" warfare it had rehearsed. Donahue said any war college would have failed the plan.
  • The cyber war that wasn't. About four dozen US "hunt forward" operators had spent two months hardening Ukrainian networks. Russia's best strike wiped Viasat satellite modems an hour before the invasion; two days later Elon Musk switched on Starlink.
  • Zelensky's moment. He refused to leave Kyiv, and also imposed martial law and consolidated control of the media.
13Escalation

Boiling the frog

Turn up the heat on Putin so gradually that he never decides to jump at NATO.

  • Two goals in tension. Help Ukraine win, but avoid war with Russia. Aid in 2021 was under $1 billion; by mid-May 2022 it was nearly $4 billion.
  • The line. Lawyers held that sharing intelligence was legal as long as Ukrainians made the final decision to fire. Biden was furious when the press linked US data to the sinking of the Moskva.
  • The pattern. Each big request ran the same course: no, then "studying", then yes with limits. Zelensky's representatives argued Washington could save time by starting with yes.

14Taiwan

Pelosi lands, the status quo moves

China used a congressional visit as cover to redraw the rules around Taiwan.

  • Ambiguity, wearing thin. Washington had long left its response to an attack on Taiwan deliberately unclear. Between August 2021 and September 2022 Biden said four times it would defend the island, finally with a flat "Yes" to sending troops.
  • How Taiwan became indispensable. In 1976 RCA began teaching Taiwan to make semiconductors. In 1992 both sides agreed there was one China that each could define, which Su Chi called "creative ambiguity".
  • August 2022. Pelosi landed on August 2. After she left, China fired eleven ballistic missiles around Taiwan, the first since 1996, five of them into Japan's economic zone. More than 50 warships and 200 aircraft drilled, and China stopped respecting the Strait's median line.
15Nuclear

The nuclear paradox

The better Ukraine did, the closer Putin came to reaching for a nuclear weapon.

  • The "Armageddon speech". At a fundraiser on October 6, 2022, Biden said nuclear use was a real threat for the first time since the Cuban Missile Crisis. Intercepts showed a senior Russian commander discussing the logistics of a battlefield strike.
  • Hostage reactors. Russian troops occupied Zaporizhzhya, Europe's largest nuclear plant, source of about 20% of Ukraine's 2021 electricity. The IAEA's Rafael Grossi drove through artillery fire to reach it and left inspectors behind without asking permission.
  • The trigger. Ukraine retook much of Kharkiv province in September and Kherson on November 11. Putin mobilised 300,000 men and said his nuclear threats were no bluff.

16Chips

A high fence for a small yard

America let chip manufacturing drift to one vulnerable island. Now it is trying to rebuild at home and lock China out of the most advanced tools.

  • The single point of failure. Morris Chang's foundry model turned TSMC into everyone's factory. By an often-cited estimate Taiwan makes nearly 90% of the most advanced chips, while the US share of world manufacturing fell from 37% in 1990 to about 12% in 2020.
  • CHIPS. Intel stalled its Ohio groundbreaking to pressure Congress. Kathleen Hicks told senators 98% of the chips in Pentagon systems are finished in Asia, and the $52 billion act passed the Senate 64–33.
  • The fence. Rules issued on October 7, 2022, cut China off from top AI chips and chipmaking equipment. Nvidia promptly designed chips just under the limits, and the Dutch quietly kept ASML's machines out of China. Even before the rules, SMIC had made a 7-nanometer chip, up from 40 nanometers in 2020.

Where the chokepoints are

The book splits chipmaking into design (the West), manufacturing (TSMC) and packaging (China). Because chips are dual-use, Raimondo admits, fencing off military uses blocks some commercial ones too.

17Middle powers

"Don't make us choose"

The contest is fought less over loyalty than over hundreds of small technical choices, and smaller countries want both suitors.

  • Jeddah. China buys 18% of Saudi oil and had become the kingdom's largest trading partner. The Saudi ambassador likened US and Chinese 5G to having both a Starbucks and a Coffee Bean.
  • The Solomons. The US closed its embassy in the 1990s to save $387,000 a year. In 2019 the islands dropped Taiwan for about $730 million in Chinese aid, and in 2022 signed a security pact with Beijing.
  • The balloon. After China abruptly ended zero-COVID, its economy slumped. Then a surveillance balloon drifted over Montana; US intelligence concluded Xi learned of its course late. At Munich, Wang Yi demanded an apology.

Part IV · Chapters 18–20

Fighting for control

By 2023 the war had settled into trenches, Europe was arguing about how it ends, and all three powers were struggling with problems at home.

18Year two

Searching for endgames

Allies agreed Putin had to fail. They didn't agree what failure meant.

  • Tanks. Scholz wouldn't send Leopards until America sent Abrams; Biden's 31 unlocked them. At Grafenwoehr, 60–70% of Ukrainian recruits had never been near artillery.
  • Three camps. The frontline states (Poland and the Baltics) wanted no Russian gain at all. France and Germany expected a settlement. Washington talked like the first camp and privately planned like the second. Milley doubted either side could win outright. Year one cost Russia about 200,000 casualties, including up to 60,000 dead.
  • Symbols. Biden reached Kyiv by train in February 2023, having told Moscow in advance. Putin then suspended New START.
"Putin did that."
Finnish president Sauli Niinistö, on why Finland joined NATO, adding 800 miles of border
19Technology

The digital war and its limits

Ukraine became a laboratory for data and drones, but software can't clear seven lines of trenches.

  • The Pit. At a US base in Europe, a "single pane of glass" fused commercial satellite images, Telegram posts, soldiers' phone locations and classified intercepts. One official said the US handed Ukraine "a flashlight", not a target list.
  • Maven. The Pentagon's AI program survived a 2018 Google staff revolt and grew, with Palantir's software, into a live battlefield picture. Starlink carried Ukraine's communications, though Musk refused coverage for a strike near Crimea.
  • Cheap mass. A Kyiv factory turned out 20 kamikaze drones a day; a good pilot needed four to six per target. In 2023 the Pentagon launched Replicator for weapons that are "small, smart, cheap, and many".

202023

The downward spiral

None of the three powers controlled as much as it believed, at home or abroad.

  • Russia. Prigozhin's June 2023 mutiny stopped 125 miles from Moscow; two months later his jet blew up. Ukraine's counteroffensive gained about as much land as it lost. General Zaluzhny admitted: "There will most likely be no deep and beautiful breakthrough." US intelligence put Russian casualties above 300,000, including 120,000 dead.
  • Ukraine's bind. At Vilnius, NATO said only that Ukraine's future was in NATO. Haines said Putin believed time was on his side.
  • China. Youth unemployment hit 21% before Beijing stopped publishing it, and senior officials vanished. Chinese code turned up in infrastructure near Guam. In November, Xi and Biden restored military hotlines and agreed to talks on fentanyl and AI.
  • The Middle East returns. Days after Sullivan called the region the quietest in two decades, Hamas killed more than 1,200 people in Israel and seized about 240 hostages. Sanger is sharply critical that Biden set no conditions on American weapons as Gaza's death toll passed 20,000, by the Hamas-run health ministry's count.
Epilogue

Conflicts nested inside each other

Sanger expects this era to outlast the elderly leaders of all three powers. He warns against treating it as a replay of the twentieth century.

For roughly 6% of its defence budget, the US exposed the rot in Russia's military. But Putin learned to evade sanctions, and by late 2023 hinted at a ceasefire on current lines, keeping about a fifth of Ukraine. Sanger's model is Korea: an armistice, steady arms and eventual EU and NATO membership, at the price of rewarding aggression.

On China he credits Biden with a credible but poorly explained plan, and names three risks: abandoning the technology strategy, overreacting to every Chinese advance, and pushing Moscow and Beijing closer. Rather than match China warhead for warhead, he favours resilient satellites, cyber defence and AI.

America's advantages are allies and its pull on talent, while a "hedging middle" plays the powers off each other. The old Cold War's real achievement, Sanger concludes, was eight decades without direct great-power war, a streak that must not break.

Three arms races, one chip

Nuclear

Xi's personal project. China has more than doubled its arsenal to about 500 warheads and won't discuss limits until it reaches parity.

Space

Starlink's roughly 5,000 satellites rivalled costly military systems. China plans to blind US forces in a conflict.

AI

Most of the talent is in private companies. It will speed up everything from targeting to ransomware.

Takeaways

The toolkit

The book is reportage, not a manual, but its record of misjudgements suggests some habits.

Reading rivals

Believe stated intentPutin at Munich in 2007 and Xi in 2013 said where they were going. Both were treated as rhetoric.
Don't project your own logicEurope assumed gas revenue would restrain Putin. Wounded pride and history mattered more.
Watch logistics, not wordsBlood supplies and a reserve occupation force revealed the invasion, not diplomatic denials.
Mind the price of each moveCheap land grabs invite the next one. Count what the last act of aggression cost.

Competing

Keep the yard smallGuard a few chokepoint technologies tightly; not every advance is a Sputnik moment.
Fund it as securityFabs cost about as much as carriers. Treat CHIPS as a down payment, not a one-time fix.
Allies multiply powerChina has partners of convenience. America's network is its edge, if it keeps showing up.
Separate engagement from diplomacyTalk to manage conflict, not because talking will change the other system.

Managing escalation

Publish to disarmReleasing intelligence removes pretext and surprise, if you accept that plots get cancelled.
Test red lines step by stepTurning up the heat gradually kept NATO out of the war, but each delay had a cost on the battlefield.
Leave an exitNever force a choice between humiliation and the bomb. Warn through many voices, including the adversary's friends.
Match ends to meansTotal victory with limited support "never ends well", as a retired general put it.
