Into the quiet

Following Jesus in a world of noise

Comer's case that solitude, silence and stillness are as essential to following Jesus as community is, and that the noise of modern life is as dangerous to the soul as hurry.

7 movements3 encounters10 practices~56,000 words, condensed

The cover and the roundel here both crop Moretto da Brescia's Christ in the Wilderness, which Comer discusses near the end of the book.

Comer once shook a jar of muddy Oregon creek water at the start of a sermon and set it on the pulpit. Forty minutes later the silt lay on the bottom and the water above was clear. That, he says, is what happens in the quiet if you stay long enough.

The big idea

The greatest threat to faith may be distraction, not secularism

Comer wrote The Ruthless Elimination of Hurry about the speed of modern life. This book is its companion, about the noise. Cities are loud, culture is loud, churches are loud, and even our prayer is crowded with books, apps and earbuds. We are the first generation in history for whom it is normal to banish quiet completely.

His answer is an old one. Jesus "often withdrew to lonely places and prayed." The Greek word for those places is eremos: the wilderness, the deserted place, the quiet place. Jesus lived a rhythm of retreat and return, and Comer argues that solitude and community are the two legs every apprentice of Jesus walks on.

Going into the quiet isn't a spa day. Following Henri Nouwen, Comer calls it the place of "the great encounter", and he maps three of them: with ourselves, with our enemy, and finally with our God. The book ends with ten practical ways to build a quieter life.

Where it starts
Twenty-one days in Puget Sound

After stepping down from twenty years as a pastor, Comer spent three weeks alone in a cabin. No phone, no books, no alcohol, no exercise, one coffee a day. His only human contact was ninety minutes of depth therapy at 5:50 a.m. with a long-haired psychologist he nicknamed Gandalf.

An introvert who had taught on solitude for years, he expected it to be easy. By day three he was more depressed than he had been in years. He realised his "solitude" had always come with props: books, a laptop, projects, the occasional bottle of wine.

He asked why books were banned. The answer stayed with him: "The desert teaches by taking away."

It was "one of the most painful, harrowing, terrifying things I have ever done." It was also the turning point of his life, and the reason for the book.

Beginning & OneA world of noise · The eremos

Jesus often withdrew

Noise is doing measurable harm to our bodies and quiet harm to our souls. Jesus lived by a different rhythm, and Comer argues every follower needs it, introvert or not.

The problemNoise is stress

We live in a world of noise

Our bodies evolved for quiet soundscapes. Our brains still hear a freeway as a predator.

  • Mathias Basner, who has studied noise for twenty-five years, puts it plainly: "Noise is stress." Loud sound triggers the amygdala and cortisol before the mind can name it. A commute home can feel like fighting off sabre-toothed tigers the whole way.
  • Noise has been tied to hypertension, heart disease, stroke, anxiety, depression and dementia. Comer expects we'll one day look back on city noise the way we look at lead paint.
  • The deeper problem is that it's now possible to never be alone. The moment we are, we reach for the phone. In Korea, stressed workers pay to spend 24 hours in a mock prison cell just to be away from their devices. "The real prison is where we return to," said one.
  • Much of what we call "God's silence" or "the dark night of the soul" may simply be overloaded brains. Comer asks whether the wreckage of Western Christianity was caused in part by the sheer noise of modern life.

The traditionThe power of silence

The Desert Fathers and Mothers

Thousands fled the crumbling Roman world to pray in the deserts of Egypt, Syria and Judaea. Their sayings still map our inner lives.

  • In the third century, a young Egyptian aristocrat named Antony heard Matthew 19:21 read in church ("sell your possessions… then come, follow me"), gave everything away and went into the desert. Athanasius's Life of Antony swept the Mediterranean.
  • They came from everywhere: Arsenius had tutored an emperor's sons, Abba Moses had been a slave and a robber, and Syncletica and Theodora were women in a man's world. They lived in mud-brick "cells", wove baskets so they could pray while working, and mostly sat in silence.
  • Their conviction, in Mother Teresa's later words: "God is the friend of silence." Abba Ammonas wrote that "it is by silence that the saints grew."
The patternRetreat and return

The busier Jesus got, the more he slipped away

Most people pull back from quiet as demands rise. Jesus did the opposite.

Every time the Gospels show Jesus going to the eremos

After his baptism: forty days in the wildernessMatt 4
After his first day preaching: up before dawn to a solitary placeMark 1
Before choosing the twelve: all night on a mountainLuke 6
After feeding the five thousand: dismissed the crowds and prayed aloneMatt 14
After John the Baptist's death: by boat to a solitary placeMatt 14
When the crowds tried to make him king: withdrew to a mountainJohn 6
With disciples too busy to eat: "Come with me by yourselves to a quiet place"Mark 6

But he was no hermit. His longest retreat lasted forty days; most were hours. Each time he came back to his community "to love and serve the world."

Take away community
  • No church, no table, no friends or mentors
  • What's left: a lone, lonely Christian dying out "like a brand plucked from the fire"
Take away quiet
  • No silent prayer, no time in nature, no long listening
  • What's left: "a semi-normal Christian in the digital age. Hollow, distracted, and frayed."

Comer lists the objections he expects (I'm an extrovert, I'm not a monk, I have toddlers, isn't this privilege?) and answers with one line: Jesus often withdrew. And he was Jesus. People were tearing open roofs to reach him.

DefinitionsAlone with God

What solitude is, and what it isn't

LonelinessA felt absence. Richard Foster: "Loneliness is inner emptiness. Solitude is inner fulfillment."
IsolationA move away from people out of fear or shame. Solitude moves toward God and, in time, toward others.
AlonenessBeing by yourself while texting, streaming or reading other people. "Alone together," in Sherry Turkle's phrase.
Comer's definition

Solitude is intentional time in the quiet to be with ourselves and God.

Only two inputs: God, and your heart laid open before God. Abba Hor told a disciple, "Take care that you never bring into this cell the words of another." And when solitude is separated from aloneness, studies find introverts want it no more than extroverts do. It's hard for everyone.

SolitudeNo people

Withdrawing from every other input so the faculties that perceive God can sharpen. Saint Jerome: "never less alone than when alone."

SilenceNo noise

Exterior silence is the easy part: no TV, earbuds or traffic. Interior silence means calming the "psychic entropy" of your own thoughts. One study found two hours of silence a day grew new cells in the hippocampus.

StillnessNo striving

Kallistos Ware: "an attitude of listening to God and of an openness to God." A surrendered will, like the clear water above the silt. Monks on the Mediterranean said, "Dolphins only surface in calm waters."

Why it mattersIntimacy doesn't scale

Go into your room and close the door

Jesus's first advice on prayer wasn't what to pray. It was where.

The anniversary

For their 25th anniversary Comer took his wife to San Sebastián, alone. If a couple were never alone, or only ever alone in front of screens, they could be friends but never lovers. The more intimate the relationship, the more time alone it needs. God is no different.

When I go into the quiet…
  • My body unwinds; my jaw softens; I breathe slower
  • I'm forced to look honestly at my heart, under God's compassionate gaze
  • The sharp emotions ease and love, joy and peace come up
  • I hear the "still small voice" and remember who I am, and who I'm not
  • Approval and criticism lose their hook. If only for a moment, I'm free
When I don't…
  • I feel distant from God; "in Christ" becomes a doctrine, not an experience
  • I let whoever is noisiest set the agenda: a headline, a boss, my inbox
  • I get reactive, run on low-grade anxiety and anger, snap at my family
  • I get so tired that I drift from engagement to escapism: chips, phone, wine
  • Old temptations and lies return "with a suspicious power"
  • The eremos is a place, but also a practice and a posture. It can be a park at sunrise, a backyard fire, or the hour after the kids are in bed. Saint Jerome: "Find in the busy city the desert of the monks."
  • Dallas Willard called solitude and silence "the most radical of the disciplines." Comer asks whether that makes them the most neglected.
  • Nothing in the New Testament commands you to switch off your phone and wait on God. Jesus just does it and says, "Come, follow me." You're invited, not commanded.

  • Wellness culture sells us saunas, sleep rings and supplements to offset modern stress. Comer has a sauna and a cold plunge and loves them, but no subscription makes a finite, flawed person whole.
  • So we bring the wellness mindset to solitude and expect instant peace. Sometimes it comes. More often there's a U shape: you go down before you come back up.

The U shape of solitude

Thomas Keating called the low point "the unloading of the unconscious." Without outside stimulation the brain shifts into its default mode network and turns to the inner world, especially the painful parts.

What comes upFive feelings

The most common things we feel in the quiet

ExhaustionFearShameSadnessAnger
  • Exhaustion. Comer calls it The Crash. After his triumph on Mount Carmel, Elijah went into the wilderness, asked to die, and slept. Then he ate, and slept again. Rest matters because tired people are rarely loving people. "Napping is a spiritual discipline too."
  • Fear. Often it feels like "worrying in God's general direction." Nietzsche said we "drug ourselves with sociability" because we fear what will be whispered in our ear. Many people aren't afraid of solitude so much as of feeling their fears.
  • Shame. Before the Fall, Adam and Eve "were both naked, and they felt no shame." Shame is the fear that if people knew who we really are, they'd reject us. In the desert there are no filters and no fig leaves. That is also the only way shame can be healed: by love.
  • Sadness. On retreat in Big Sur, Comer found himself grieving a lost friendship he thought he'd finished grieving. "The grief was real, and it wasn't a distraction from my retreat; it was my retreat." Gabor Maté: "Grief is the antidote to trauma."
  • Anger. Nouwen described his mind in prayer as "monkeys in a banana tree", and the first monkey was anger: "I give long, hostile speeches to my enemies." Unlike sadness, rehearsed anger works itself further in, not out.

Big Sur

At the monastery's silent adoration, twenty minutes in, Comer heard tapping. The new monk leading the session, a former doctor, was playing on his phone. Whether it's cocaine or Candy Crush, it's a diversion from our pain and from God, not a path through it.

  • Brother Ignatius, prior of the Big Sur community, on what retreatants face: "You have to feel." Things you haven't resolved come up and have to be worked through. He called that process beautiful.
  • Keating called it "the divine therapy": the Spirit evacuating "the undigested psychological material of a lifetime." Like surgery, it gets worse before it gets better.
Two, continued · and the Intermission

The Gethsemane prayer

The one time the Gospels let us watch Jesus in the eremos, he "began to be sorrowful and troubled." He didn't deny, detach or drug. He went to the place of pain on purpose and prayed in three movements.

1 · Feelings Give God your feelings

"My soul is overwhelmed with sorrow to the point of death."

No filter, no polish. Pete Greig: "Pray what you've got." Two-thirds of the Psalms are lament, which isn't complaining but complaining to God. Even the violent imprecatory psalms are there because those feelings are in us. John Ortberg: "Prayer isn't a place to be good; it's a place to be honest."

2 · Desires Give God your desires

"My Father, if it is possible, may this cup be taken from me."

Desire drives every feeling. Augustine defined sin as disordered desire. Offer them all, even the ones you know are wrong: "God, I want to lust right now… Have mercy." Comer admits much of his prayer comes down to "Lord Jesus Christ, have mercy."

3 · Trust Give God your trust

"Yet not as I will, but as you will."

Surrender: we stop trying to engineer a pain-free outcome. Comer's go-to is the Welcoming Prayer: "I let go of my desire for security, affection, and control, and embrace this moment as it is."

While you pray
  • Let feelings come like a wave you don't fight. Rilke: "No feeling is final."
  • Name them, as specifically as you can. Therapists call it affect labelling. Naming shows you that you are not your feelings.
  • Martin Laird: our feelings are the weather over a mountain. "We are not the weather. We are the mountain."
  • Self-awareness alone can still leave a person narcissistic. The point is to name your feelings to God.
The Intermission, in full

Comer stops the book here. Put it down. Find a quiet place. Leave the phone behind and bring nothing but yourself. Come as you are, let whatever is down there come up, and pray through Gethsemane.

Go, now, into the quiet.

The way throughToward the pain

We heal by moving toward our pain

"Our brokenness is the geography of God's arrival." (Jay Stringer)

The campfire

At his son Jude's sixteenth birthday, around a fire at 8,000 feet, Comer asked each boy for his deepest fear. Jude said, "Being alone in the quiet. That's why I'm always listening to music." Comer admits he sometimes feels it too, even as the man writing the book on solitude.

  • In story after story, Jesus goes to people in their pain: Mary Magdalene, Bartimaeus, Martha and Mary, the woman bleeding for twelve years, Peter after his denial, even Judas.
  • A few weeks after Comer's grief in Big Sur, the friend he'd lost called. While praying early one morning, God had reframed what happened between them. The insight that began their reconciliation came in the quiet, not in therapy or a sermon.
  • Every hurt carries an invitation: to face reality, to stop distracting yourself, to open up to community, to surrender, to trust the Shepherd's path.
  • It takes time. Parker Palmer likened the soul to a wild animal: you won't find it by crashing through the brush. You sit on a rock and wait until it appears.
ThreeThe encounter with our enemy

Here be dragons

The desert monks didn't go into the wilderness for rest. They went to fight. Comer argues that's closer to how we actually experience solitude than any self-care model.

The paradigmNot to flee, but to fight

Jesus was led into the wilderness to be tempted

Facing the devil was the purpose of the trip, not a detour from it.

Skellig Michael

Comer and Jude visited the jagged island off south-west Ireland where sixth-century Celtic monks built beehive huts at the edge of the known world (later a Star Wars set). They named it after the archangel who fights Satan in Revelation. For seven centuries they prayed there "not to find inner peace… but to make war on hell."

  • When Constantine legalised Christianity, safety brought compromise. Church posts were bought and sold. The desert became the refuge of those who wanted to follow Jesus rather than be good Romans.
  • Their model for solitude wasn't Sabbath rest but spiritual war. Matthew 4: Jesus was led by the Spirit into the eremos "to be tempted by the devil." They followed him there "not to escape but to engage."
  • Antony moved into an abandoned Roman fort. Visitors heard him shouting, "Away from me, you demons!"

The three enemies of the soul

The worldA culture's lies

The values and norms of a society that defines good and evil for itself. In the quiet you notice how much you've accepted because "everybody's doing it."

The fleshWarped appetites

Primal drives for sex and survival, bent by sin. In solitude they don't vanish. They move to the foreground.

The devilHostile voices

Real spiritual beings, Comer argues, not a metaphor. Voices that say You are worthless. It's hopeless. God isn't real. If anyone finds out, it's over.

The battle320 speeches a day

Chatter, and the voices behind it

"It is the most freeing thing in the world when you discover that not every thought that goes through your head is yours." (John Eldredge)

  • Psychologist Ethan Kross calls it chatter: looping negative thought. He estimates we listen to the equivalent of 320 State of the Union addresses of self-talk every day.
  • Phil Stutz, the therapist from the documentary Stutz, calls the voice that fights your growth "Part X" and describes it as an "inner demon." His two tasks: name it, then defeat it.
  • The desert tradition adds that there isn't one voice in your head but several, and they aren't all friendly. Comer asks: what if secularism is the superstition of our day?

Watchfulness and "talking back"

The hard part is telling them apart. Evagrius told young monks to question every thought at the door: "Are you on our side or the side of our foes?" His test: "If it is one of ours, it will fill you with tranquility."

The methodChange the channel

How Jesus fought: he didn't debate

The devil lied; Jesus quoted Scripture. The devil lied again; Jesus quoted Scripture again. Eventually the devil left.

  • The desert monks called mindfulness watchfulness. Saint Romuald: "Watch your thoughts like a good fisherman watching for fish."
  • Evagrius wrote Talking Back: A Monastic Handbook for Combating Demons ("the best subtitle of all time"). His insight: Jesus redirected his attention rather than arguing.
  • A 2021 Harvard study by Kathryn Devaney found long-term meditators gain "suppressive control" over the ruminative default mode network. The chatter can be quieted. Historian Jamie Kreiner notes that meditation has deep Christian monastic roots that the popular origin story tends to leave out.

Four responses when your mind wanders in prayer

Ordinary wandering
Gently come back

Shopping lists and jacket choices are just a brain being a brain. Don't scold yourself. Keating: if your mind wanders a thousand times, that's a thousand chances to return to God.

Chatter · 1
Pray the psalms

Pray them, don't just read them. Better still, memorise a few and follow the words back to God. Romuald: "The path you must follow is in the Psalms. Never leave it."

Chatter · 2
Fight lies with truth

Evagrius's "talking back." When a lie keeps returning, ask for a verse that answers it, write it down, and quote it as often as needed.

Chatter · 3
Turn thoughts into prayers

A judo move: use the thought's own force. Worried about your kids? Pray for your kids. The enemy tends to attack where you're vulnerable or where you're called, the same places God speaks into.

The secretStay

"Stay in your cell and it will teach you everything"

Abba Moses the Black, a former criminal who became one of the gentlest leaders of the fourth-century church.

  • Pascal, centuries before the iPhone: "All the unhappiness of men arises from one single fact, that they cannot stay quietly in their own chamber." Diversion works like a credit card. The bill comes later, with interest.
  • Try to go on retreat and things go wrong: a leaking dishwasher, a flat tyre, an angry client. Coincidence, demons or self-sabotage, Comer isn't sure. The resistance is real either way. Silicon Valley and hell both want you in the noise.
  • Most people abandon the cell before it does its work. Nouwen: stay "until all my seductive visitors get tired of pounding on my door."
  • Comer asked Gandalf why 21 days. Because Jesus, Moses and Elijah had forty, and "forty days" is Hebrew for "an awfully long time." Solitude can't be rushed.
  • Irish monks called it green martyrdom: dying to ordinary life for the sake of prayer, with a resurrection on the other side.

FourThe encounter with our God

Silence is God's first language

We meet ourselves and our enemy in the quiet whether we want to or not. But what we came for is God: to hear his voice, and to rest in his love.

The aimWasting time on God

To be with him, before anything else

Not the calm nervous system or the clear vision. Those come second. The point is love.

St. Michael's Abbey

A day alone in a Norbertine chapel so quiet that every click of his pen echoed. The first hours were all "monkeys in a banana tree": deadlines, a house move. Then the waters calmed. No fireworks, no visions. He drove home at peace, clearer about who he is and what's next, and aware of how loved he is.

  • He tries to do this monthly, though, borrowing Tish Harrison Warren's joke about praying daily, "which means about four days a week."
  • Nouwen defined solitude as "wasting time on God." Lettie Cowman: "no large growth in holiness was ever gained by one who did not take time to be often, and long, alone with God."
  • Hosea 2:14, God speaking of Israel: "I will lead her into the wilderness and speak tenderly to her."

What a "little pull on the line" can sound like

Slow down…Be gentle with yourself…Forgive her…What if you…Say no to that, it's not from me…Remember…It's time to…Do you trust me?

How Comer recognises it: from outside his own brain yet deep within. Warm, almost never angry or afraid, profoundly gentle. It usually nudges him toward a small act of love and lifts his heart "like a buoy, pulling me up for air." Elijah heard it not in the wind, earthquake or fire, but in "the sound of gentle silence."

Beyond wordsUnion

Don't try to make something happen

"It's really important to not try to make something happen in solitude but just to offer it to God." (John Ortberg)

  • Sometimes not much happens. That's fine. What comes out of solitude matters less than who comes out. Hold off on judging a retreat as good or bad.
  • If you hear nothing, something may be blocking your ears: sin, shame, exhaustion, a fried attention span, a wound. Or God may truly be silent for now.
  • Underneath the fear of pain and of demons is a fear of God himself. Bonhoeffer: we are afraid "that he may disturb us and discover who we really are." Jesus's answer, again and again: "Do not be afraid."

Beneath listening is beholding: looking at God looking at you in love. When his wife stops talking mid-sentence while they cook, Comer looks up into her face. When you can't hear a word from God, look up.

  • Christians have many names for this: union with God, Julian of Norwich's "oneing", the Orthodox theosis, the Reformed "union with Christ", the evangelical "communion with God."
  • Contemplation, in Gregory the Great's words, is "the knowledge of God that is impregnated with love." Theophan the Recluse: "To pray is to descend with the mind into the heart, and there to stand before the face of the Lord."
  • Behind the door to what we most fear is what we most desire: God, saying over and over, "I love you."
The turnFor the world

We retreat from the world, for the world

The same Spirit who calls you away will send you back.

The Jesus Abbey, South Korea

Comer took his oldest son to Yesu-won, a Protestant monastery in the Taebaek mountains with celibates and families side by side. They sat on the floor for three hours of prayer a day, all in Korean, for a week. Liz Torrey, who leads it, explained their noon hour: "We never pray for ourselves; we always pray for the world." Comer realised how often his own retreats had been only for himself.

  • The opposite of contemplation isn't action. It's reaction: a ping-pong life run by whoever is noisiest.
  • The desert's gift is perspective. On yourself: "Solomon's paradox" is that we see others more clearly than ourselves. On others: Merton, "We do not go into the desert to escape people but to learn how to find them." On the world: Pico Iyer, distance lets you "see it whole."
  • Richard Foster: solitude gives us "power not to win the rat race but to ignore the rat race altogether."
  • The warning is quietism, the seventeenth-century movement the Catholic church judged a heresy: prayer cut off from love and service. Retreat without return. The cure: we come away, and then we come back.

Wrong question. Ask how.

  • Asking whether you need quiet to be a Christian is like asking whether you need community. To get into heaven, perhaps not. To be an apprentice of Jesus, yes. "Following Jesus without going into the eremos would be like apprenticing under a master painter but refusing to get paint on your hands."
  • There's no quota. Susanna Wesley had nineteen children in a small house. When she pulled her apron over her head, the children knew not to interrupt. They called it "the tent of meeting."
  • Dallas Willard prayed for hours each morning in a converted garage. His old couch still holds the impression.
  • People who spend long years in quiet often radiate light. In old icons, saints aren't lit from an angle but from within. Comer notices how many of the oldest, godliest people he knows have bright, piercing eyes.
EndA quiet life

Make it your ambition

"Make it your ambition to lead a quiet life" (1 Thessalonians 4:11). In a world this loud, quiet won't just happen. It takes planning, practices, sacrifice and other people.

Comer wrote the last chapter at the New Camaldoli Hermitage in Big Sur, where he'd also started the book. Even on the drive up, his mind kept inventing reasons to turn back. He arrived, fell asleep by 8:30, and woke to a sunrise "and to joy."

He names two risks at the end of a book like this. One is turning a simple practice into a technique or another religious chore. The more dangerous one is closing the book inspired and doing nothing. Willpower and good intentions rarely change much. Practices do: "The practices are how we do what we can do so the Spirit of Jesus can do in us, by grace, what we currently cannot do."

The toolkit

Ten ways to de-noise your life

His advice: pick one, or two at most. Aim for a life 10 percent quieter, then build. Political scientist Aaron Wildavsky called this radical incrementalism.

1
Begin your day with a quiet time

Bonhoeffer: "God should have the first word." The Comers lock their phones in a safe at night and keep the early house hushed. His wife prefers late nights by the firepit instead. Any time works if it's protected.

Phone out of the bedroom; ten minutes before you check it

2
Turn the volume down, literally

John Cassian: how you are outside prayer shapes how you are inside it. Merton: "Christians should have quiet homes." Comer rarely listens to podcasts or music now and drives in silence.

One commute a week with nothing playing

3
Keep the Sabbath

A whole day of unhurried, undisturbed time. The Comers start with a feast with friends, then spend long hours each alone in a corner of the house.

One phone-free afternoon this weekend

4
Go on retreat

Thirty minutes a day flies by; it can take that long just to quiet the chatter. The deeper work takes longer. Lately he solo-camps on public land outside the city: 24 hours, free.

Put one day alone with God on the calendar

5
Build a poustinia

Russian for "desert": a small, bare prayer hut that a church opens to anyone who needs quiet. Silence is becoming a luxury good, so share it. The Comers bought an 11 × 13 ft micro cabin for their church.

Claim one corner of your home for prayer only

6
Get out in nature

Jesus prayed in deserts, gardens and on mountains. A Stanford study found a 90-minute walk in a park reduced rumination where a walk along a highway didn't. In 2018 Scotland began prescribing nature for anxiety and depression.

Tea outside tomorrow morning, no phone

7
Walk with God

Gen Z has rediscovered it as "silent walking." Comer suspects walking helps us process emotional pain. He ends workdays walking the dog for thirty minutes, praying the Examen.

A short daily walk without earbuds

8
Embrace digital asceticism

Francis and Dominic answered a wealthy church with the vow of poverty. Our version: no alerts, a digital Sabbath, a dumbphone, no TV. "No version of robust discipleship in the digital age" goes without it.

Turn off every non-human notification

9
Learn to meditate

The mind can't switch off, so give it something to think on: Scripture, creation, the gifts of ordinary life. Our brains lean negative by roughly 4:1. "Where attention goes, neural firing flows" (Daniel Siegel).

Sit with one psalm for ten minutes

10
Talk less

James: "quick to listen, slow to speak." The Quakers practise simplicity of speech. Salvator Rosa painted himself holding a sign: "Be silent unless what you have to say is better than silence."

In your next conversation, let a pause sit

The last wordTogether

A book on solitude that ends with community

The noise is too loud to fight alone.

“Let him who cannot be alone beware of community. Let him who is not in community beware of being alone.”
Dietrich Bonhoeffer, to his seminary community at Finkenwalde
  • On Nyepi, Bali's new year, the whole island goes silent for 24 hours: shops shut, the airport closes, the power goes off. Comer can't imagine that in the West.
  • Cardinal Robert Sarah calls Western culture "a dictatorship of noise" and warns that "a noisy Church would become vain, unfaithful, and dangerous."
  • In 1988 and 1989, quiet Christians with candles and prayer helped bring down communist regimes: the Candle Demonstration in Bratislava, the peace prayers at Leipzig's St. Nicholas Church. "The only thing [the government wasn't] prepared for was candles and prayers," said Pastor Christian Führer.
  • Comer's hope is churches for "noise refugees": communities that keep the Sabbath, gather to quiet the mind before God, and send people back into the city as desert mothers and fathers.
Driving home

He knows he'll fail. An hour south of Big Sur his phone will reconnect and the anxious messages will flood in. He'll overwork, get grumpy, scroll too long. But he will carry the eremos with him as a memory in his body, and come back. "And I will awake, again, to the mercy and the great quiet of God."

Jesus's own words, used as a template. Find a quiet place, leave the phone, let whatever is in you come up, then pray it in this order in your own words: tell God how you feel, tell him what you want, and hand him the outcome.

Name the desire honestly in the moment, then pray the old refrain. Comer says "you would not believe how much of my prayer life" comes down to that last line.

For afflictive emotionsMary Mrozowski, after Thomas Keating · ch. 2

The Welcoming Prayer

First, pray slowly through your body from top to bottom. Notice wherever you feel pain or tension, and breathe it out to God.

I let go of my desire for security, affection, and control, and embrace this moment as it is.

Comer's go-to prayer. In the words of Jesus, it's simply "Your will be done."

Turn the thought into a prayer

God, I set before you …

Then lean on 1 Peter 5:7: "Cast all your cares on God, for he cares for you."

The judo move: use the worry's own momentum. Anxious about your kids? Stop and pray for your kids, and invite God into that very place.

It is written…

Before you need it, ask the Spirit for a verse that answers your recurring lie ("God isn't real," "It's all going to fall apart"). Write it down, memorise it if you can, and quote it as often as the thought comes back.

Jesus's method in the wilderness: no debate, just Scripture.

Romuald's Brief Rule · A.D. 1006 · printed at the close of the book
  1. Sit in your cell as in paradise.
  2. Put the whole world behind you and forget it.
  3. Watch your thoughts like a good fisherman watching for fish.
  4. The path you must follow is in the Psalms. Never leave it.
  5. And if your mind wanders as you read, do not give up; hurry back and apply your mind to the words once more.
  6. Realize above all that you are in God's presence.
  7. Empty yourself completely and sit waiting, content with the grace of God, like the chick who tastes nothing and eats nothing but what his mother brings him.
