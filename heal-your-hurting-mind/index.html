If you are struggling, talk to a doctor, a licensed counselor or someone you trust. The big idea

You are only as strong as you are honest

Nearly thirty years into a steady ministry, Craig Groeschel, pastor of one of America's largest churches, quietly fell apart. Nobody noticed. He kept preaching every weekend while panic and dread built up inside him, until he became convinced it might kill him. After a run of sleepless nights he finally called a counselor. Over the next eighteen months, his life changed.

That counselor was Dr. Wayne Chappelle ("Dr. C."), a clinical psychologist who spent two decades preparing military special-operations crews for high-risk missions before turning to elite athletes and then to pastors. Groeschel was his first pastor client. For this book he waived confidentiality, so Dr. C. can describe what he saw in their sessions. Dr. C. also checked every chapter and closes each one with a clinical reflection.

The result is a book in two voices. Groeschel writes about the spiritual side, usually through one Bible story per struggle. Dr. C. adds the psychology: what happens in the body, which thinking patterns make things worse, and which habits help. It starts with radical honesty, with God and with yourself. In John 5, before healing a man who had been ill for thirty-eight years, Jesus asks him: "Would you like to get well?"

Why it matters
1 in 5

~½

18 mo

How long Groeschel worked with Dr. C. before he could say out loud that he was getting better.

Three voices, one book

The introduction has three parts: Me (when Craig crashed), Dr. C. (whom he called), and You (help for when you crash). Each part ends with questions for reflection and space to journal, and the authors suggest reading it with a friend or a small group.

What shapes your mental health

Dr. C. lists five interacting causes. None of them is a lack of faith. Groeschel's point from Mark 12 is that Jesus addressed the whole person: heart, soul, mind and strength, in relationship with others. Mental health is dynamic. It moves up and down over a lifetime, and nobody is immune.

Outside vs. inside

Groeschel thought breakdowns happened to other people, because he was so consistent. His point is that a steady outside says little about the inside, and that is why struggling people need to be asked, not assessed.

Groundwork · Introduction & Part 1

Busting the myths

Groeschel thinks many Christians don't get the healing God offers because of a few toxic misunderstandings about mental health. They have to be cleared away before any of the six struggles can be tackled.

00Introduction

Help for when you crash

"Asking for help isn't a sign of weakness but a sign of wisdom."

  • Groeschel's first coping strategy was denial: it's just a hard season, I can handle this. The dread kept growing anyway. He is upfront that he is a pastor, not a clinician, which is why a psychologist checked the whole book.
  • The book covers six struggles: anxiety, depression, negativity, anger, trauma and burnout.
  • What it asks of you is radical honesty with God and with yourself. He quotes psychiatrist M. Scott Peck: mental health is a commitment to reality "at all costs."
  • His message to anyone who feels helpless is short: there is hope. He knows it can sound too good to be true from where you are.
Dr. C.'s reflection

Change takes real work, raw honesty and fierce courage. He presents himself as someone who has seen many people grow through hard seasons, not as a miracle worker.

01Myths About Mental Health

Struggling doesn't mean your faith is broken

It means you're human. "We have this treasure in jars of clay."

  • Mental health covers emotional, psychological and social well-being. It affects how you think, feel, act, handle stress, relate to others, make choices and bounce back.
  • Myth 1: Christians shouldn't struggle. Elijah asked to die, David wondered whether God had abandoned him, and Jeremiah cursed the day he was born. Luther, Spurgeon, Martin Luther King Jr. and Mother Teresa all battled anxiety or depression. The fix: stop assuming mental health and start pursuing it.
  • Myth 2: Faith alone should fix it. Nobody expects coming to Jesus to fix their cholesterol or credit score. Faith deals with sin, but you may also need better sleep, a better diet, a doctor, better friends, or therapy with a trained professional. If you break a bone, you need a surgeon, not a verse thrown at you.
  • Myth 3: God doesn't care. The Psalms, the Bible's biggest book, contain more laments than coffee-mug verses. Psalm 88 by Heman, a respected worship leader, ends in darkness with no hint of hope, and Israel sang it together in worship. God isn't afraid of our honesty.
  • Jesus understands from the inside. Some scholars count around thirty-nine different emotions in the Gospels, anxiety and anger among them. Hebrews 4 says we have a high priest who can empathise with our weaknesses.

Worry & gloom · Parts 2–4

The inner weather

Anxiety, depression and negativity all start in what the mind keeps returning to. In each one, the authors pair a spiritual practice with a way of retraining your thinking.

02Anxiety

Anxiety is not a sin, it's a signal

Like a warning light on a dashboard, it tells you something needs attention. Here, it's time to pray, pause and praise.

  • At 23, ordered to preach five times in one week, Groeschel vomited before every sermon. When he confided in an older church leader, the reply was that Christians shouldn't have anxiety, "especially pastors," followed by Philippians 4:6 quoted at him rather than to him.
  • He coins the word andxiety: we could handle one enemy, but it's this and that and the other. King Jehoshaphat faced three armies at once (2 Chronicles 20).
  • Jesus never sinned, yet in Gethsemane he was in such anguish that his sweat fell like drops of blood. So anxiety can't be a sin.
  • The biology: the amygdala sounds the alarm and floods you with adrenaline and cortisol to fight or flee. Today's stresses rarely allow either, so the chemicals linger. The New Testament word usually translated "anxiety," merimnao, means dwelling on fearful thoughts. Dwelling is the crux of the problem.
  • Pray. Anxiety is natural, prayer is supernatural. Pray as honestly as Jehoshaphat did: "We do not know what to do, but our eyes are on you." To "cast" your anxiety (1 Peter 5:7) is to throw it onto God, like the cloaks thrown onto the colt. If it's big enough to worry about, it's big enough to pray about.
  • Pause. After praying, the people "stood there before the LORD." Instead of don't just stand there, do something, try don't just do something, stand there. God may nudge you toward a verse, a diet change, a doctor or a therapist.
  • Praise. Jehoshaphat put singers at the front of the army, and the three armies turned on each other. They praised before the provision, and the battlefield became the Valley of Berakah, "blessing."

Why dwelling keeps anxiety going, and where prayer breaks the loop

The more often you think a thought, the easier it is to think it again (neuroplasticity). Prayer interrupts the loop by turning your attention elsewhere (Phil. 4:8). Groeschel cites Dr. Caroline Leaf's claim that twelve minutes of focused daily prayer for eight weeks produces brain changes visible on a scan. Dr. C. adds slow, deliberate breathing to calm the body's response.

Dr. C.'s point is that events don't cause anxiety on their own. The way your mind interprets them sets how intense it gets. Medication can calm symptoms quickly, but it doesn't cure, and some anti-anxiety drugs can become addictive. Long term, the work is on how you think about and respond to problems.

Where Craig is now

It took about six months before Groeschel felt better, and longer before he dared to say so out loud. He says he still isn't completely free of anxiety: when his schedule tightens, his neck tenses and his breathing gets shallow. Now he treats those signs as his cue to pray, pause and praise.

03Depression

Your emotions are real, but not complete and not permanent

Acknowledge your emotions, acknowledge there is hope, acknowledge you need help.

  • Groeschel's high-school friend "Tim" is the most encouraging person you could meet, and for decades he fought severe depression behind that smile. Depression is often invisible from the outside.
  • Depression isn't an ordinary bad day. It is sadness you can't bounce back from, a hopelessness that colours everything, joy that won't come, and sleep and daily life that stop working.
  • Jeremiah, the "Weeping Prophet," saw Jerusalem and the temple destroyed and preached for years without one listener repenting. In Lamentations 3 he writes that he has been walled in and weighed down with chains. Groeschel connects it to what he saw as a young pastor after the 1995 Oklahoma City bombing, when his church lobby became a temporary morgue.
  • Acknowledge your emotions. Dismissing feelings ("good vibes only") is emotional invalidation. Name them precisely: hopeless, guilty, numb, ashamed. In a study of people afraid of spiders, those who said their feelings out loud reacted far less a week later, and many touched the tarantula. Naming an emotion is the first step to changing it.
  • Because feelings are temporary, don't make a permanent decision based on a temporary emotion. The thought that everyone would be better off without you can feel true, the book says, but it never is.
  • Acknowledge there is hope. "Yet this I call to mind and therefore I have hope." Jeremiah chose this deliberately; he didn't feel it first. He remembered God's hesed (unbreakable, devoted love) and rahamaw (compassion, from the word for womb), which are "new every morning."
  • Acknowledge you need help. We don't heal in isolation, and loneliness predicts depression more strongly than marital status or stress. See a counselor or doctor, join a small group, and ask who is holding your rope. Luther credited friends' prayers with pulling him out, then wrote "A Mighty Fortress Is Our God."

Seven possible causes, so there is no one-size cure

Some Christians say spiritual warfare is the only cause, and others say depression isn't real. The book rejects both views. Because there are so many possible causes, Dr. C. recommends combining several approaches: people who rely on medication alone struggle more than those who also change their habits and their thinking.

The story

Tim never gave up, even when everything in him wanted to. Counseling helped a little. After trying several medicines he found one that helped a little. Changing his diet and starting to exercise helped a little. Prayer helped a lot, and so did joining (and later leading) a small group and filling his mind with new thoughts. After decades, he says the depression is gone. His message for readers: your emotions are valid but not permanent, and with God there is always hope.

04Negativity

Your thoughts have power, and you have power over your thoughts

Nobody talks to you more than you do. So the question is how well you are counseling yourself.

  • Each morning during his crash, Groeschel woke up to the same script: too much to do, I'll fall behind, I don't have what it takes. Every evening it predicted that tomorrow would be worse. This kind of loop trains the mind to be miserable.
  • The law of cognition: what you think shapes what you believe, which shapes what you feel, which shapes what you do. In his paraphrase, your life moves in the direction of your strongest thoughts (Proverbs 4:23; Romans 8:6).
  • Negative things stick to the brain faster and stay longer. In one study, people who first heard a surgery had a "70% success" rate changed their minds once told about the "30% failure" rate. People told "30% failure" first didn't change their minds when told about the 70%. Smartphones and social media keep feeding negativity, and it spreads from person to person like the flu.
  • You can't defeat what you don't define. Name your gateway negativity (see the table below).
  • I command myself. At Ziklag, David's town was burned, the families were taken, and his own men talked about stoning him. "But David strengthened himself in the LORD his God" (1 Samuel 30:6).
  • I talk to myself. In Psalm 103 David speaks to his own soul ("Praise the LORD, my soul… forget not all his benefits") and tells himself who God is and what he has done. His recurring line, "compassionate and gracious, slow to anger, abounding in love," comes from Exodus 34:6. It was effectively his mantra, the way athletes use one.
  • I ruminate. To meditate is to ruminate, the way a cow chews its cud. Go over a verse again and again until it becomes the new rut your thoughts fall into (Romans 12:2).
  • Start a negativity fast: cut back on the news, feeds, YouTube rabbit holes and gossiping friends that keep feeding the old ruts.

Digging a new rut

Dr. C. says most negativity is a learned habit of thinking, not something fixed. It is reinforced by cortisol and by well-worn neural pathways, and the same neuroplasticity lets you rebuild them. He suggests holding a replacement thought for about five minutes, and expects a new habit to take three to six months of daily practice. Groeschel prayed Romans 15:13 hundreds of times until it became "the soundtrack" of his life.

Fire, wounds & fatigue · Parts 5–7

Under pressure

Anger flares up in a moment, trauma leaves lasting wounds, and burnout builds slowly. Each chapter is built on a Bible figure: Jesus at the temple, Paul in his sufferings, Elijah under a broom tree.

05Anger

Flip tables, not people

Anger isn't always sinful, but it is almost always unwise. If you're going to be angry, be angry the way Jesus was.

  • "In your anger do not sin" (Ephesians 4:26) is often quoted to excuse anger. A few verses later Paul says to get rid of all anger. Anger shouldn't stay overnight ("if it has its own toothbrush at your house…"), and it gives the devil a topos, a room in your house. James 1:20: anger "does not produce the righteousness that God desires."
  • Groeschel's confession: blocked at a neighbourhood gate for ten minutes, he pounded on a stranger's car window until the man threatened to call the police. Another time he argued with Amy until they were too late for church.
  • Angry like Jesus, from the temple clearing that all four Gospels record. (1) Jesus got angry on behalf of the mistreated, never over insults to himself. (2) He flipped tables (an unjust system), not people. (3) Afterwards "the blind and the lame came to him… and he healed them." Every time Jesus is righteously angry, someone is helped or healed.
  • Stop being shocked. Jesus was never shocked by sin, because "he knew what was in each person's heart." Quoting Brant Hansen's Unoffendable, Groeschel argues that you get angry less once you accept how people are. Cognitive biases (attribution bias, confirmation bias, overplacement, the bias blind spot) make us shocked by other people's sin and not by our own. The Pharisee and the tax collector show the same thing.
  • We aren't called to be right. We're called to be loving. And Jesus cancelled our sin, not us: Herod, Pilate, Judas and Peter all wronged him, and he cancelled none of them.

Four questions in the gap, with the iceberg underneath

Anger is often a secondary emotion (the Gottman Institute's "anger iceberg"). Underneath it is usually anxiety, rejection or hurt, and the feeling of control that anger gives is false. Stephen Covey's gap between stimulus and response is where you get to choose. In Psalm 109 David prays furious curses, yet as a king with an army he never carries them out, and by the end of the psalm he is giving thanks.

The story

In 2018 a Saturday Night Live comedian mocked the eye patch of Navy SEAL turned congressman Dan Crenshaw. When the comedian later posted that he was in a very dark place, Crenshaw contacted him, told him he had value and a purpose, and later went on the show with him. Crenshaw had been mistreated himself, but he chose not to retaliate and helped the man instead.

06Trauma

Process the pain, press into God, pursue purpose

You can't heal a wound you ignore. Your trauma may not have been your fault, but pursuing healing is your responsibility.

  • At sixteen, Groeschel was in the passenger seat when a friend shouted "Watch this!" and drove through a stop sign. An oncoming truck hit them less than a foot behind his door. Decades later, he is a pilot who has flown in high-risk conditions, yet he still grips the wheel from the passenger seat. Dr. C.'s advice: don't compare your trauma with anyone else's, and do seek healing.
  • Trauma is Greek for "wound." It includes both the event and how you experienced and responded to it, which is why two children from the same chaotic home can be affected differently. More than 80% of Americans have experienced at least one trauma-inducing event, and close to 30% have experienced four or more.
  • Paul could be called the apostle to the traumatized. He approved Stephen's stoning and persecuted Christians. Later he was blinded, stoned and left for dead, flogged, shipwrecked three times, and repeatedly hunted. He admitted he "despaired of life itself" (2 Corinthians 1:8).
  • Process the pain. Pushing it down doesn't ease the pain, it only rules out healing. Brené Brown: vulnerability is "the greatest casualty of trauma." Robert Stolorow: trauma lasts when pain "cannot find a relational home." Paul wrote openly about his hurts. Talk to trusted friends, a recovery group, a pastor, or a Christian therapist.
  • Prayerfully press into God. Paul pleaded three times (perhaps three long seasons of prayer) for his "thorn" to be removed. The answer was "My grace is sufficient for you." He didn't receive the healing he asked for, but the one God knew he needed.
  • Pursue purpose. God comforts us "so that we can comfort those in any trouble" (2 Corinthians 1:4). Groeschel's sister Lisa survived years of abuse by a teacher. With counselors, a lot of hard work and prayer, she forgave him and now helps other survivors.

Three shapes of trauma

Trauma-inducing events range from life-threatening ones (war, assault, serious accidents) to non-life-threatening ones (severe bullying, emotional neglect). Either kind can leave lasting symptoms. One study of Ukrainian families after the 1932–33 famine found the lesson "don't trust the world" being passed down through three generations.

The story

Groeschel lists his own wounds from ministry: a mentor's death, the hurt of leaving his first pastor, friends who left, death threats, threats against his children, and his own mistakes. His response is always the same: process the pain, press into God, pursue purpose. Not long ago a deer ran into the wing of a small plane he was in as it landed and pushed it off the runway. Two days later he was flying again.

07Burnout

You're not just tired. You're empty, and you need to be refilled

"Sometimes the most spiritual thing you can do is take a nap."

  • Over two decades at Life.Church, Groeschel took on a little more every year: more preaching, more campuses, more staff, more writing. It grew into a mountain that crushed him. After a couple of sessions Dr. C. named it in three words: severe occupational burnout.
  • Stress is short-lived and tied to a situation. Burnout is chronic and combines emotional exhaustion, cynicism and reduced efficacy. It comes from work and also from social, relational, financial and personal pressure. Dr. C. describes it as an inability to balance competing demands.
  • Elijah won on Mount Carmel, then fled after a single threat from Jezebel. He left his servant behind, sat under a broom bush and prayed to die: "I have had enough, LORD." The book's point is that Elijah "was as human as we are" (James 5:17).
  • Burnout shows up in the body (fatigue, poor sleep, aches, numbing habits), the mind (the amygdala takes over and the prefrontal cortex weakens) and the emotions (self-doubt, cynicism, blaming God). Groeschel lost two weeks he can't remember at all.
  • Check the diagnosis first. Groeschel tells of a "copperhead" on a walk that turned out to be a stick lying on an old sock. Ask an actual expert.
  • The angel didn't scold Elijah. It touched him, gave him bread and water, and let him sleep, twice. Rest is a commandment: the Sabbath. Don't let your mind bully your body.
  • God was not in the wind, the earthquake or the fire, but in "a gentle whisper." He whispers because he is close and wants to draw you closer, often in ordinary moments like the dishes, the bills or the school run.

Elijah's three steps to burnout, and the way back

Mileage as Groeschel calculates it from 1 Kings 18–19: up and down Mount Carmel twice, a sprint to Jezreel ahead of Ahab's chariot, then more than 100 miles to Beersheba. He compares it to almost six marathons, run while fleeing for his life. Notice that God doesn't correct Elijah's exaggerations. He meets his body's needs first, then speaks to him.

What do you need more and less of?Every yes costs something somewhere else.Less time thinking about work, more walks with Amy with the phone put away.
Where do you recover?Places and devices that trigger stress.Digital disconnection, no news, fewer comparison feeds. A staycation counts.
Scroll sideways on small screensBurnout = high exhaustion + high cynicism + low confidence
How it ended

Groeschel didn't recover instantly. It took about eighteen months of slow, deliberate changes, a lot of prayer and new challenges to occupy his mind. Then one day Amy said, "I think you're doing good," and he realised she was right: he had peace. Dr. C.'s summary is that Groeschel used to live in one gear, all-out. He learned to ease off, change pace and make regular stops to "rest, repair, and refuel," so he could keep leading without "leading while bleeding."

Conclusion

"Craig, what do you need to heal?"

While Groeschel was writing the book, his father died. They had been very close, talking almost every day, and Groeschel led the funeral himself. What upset him wasn't the death but the memories that came back. Before his father came to faith, he was an alcoholic who got drunk every night. As a small boy, Groeschel's job was to distract him while his mother watered down the Scotch.

Talking it through with Dr. C., he learned that this childhood gave him determination, resilience and drive, and also made it very hard for him to ask for help or accept it. Dr. C. asked him the same question as before: what do you need to heal?

The book ends by asking you the same question. You can't change what you don't define. So: where are you broken, and what do you need to move forward? Groeschel's answer comes in a list of names for Jesus: Good Shepherd, Light of the World, rock and fortress, and the High Priest who understands and is praying for you now.

The whole book in six sentences

  Anxiety is a signal: pray, pause, praise.

  Depression: your emotions are valid but not complete, and real but not permanent.

  Negativity: your thoughts have power, and you have power over your thoughts.

  Anger isn't always sin, but it often leads to it. Take it to God.

  Trauma: process the pain, press into God, and one day find purpose in it.

  Burnout: God is in the whisper. He whispers because he is close.

The book closes with Isaiah 41:10: "Don't be afraid, for I am with you."

Appendix: the thinking errors Dr. C. keeps referring to

"Pondering," "contemplating" and "evaluating" all start with spotting these. Here they are grouped by the chapter where they cause the most trouble.

Worry-makersFortune-telling (the future is certain), magnification (catastrophizing), control fallacy (believing you control what you can't), jumping to conclusions.
Mood-sinkersNegative filter, overgeneralizing (one event becomes a pattern), labeling ("I'm worthless"), emotional reasoning (I feel it, so it's true), should statements.
Fight-startersMind reading, polarized thinking, relational cynicism, confirmation bias, just-world thinking (good things happen only to good people).
Self-blindersBias blind spot (I can see bias in others but not in myself), third-person effect (the media sways others, not me), victim mentality.
Practices from the book

The toolkit

Each part ends with questions for reflection and space to journal. These are the practices the authors come back to most often, sorted by where they act.

Toward God

Pray, pause, praiseTake the worry to God honestly, wait in silence, and thank him before anything changes.
Pick a verse and ruminate on itChew on one verse until it becomes your mantra: Romans 15:13, Isaiah 26:3, Exodus 34:6. Pray it phrase by phrase.
Pray the honest psalmsPsalm 88, Psalm 109 and Lamentations 3 show that God can handle raw honesty, rage included.
Keep the Sabbath and listen for the whisperRest is commanded. Stillness is where God speaks.

Toward yourself

Name the emotion precisely"I feel betrayed," not just "I'm down." Write it down or say it out loud.
Kill the ANTs, grow GRITCatch automatic negative thoughts, check them against the thinking errors, and replace them with genuine, realistic, inspirational thoughts.
Box breathing and taking a kneeBreathe in for 4, hold for 4, out for 4, and repeat. Widen the gap before you react.
Look after your bodySleep 6–8 hours, eat balanced meals, drink water, exercise 3–4 times a week, cut back caffeine, nicotine and alcohol. Ask a physician.
Use small movesTen-minute bursts, a gratitude journal, making something, a negativity fast.

Toward others

Get professional helpA counselor, a doctor and, where needed, medication. The book sees all of these as compatible with faith. Medication can ease symptoms, but it works best alongside new habits and new ways of thinking.
Choose a wingmanSomeone who has your back and lovingly steers you back on course.
Join a small groupWe don't heal in isolation. Ask who is holding your rope, and whose rope you are holding.
Say no, and ask for helpBoundaries and delegating are wisdom, not weakness.
Turn your pain into help for othersOnce you are further along, comfort others with the comfort you received.
