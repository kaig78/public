BeingLogical

A guide to good thinking

A slim handbook in the spirit of Strunk and White. McInerny's claim is that logic is the habit of matching what you think and say to the way things actually are, more than a set of rules.

5 parts66 short sections28 fallacies~34,000 words, condensed
Loose thinkingHeld together
The big idea

Logic is about reality before it is about rules

McInerny teaches logic the way a coach teaches a sport. The voice is direct, the examples are homely (spilled milk, a leaky pipe, Louise out for a run), and he keeps returning to the obvious, because the obvious is what we most often skip.

His central thread is correspondence. Things exist first. Our ideas are faithful or unfaithful copies of them. Words carry those ideas to other people. A statement is true when it matches what is actually the case, and an argument is good when true statements are linked in a structure that carries their truth forward.

So "being logical" means more than reasoning consistently. A mind can be perfectly consistent from a false starting point. For McInerny logic is aimed at truth, and every chapter is about keeping thought tied to the world.

If you are unsure whether "the dog is in the garage" is true, thinking harder about dogs and garages won't settle it. Go and look in the garage. Truth is settled out in the world, not in your head.

Part 1 · Sections 1–10

Preparing the mind

Before any rule of inference, three habits: pay attention, respect facts, and keep your ideas and words honest. Logic has nothing to work on until they're in place.

01Be Attentive · Get the Facts Straight

Attention costs something

Most reasoning errors start earlier, with a situation we skimmed instead of read.

  • Familiarity is the trap. We assume today repeats yesterday and misread what's in front of us. Strictly speaking no situation repeats. "Pay attention" is apt wording: it takes effort, and you can't be attentive and passive at once. Don't just look, see. Don't just hear, listen.
  • A fact is something made or done, with a stubborn existence of its own. There are two kinds: things (the White House) and events (Lincoln's assassination). Things are more basic, because events are made of things and their actions.
  • The surest evidence of a thing is to go and look at it. For most events we have to rely on indirect evidence (documents, photographs, eyewitnesses), so everything depends on how authentic and reliable the sources are.
  • Facts can be objective (public, in principle checkable by anyone) or subjective (your headache). I can know about your headache only by trusting you, so subjective facts stand or fall with the person reporting them. You can even get your own subjective facts wrong through self-delusion or rationalization.
02Ideas · Origins · Matching

First the thing, then the idea, then the word

An idea is clear when it faithfully reflects its object. A word works when it faithfully carries the idea.

The chain of knowledge

"Cat" is a simple idea: one idea, one kind of object, easy to check. "Democracy" is a complex idea: it draws on many things at once (people, constitutions, institutions, history), so it stays clear only if you keep touching base with all of them.

  • We know the world through our ideas, but our ideas aren't what we know. They are the means, not the end. The quickest way to clarify an idea is to look through it at the thing it represents.
  • We favour our own ideas like our own children, but every idea owes its existence to something outside the mind. The longer we attend to ideas while ignoring where they came from, the more we see a projected world instead of the real one.
  • Bad ideas don't just happen. They come from carelessness, or worse, from deliberately ignoring facts. A bad idea can still be informative, but about the person who holds it, not about the world.
  • When you can't find the right word, the problem is usually the idea. Clarify the idea against its object and the word tends to arrive. You can describe an imaginary Lilliput in perfect words. All they reveal is the state of your mind.
03Communication · Vagueness · Evasion

Say it plainly

Logic begins with the statement: a sentence you can answer with "true" or "false". You can't judge a statement you can't understand.

  • Make background explicit. Don't assume the audience knows what you know. When in doubt, err on the side of saying too much.
  • Speak in complete sentences. "Dog turtle" links ideas without saying how. "The dog bit the turtle" is a statement.
  • Don't pass off evaluations as facts. "The Pearce Building is on Main Street" is true or false. "The Pearce Building is ugly" mixes subjective and objective elements and has to be argued for.
  • Drop double negatives. "It is not unlikely she'd be welcome" means "she'd be welcome", so say that.
  • Fit the language to the audience. Don't talk down to people and don't talk over their heads. You can only do that if you know who's listening.

Vague vs. ambiguous

Vague: blurred referent
  • "People don't like music like that." Which people, what music?
  • "They say he won't run again." Who are "they"?
  • The more general the word, the vaguer it gets. Rich words like love, fairness and democracy need to be defined before you use them.
Ambiguous: two meanings
  • A trail sign reads BEAR TO THE RIGHT. Is it a direction or a grizzly warning?
  • The fix is to spell it out: "Keep left. Grizzly bears on the right-hand trail."

"Vague" comes from Latin for wandering. Both faults let a word wander between ideas instead of settling on one.

Evasive language changes its user too

Euphemism has a place, but words like "final solution", "re-education" and "cultural revolution" were used to hide atrocities.

McInerny sees two harms. Evasive language deceives the audience, and over time it distorts the speaker's own sense of reality: we shape language, and language shapes us back. Shocking language is juvenile when it only aims to shock, but it beats evasive language if it gets people to the truth.

04Truth

Truth is correspondence

The whole purpose of reasoning is to arrive at the truth, and the truth is something we can actually reach.

  • Ontological truth is the truth of being: the lamp on the desk is really there. Logical truth is the truth of statements. The second rests on the first, because what makes a statement true is what exists.
  • "The boat is tied to the pier" is true if there is a boat, there is a pier, and the boat really is tied to it. A true statement declares a correspondence between ideas in the mind and states of affairs in the world.
  • The coherence theory (a statement is true if it fits an established system) is legitimate only when the system itself corresponds to reality. A claim that fits a dubious theory is just as dubious.
  • Lying is more a psychological failure than a logical one. The liar knows that A is B and deliberately says A is not B.
  • It would be absurd to treat truth as forever pursued and never reached. That would make the whole activity pointless.
Part 2 · Sections 1–8

The basic principles

The governing truths of logic, and the habits that follow from them: never stop short at an immediate cause, define your terms, and say exactly how much you mean.

05First Principles · Gray Areas

Four truths you can't prove and can't do without

First principles are self-evident. Once you understand one, your response is "of course". That is exactly why they can't be proven: there's nothing more basic to prove them from.

The first principles of logic (and of reason itself)

IdentityA thing is what it is.Reality is made of distinct individuals. An apple is an apple, not an orange.
Excluded middleBetween being and non-being there is no middle state.Becoming isn't a halfway house. Elaine isn't becoming Elaine. She is becoming a better musician.
Sufficient reasonThere is a sufficient reason for everything.Nothing in the physical world explains itself. Something causes it to exist, or to exist in the way it does.
ContradictionNothing can both be and not be at the same time and in the same respect.You can be in New York in body and in San Francisco in mind, but not in both cities in body.

McInerny's focus is contradiction. Avoiding contradiction amounts to avoiding falsehood. We sometimes hold contradictory beliefs on purpose by not looking at the facts ("don't bother me with the facts, I've made up my mind"). That isn't reasoning. It's rationalization, reasoning in the service of falsehood.

Real gray areas, manufactured gray areas

Life has real gray areas, and they should be accepted cheerfully. But gray only exists because black and white do. If you can't see clear alternatives, that doesn't mean none exist. Don't project your own uncertainty onto the world. The fact that you recognise uncertainty shows you have known certainty, so certainty is possible here too, eventually.

06Explanations · Causes

Trace effects back to causes, and keep going

Everything has an explanation eventually. To know a cause is often to be able to control it, and through it the effect.

  • Every effect carries the mark of its cause, so you can size up a cause by studying the effect. Milk is all over the kitchen floor. The three ants on the counter couldn't have done it, and neither could the canary. The neighbour's cat in the yard could. That's not proof, but the cat is now "under serious suspicion".
  • Causes come in series. If A causes B and B causes C, fixing B leaves the problem in place. Laziness and impatience both push us towards the quick fix.
  • A thing is understood more fully the more of its four causes you can name. Not everything has all four. An idea, for example, has no material cause.
  • Among efficient causes, the principal cause (the cellist) matters more than the instrumental cause (the cello), although both are needed. A great player can do more with a poor instrument than a poor player can with a great one. Don't invest in instruments and neglect the people who play them.

Don't stop short in the search for causes

Sam can keep emptying the reeking bucket under the sink, or he can fix the pipe that keeps filling it. B is the immediate cause of the smell. A is the ultimate one.

Four causes of a birdhouse

McInerny takes this Aristotelian scheme from the efficient cause, which most of us mean by "cause", out to purpose, material and form.

07Define · Categorical · Generalize

Define your terms, then say how much you mean

Defining a term is giving its object a precise location: first the class it belongs to, then what sets it apart.

Two-step definition

term  = proximate genus  + specific difference

man     = animal          + rational
justice = social virtue   + rendering each their due
fear    = emotion         + that makes us withdraw
                            from perceived danger

The genus should be the nearest class. Toys are in the toy box, the room, the house and Lansing, Iowa, but the toy box is the useful answer. "Thing" or "concept" is too broad. When you don't yet know something's nature well enough to define it, give a careful description instead.

Four kinds of general statement

Affirmative Negative Universal
All S are PAll whales are mammals.Precise, with no exceptions.
No S are PNo fish have feet.Severs subject from predicate completely.
Particular
Some S are PSome mammals are arboreal."Some" could mean 2% or 99%.
Some S are not PSome potatoes are not new.Severs only part of the class.

Quantity (universal or particular) and quality (affirmative or negative) are the two dials of every statement. A trap: "All dogs are not mongrels" looks universal but means not all, so it's a particular negative.

  • Speak categorically only when you're certain. "The radio is in the back seat" gives knowledge. "The radio may be in the back seat" doesn't. But a categorical form doesn't make something fact: "The Cubs are the best team in baseball" is an opinion in confident clothing.
  • Make qualifiers explicit. "Carthaginians are cruel" implies all without saying so, which leaves the speaker an escape hatch. Say "all" or "some".
  • Be as precise as your knowledge allows. "Sixteen percent of runners finished in under two hours" beats "some runners were fast".
Part 3 · Sections 1–19

Argument: the language of logic

An argument is reasoning made public. The heart of it is the inferential move: accepting one statement as true because of another. This is the longest part of the book.

08Founding an Argument

Premises hold up a conclusion

However complicated an argument gets, it has only two parts: supporting statements and a supported one.

Anatomy of an argument

Logical indicators mark the roles: because, since, on account of for premises, and therefore, thus, so for conclusions. An argument can also explain rather than prove ("Because he kept fighting with his boss, Dave was transferred to Houston"). Even then, the explanation only works if the premise is true.

  • Universal → particular is safe. If every dog is carnivorous, then some dogs are. If you know something about the whole group, you know it about any part. The conclusion follows necessarily.
  • Particular → universal is not. "Some women are mothers" can't support "every woman is a mother". A part can't contain the whole. You can still go from part to whole, but only to a probable conclusion. Every Clare native I've met has red hair, so it may be that they all do. McInerny calls the overreach one of humanity's "favorite fallacies".
  • Avoid multiple conclusions. The strongest arguments try to make a single point.
09Predication · Negatives · Comparison

Joining ideas, separating them, and comparing them

A statement is the mind's most basic comparison: it relates a subject to a predicate. It's sound when the link it makes is a real link in the world.

  • Predication attributes something to something else. "Measles is infectious" is sound because the two ideas really belong together. "Jane Austen wrote Persuasion in New Hampshire" is false because they don't.
  • Negative statements disconnect ideas: completely ("No philosophers are infallible") or partly ("Some North Dakotans don't read Dickens"). Where the same thing can be said either way, the affirmative is more direct and points attention at what is the case. Negatives are good for correcting sweeping claims ("Not every artist is neurotic"). Softening a blunt verdict with a negative ("perhaps not the most prudent decision") can be fine. Let circumstances decide how blunt to be.
  • Comparison is how thought works at all. No two things are so alike that they stop being two, and none are so unlike that they share nothing (at least they both exist). What counts is the significance of the shared traits, not their number. Tell an audience everything mice and elephants have in common and leave out size, and you've misled them.
  • Argument by analogy: A and B share traits R through Y. A also has Z, so B probably has Z too. It's useful when B can't be inspected directly, as with a planned military operation compared to Vietnam. The conclusion is probable at best, and only as strong as the significance of the shared traits.
10Sound Argument · Truth & Relevance of Premises

Sound means true content in a valid form

Truth belongs to statements. Validity belongs to the structure that links them. A sound argument needs both.

—

"Valid" means the structure is such that if the premises are true, the conclusion must be true. It says nothing about whether they actually are.

  • Why argue at all instead of trusting an expert? Because with a sound argument you see for yourself. You know the reasons behind your knowledge.
  • True isn't enough. Premises must also be relevant. Pierre Poseur was an All-American, made his first million before thirty, and has a winning smile. All true, and none of it bears on whether he should be governor. His opponent's Peace Corps service, law career, two terms as mayor and twelve years in the legislature do.
  • Facts vs. values. "Musicians make music" can ground a solid argument. "Musicians are superior people" can't, because evaluations can be argued over endlessly. A value judgment is as reliable as the facts under it: trust Robert Frost on poetry and Ted Williams on baseball, but not the other way round.
11Conjunctive · Disjunctive · Conditional

"And", "or", and "if… then"

The simple compound arguments, each with a clear set of valid moves. The conditional is the one we use most and misuse most.

Conjunctive: A · B

Anne is a sophomore AND majors in biology.
A · B          A · B
∴ A            ∴ B

It's a package deal. If the whole is true, each part is true, so you can't deny either half.

Disjunctive: A ∨ B (exclusive)

Arden took the train OR the plane.
A ∨ B    A ∨ B    A ∨ B    A ∨ B
A        B        ¬A       ¬B
∴ ¬B     ∴ ¬A     ∴ B      ∴ A

This needs a minor premise telling you which side holds. "Either A or B" also promises that one of them is true. If you mean "neither", say so.

Conditional: if A, then B

Affirming the antecedentvalid
If Louise is running, she is moving.
She is running.
∴ She is moving.
Denying the consequentvalid
If Louise is running, she is moving.
She is not moving.
∴ She is not running.
Denying the antecedentinvalid
If Louise is running, she is moving.
She is not running.
∴ She is not moving.

She could be walking, rocking in a chair or rolling over in her sleep.

Affirming the consequentinvalid
If Louise is running, she is moving.
She is moving.
∴ She is running.

Running is one way to move, not the only one.

The strict conditional needs a necessary link between antecedent and consequent: you can't run without moving. Most everyday if/thens aren't like that. "If it's nice on Thursday we'll picnic" can fail in many ways. They still have value, but their conclusions are probable, and only as strong as the link. A friend's "If I win the lottery I'll give it all to charity" carries a very big if. "If Uncle Louis comes to town he'll want Schmidty's" rests on fifteen years of evidence.

12Syllogism · Form · Quantity · Quality

The syllogism: relating a part to a whole

If M is part of P and S is part of M, then S is part of P. You can literally see the conclusion follow.

major premise   Every M is P     Every NFL player is a professional athlete.
minor premise   Every S is M     The Vikings are NFL players.
conclusion    ∴ Every S is P     The Vikings are professional athletes.

M = middle term (appears in both premises, never in the conclusion)
P = major term · S = minor term · exactly three terms, no more

Why some syllogisms work and others only look like they do

The middle term has to cover its whole class (be "distributed") at least once, or it can't connect the other two. The predicate of an affirmative statement never covers its whole class. "Every squirrel is a mammal" doesn't mean every mammal is a squirrel. So when "mammal" sits in the predicate of both premises, it links nothing. A wool cap and a wool sweater are both wool, and they are still not the same thing.

An invalid form can still be used honestly if you state its conclusion as probable. Halverson and Policinski were both at the Chicago conference, so it's possible they met.

13Induction · Assessing · Constructing

Deduction unpacks, induction gathers

The real difference: deduction can yield necessary conclusions, induction only probable ones. Yet induction supplies deduction with its starting points.

Two directions of reasoning

Harry noticed his golden retriever flinch when one niece reached down over its head, then accept the other niece's open hand raised from below. He repeated the test with all five dogs and got the same result every time. His conclusion is tentative and fair. Science runs on exactly this. A hypothesis says where to look, and the sample has to be big enough to hold all the variety of the whole group.

Assessing an argument, in order

1. Is there an argument at all?
   A point repeated loudly is not a conclusion.
   An unsupported claim is just an opinion.
2. Are the premises true?
   Watch for claims that are nearly true.
3. Are they relevant and strong enough?
4. Is the structure valid?
   Does it claim necessity it doesn't have?
5. Does it match how things actually are?

Constructing one

  • Know your single point, then put your effort into the premises.
  • Check every fact beforehand. A claim with several parts must be true in all of them. If your candidate served in Vietnam but wasn't a Marine and never won the Purple Heart, the whole argument collapses.
  • Cut weak premises. They distract from strong ones. Even among strong premises, fewer gives sharper focus.
  • Know your audience. Telling a British audience that Burns is "a Buckeye from birth" won't land. Logic is a science, but persuasion is an art.
Part 4 · Sections 1–9

Where illogic comes from

Some errors are accidents and some are carelessness. The more serious ones come from fixed attitudes that warp reasoning before it starts.

14Skepticism · Agnosticism · Cynicism · Narrowness

Every bad posture has a healthy twin

The problem is never doubt, caution or focus as such. It's turning a fitting response into a permanent stance.

Attitude
Healthy form
Corrosive form
Skepticism
Selective doubt: hold off judging until there's enough evidence, especially when the premises look shaky.
Doubt as a worldview. "There is no truth" refutes itself. "Truth exists but we can't reach it" amounts to the same thing.
Agnosticism
Honestly admitting ignorance after real investigation.

The pearl earring on the football field

Narrowing a search arbitrarily throws away 90% of your chances. The opposite failure is just as real. In Chesterton's line, an open mind, like an open mouth, should eventually close on something.

15Emotion · Purpose · Quarrels · Sincerity · Common Sense

Reason leads, emotion comes along

The stronger the feeling, the harder it is to think clearly. The aim is to keep reason in front, not to cut emotion out.

  • Emotion. Reason and emotion share the same home, and no idea is free of feeling. But a conclusion should be accepted because it's true, not because it feels good. His rule of thumb is never to appeal directly to emotions. Bring people to where they can see for themselves.
  • The reason for reasoning. Some notorious criminals reasoned flawlessly from false premises. Consistency isn't logic. Logic serves truth. Using argument to vent, get even or score points misuses it, and winning "at all costs" is a price nobody can afford.
  • The limits of sincerity. You must mean what you argue. Arguing a position you don't hold is an abuse of reason. But sincerity doesn't make you right. You can be completely sincere and still dead wrong.
  • Common sense. Logic is more than common sense, but it grows out of it: a respect for the obvious, for language that reveals rather than conceals, and for the difference between a cat and a kangaroo.
Argument

16Formal fallacies · §1–4

Broken forms

The four formal fallacies all mirror a rule from Part Three. If you know the valid form, the invalid one stands out.

  • Denying the antecedent and affirming the consequent are the two invalid twins of the conditional (see chapter 11). Both assume A is the only way to get B.
  • Undistributed middle, also known as guilt by association. Belonging to a club that had Nazi members may raise questions about Hans, but it proves nothing.
  • Equivocation looks like a three-term syllogism but has four, because one word quietly changes meaning between the premises. Restate each premise in what it really means ("Love of neighbour is altruism. Don Juan was a philanderer.") and there's nowhere left to go.

Proof that isn't

These look like arguments but skip the work of supporting the conclusion.

Begging the question"All the people at the table had shaved heads; Jim was at the table…" You could only know "all" if you already knew about Jim.Circular arguments hide by putting pages between the two halves.
False assumptionAssumptions are fine as working tools, as long as they don't contradict themselves or the facts. A false starting point can only lead to a false conclusion.
"You can't disprove it"The question is still open. Lack of proof against a view isn't proof for it.
False dilemmaFrom the Greek for "two possibilities". It manufactures urgency so you'll pick the lesser evil the arguer chose for you.
Post hocThe fuse burns, then the dynamite explodes, so order in time is relevant to cause. It just isn't enough on its own.

Changing the subject

The diversion fallacies don't try to answer the argument. They try to make the audience forget it.

Straw manHonest misreadings happen with complicated arguments. The fallacy is a deliberate distortion.
Ad hominemIt can win the room. But by preventing a fair hearing, you didn't win the argument in any logical sense.
Red herringTwo faults: it appeals to emotion rather than reason, and the issue it raises has nothing to do with the question.
Laughter and tearsRidicule dismisses an argument and pathos drowns it. Neither engages it.
ForceYou can coerce behaviour, but you can't coerce belief. In argument, coercion always backfires.
19§8–10, 13, 15

Borrowed authority

Age, precedent, majorities, credentials and reputations are all worth noting. None of them settles the matter.

  • Tradition cuts both ways. A practice's age is no reason to keep it, and no reason to drop it either. It may have lasted because it's good. Judge the practice itself.
  • "It's all right to do ___ because ___ has already been done" turns precedent into permission. What others did is of historical interest only.
  • Majority opinion is interesting sociology, but it doesn't make anything true. It is hard to stand against a crowd, which is what makes this fallacy so persuasive.
  • Appealing to genuine experts in their own field is legitimate. The strongest expert evidence includes the experts' reasons. A bare "trust us" amounts to "don't ask questions".
  • A bad source makes a bad product likely, not certain. Look at where Mr. Petri studied, then look at Mr. Petri.
20§14, 16–18, 25–28

Wholes, parts and ends

The last group distorts reality by cutting it down: to numbers, to parts, to the convenient facts, to the bottom line, or to an easy answer.

  • Quantifying quality. We measure blue as a wavelength, but what we see is blue. A number gives a false sense that we know a quality better. The most important things (love, beauty, kindness, justice, freedom, peace) may resist measurement altogether.
  • Analysis without synthesis. The parts of a clock piled on a table are not a clock. Understanding means knowing how the parts make the whole.
  • Reductionism picks some of the parts and calls them the whole, as when someone lists a person's faults and claims to have shown who they are.
  • Special pleading and expediency both serve a goal at truth's expense: one by leaving out facts, the other by ignoring everything about a means except whether it works.
  • Avoiding conclusions uses reason to undermine reason. Some problems may be unsolvable, but not problems as such.
  • Simplistic reasoning exploits audiences who want easy answers. Don't tell people what they want to hear. Tell them what is true.
Afterword

Logic is learned by practice

McInerny ends where a coach would. Knowing the fallacies matters, but mastering the positive principles matters more, and that only happens by using them. Every waking hour gives you a chance.

He borrows Pindar's advice to "become what you are", meaning become human. Being logical isn't the whole of being human, he says, but it is a very important part of it.

The only thing really worth feeling good about is the truth.

The toolkit

The book is already a manual, so this is the short version: twelve habits worth keeping.

Before you argue

Go and check the garageSettle a fact by looking at the world, not by thinking harder about your ideas.
Define the slippery wordsName the genus, then the difference. Do it for justice, fairness, love: any word your argument depends on.
Say "all" or "some"Make the quantity explicit and claim only as much as you know.
Find the root causeAsk "and what caused that?" until you reach the leaky pipe.

Building an argument

Make one pointA single conclusion, and premises that are true, relevant and wide enough to contain it.
Run both testsAre the premises true? Is the form valid? Only "yes" to both is sound.
Label probable as probableInduction, analogy and everyday if/thens can't give certainty. Say "may".
Cut the weak premisesFewer, stronger reasons, chosen for the audience in front of you.

When someone argues at you

Is there an argument at all?A claim repeated loudly is an opinion. Find the premises before you engage.
Draw the circlesDo the two groups really connect, or do they just share a box? Is there another way to get B?
Notice the detourAttacks on the person, red herrings, jokes and tears are all signs the argument is being avoided.
Argue, don't quarrelAim at the truth, not the person, and leave alone those who won't reason.
