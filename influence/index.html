The big idea

We say yes on autopilot, and mostly that's smart

Cialdini opens by calling himself a pushover. He bought magazines he never read and tickets to charity events he never went to. So he studied why people say yes, first in the lab and then by taking jobs as a trainee in sales, fundraising and advertising to learn the craft from the people who live by it.

His conclusion: we can't weigh every fact behind every decision, so we use shortcuts. A single cue (a price, a title, a favour, a crowd) stands in for the full picture, and we react automatically. He calls it click, whirr: the cue switches on a pre-recorded response, like a tape that starts playing.

The shortcuts usually work. That's why they exist. The danger is people who fake the cue. A professional persuader doesn't need force. Like a judo fighter, they use our own momentum against us, and we walk away believing we decided freely.

This new edition adds a seventh principle, unity, plus "E-boxes" on how each principle works online, and readers' reports from people who caught the principles at work in their own lives.

01Weapons of Influence

Shortcuts make us efficient, and exploitable

An automatic response to a reliable cue saves time and energy. It also means anyone who can fake the cue can trigger the response.

  • A mother turkey cares for her chicks only when they make a "cheep-cheep" sound. Give a stuffed polecat, her natural enemy, a tape recorder playing the sound, and she takes it under her wing. Switch it off and she attacks.
  • We have triggers too. A friend's jewellery shop couldn't sell a case of turquoise. Before a trip she left a note to sell it at half price. The assistant misread it as double, and the lot sold out. Buyers who knew nothing about turquoise used the rule expensive = good.
  • The same rule changes experience: people who paid full price for an "energy drink" solved more puzzles than those who got it at a discount. A painkiller described as costing $2.50 worked better than the same pill at 10 cents.
  • We think carefully only when we're both motivated and able. Rushed, tired, distracted, emotional or overloaded, we take the shortcut even when the stakes are high. Air crash investigators call the deadly version "captainitis": crews who see the captain's mistake and say nothing.
  • The contrast principle is a lever that's almost invisible. Clothing staff sell the suit before the sweater, because after $500 a $95 sweater feels cheap. Estate agents show a couple of overpriced "set-up" houses first.

The core framework

Seven principles, three jobs

Each principle is a shortcut that usually leads to a good decision. Following Gregory Neidert's model, Cialdini sorts them by what a persuader needs at each stage: a good relationship, then less uncertainty, then a push to act.

Three overlapping goals

A friend can prove that everyone and every doctor favours daily exercise and still not get you off the sofa. For that, they'd remind you of what you've said about your health (consistency) and what you'd lose without it (scarcity).

Every principle has the same shape. There's a rule of thumb that's right most of the time: return favours, trust experts, follow the crowd when you're lost. Because it's usually right, we apply it without thinking. And because we apply it without thinking, it can be counterfeited.

Cialdini is clear that using these principles isn't wrong in itself. A real expert who mentions her expertise, or a product that's genuinely popular, gives us honest information. The line is crossed when the cue is manufactured: fake reviews, a borrowed lab coat, a "limited offer" that never runs out.

So each chapter ends the same way: how the principle works, how it gets exploited, and how to defend yourself without becoming cynical about everyone who uses it honestly.

Goal 1 · Chapters 2, 3 and 8

Build the relationship

People listen to a message more favourably when they feel positive about the sender. Three principles create that feeling: owing someone, liking them, and feeling that they're one of us.

02Reciprocation

Give first, and people feel they owe you

Every human culture teaches us to repay what others give us. The rule is so strong that it works even for gifts we never asked for.

  • The rule is the glue of society: because the receiver is obliged to repay, the giver can give without losing out. In 1985 famine-struck Ethiopia sent $5,000 to Mexico City's earthquake victims, because Mexico had helped Ethiopia when Italy invaded in 1935.
  • It overrides liking. In Dennis Regan's study, an assistant who bought a participant an unasked-for Coke sold him clearly more raffle tickets, whether or not the participant liked him.
  • It works for uninvited favours, so we don't even get to choose whom we owe. A veterans' charity's appeal letter worked 18% of the time; with free address labels inside, 35%.
  • It produces unequal trades. To shake off the feeling of being in debt we agree to more than we got. Regan's Coke cost 10 cents and brought in 50 cents: a 500% return.
  • Gifts work best when they're personal, meaningful and unexpected. A single sweet with the bill raised tips by 3.3%; two sweets per guest raised them by 14%. Restaurants that gave kids a balloon at the door saw families spend 25% more.
  • Concessions count as gifts too. In rejection-then-retreat (door-in-the-face), you ask for something big, accept a no, then retreat to what you wanted all along. The other side feels they must concede in return.

A boy scout, two chocolate bars, and an experiment

The story · Watergate

How did Nixon's re-election committee approve the absurd, risky Watergate break-in? G. Gordon Liddy's first proposal cost $1 million and included a surveillance plane, kidnappings and a yacht with call girls. It was rejected. A week later a trimmed plan at $500,000 was rejected too. The third, at $250,000, "only" bugged the Democrats' offices, and after two retreats it felt like a concession that had to be returned. When the burglars were caught, Jeb Magruder asked: how could we have been so stupid?

How to say no

Don't refuse every offer: that makes you cold and costs you real favours. Accept gifts and concessions in good faith. But if one turns out to be a sales device, mentally redefine it as a trick. The rule says to repay favours, not tricks, so you owe nothing.

03Liking

We say yes to people we like

No surprise there. The surprise is how many small, manageable things make us like someone.

  • The Tupperware party is a masterclass: games and prizes (reciprocity), guests describing what they already own (commitment), and above all the request coming from a friend, not a stranger. A Tupperware party starts somewhere every 1.8 seconds. 92% of consumers trust recommendations from people they know.
  • Physical attractiveness creates a halo: we assume good-looking people are also more talented, kind and intelligent. 73% of voters denied looks had any influence on them.
  • Similarity works from the age of nine months. In a study of 421 million potential matches on a dating site, it was the single biggest factor. Salespeople fake it: camping gear in your boot, "Oh, I love getting out of town too."
  • Compliments work even when we know they're flattery. Most powerful: genuine ones said behind your back, or ones you want to live up to.
  • Contact and cooperation. Familiarity helps only in pleasant settings. In the Robbers Cave summer camp, contact between rival groups made things worse; shared problems (a broken water supply, a film they could only afford together) made them friends. The jigsaw classroom, where each pupil holds one piece of the lesson, builds friendships across ethnic lines.
  • Association. We link people with the news they bring. Persian messengers who brought victory were feasted; those who brought defeat were killed. TV weather presenters still get hate mail for rain. Fans say "we won" but "they lost".

Don't try to spot every tactic; there are too many. Watch for the result instead: if you find yourself liking a salesperson more than you'd expect in this short time, step back. Separate the person from the offer and judge the deal on its own merits. You'd want the car even if he were rude to you?

08Unity · new

We say yes to people who are "us"

Liking is "they're like us". Unity goes deeper: "they are us". A shared identity makes another person's interests feel like our own.

  • The "we" is a shared self, defined by tribe-like categories: family, ethnicity, home region, nation, religion, politics. We favour in-group members, take their preferences as guides, and these tendencies evolved because they helped the group, and us, survive.
  • Car seller Ali Reda beat Joe Girard's record in Dearborn, Michigan, the largest Arab-American community in the US, where he's a well-known member. International referees favour players from their own country about 10% more often.
  • Being together: kinship. Cialdini offered students one extra exam point if a parent filled in a long questionnaire. Response rates for adults are usually below 20%. 159 of 163 parents (97%) sent it back.
  • Being together: place. Home, neighbourhood and region create a family-like feeling. Children raised in homes open to many kinds of guests grow up more willing to help strangers.
  • Acting together. Moving, singing or suffering in sync makes people feel like one. Music is the scalable version: 87% of 1,513 TV ads studied used it.
  • Co-creation. Ask for advice, not opinions or expectations. Advice puts people in your shoes and blends their identity with yours. Managers who thought they'd helped shape an ad campaign rated it 50% higher than colleagues who hadn't.

The story · Chiune Sugihara

In 1940 the Japanese consul in Lithuania, a mid-ranking diplomat who loved parties and gambling, defied his superiors and wrote thousands of visas for Jewish refugees, then lost his post for it. Cialdini points to his childhood: his parents ran their home like an inn, cooking for, housing and washing the clothes of travellers of all kinds, many too poor to pay. Sugihara learned to draw the line of "family" very wide.

The dark side, and the defence

"We" can excuse wrongdoing. Companies whose codes of conduct said "we" instead of "employees" had more misconduct, and in experiments people given the "we" version lied and cheated more, expecting to be watched less and forgiven more. Unions protecting cheats and churches covering for abusers follow the same loyalty. Ask whether the shared identity is real, and whether it's being used to wave something through.

Goal 2 · Chapters 4 and 5

Remove doubt

A good relationship isn't enough. Before we say yes we want evidence that it's a sensible choice, ideally from people like us or from someone who knows better.

04Social Proof

When unsure, we copy others

We decide what's correct by looking at what people around us do. Pointing out that something is popular makes it more popular.

  • It works almost everywhere. A menu label saying "most popular" lifted a dish's sales by 13–20%. "Our most popular is the McFlurry" lifted McFlurry sales by 55%. Dutch teens told that most peers ate fruit ate 35% more. Louisville, Kentucky told ticket recipients that most people pay within two weeks, and payments rose by 130%.
  • Three conditions make it strongest. Uncertainty: in a clear emergency people help; in an ambiguous one they look at each other and do nothing. The many: if four people stare at an empty patch of sky, 80% of passers-by look up. Crowds signal that something is valid, doable and socially accepted. Similarity: we copy people like us.
  • The bystander effect: the 1964 Kitty Genovese case (later disputed in its details) made the image of 38 neighbours who did nothing famous. If you need help, don't call out to the crowd: pick one person. "You, in the blue jacket, call an ambulance."
  • Similarity has a dark edge. David Phillips found that after a widely reported suicide, suicides and fatal "accidents" rise among people similar to the victim. In Jonestown, Jim Jones moved his followers to the Guyanese jungle, where the only people like them were each other, before more than 900 died.
  • If you're not popular yet, point to the trend: "more and more people are…" works as future social proof.
  • Watch for counterfeits: canned laughter, staged queues, bought reviews. 98% of online shoppers say authentic reviews are their biggest influence, which is exactly why they get faked.

The story · a phobia cure

Children terrified of dogs watched a boy happily playing with a dog for 20 minutes a day. After four days, 67% were willing to climb into a playpen with a dog and pet it while everyone else left the room. Watching films of several different kids with several dogs worked even better. Social proof isn't only a trap; it's also a therapy.

How to say no

Stay alert to fake evidence of what similar people are doing: staged testimonials, laugh tracks, inflated numbers. And look up now and then: the crowd may be watching you while you watch them, so nobody actually knows. The behaviour of similar others shouldn't be your only basis for a decision.

05Authority

We defer to authority, and to its costumes

Obeying real experts is usually wise, so we learn to do it without thinking. Then the mere symbols of authority are enough.

  • In Stanley Milgram's experiment, ordinary adults were told to shock a "learner" for every wrong answer. Despite his screams, 65% went all the way to 450 V because a researcher in a lab coat told them to continue. Experts had predicted 1–2%. Women did the same. When the learner asked to stop, they continued; when the researcher said stop, 100% stopped.
  • Hospitals show the risk. A doctor prescribed ear drops for a patient's painful right ear and wrote "place in R ear". The nurse read "Rear" and put them in the patient's anus. Neither questioned it. In another study, 95% of nurses were ready to give an unknown drug at double the maximum dose on a phone instruction from a "doctor" they'd never met.
  • Three symbols work on us. Titles: the same man introduced to Australian students as a student, then as a lecturer, up to professor, "grew" about 1.5 cm in their estimates with each step. Clothes: a man in a security uniform got nearly all passers-by to give a stranger coins for a parking meter; in plain clothes, fewer than half. Trappings: when a luxury car didn't move at a green light, half the drivers behind waited without honking. Behind an old economy car almost everyone honked, some more than once.
  • Expert authority beats positional authority, because nobody resents advice from someone who knows more. Most persuasive of all is a credible authority: expert and trustworthy.
  • The trust trick: admit a small weakness first. "Avis: We're number two. We try harder." The honesty makes the strengths that follow more believable.

How far people went

Milgram's subjects weren't sadists. They sweated, trembled and begged to stop, and still obeyed. Cialdini's reading: deference to legitimate authority is trained into us from childhood because it usually pays.

Source: Milgram's obedience studies, ch. 5

A title adds centimetres

Students estimated the height of the same visitor. Each step up in his announced status added about 1.5 cm. People also underestimate how much a uniform sways them: asked to predict, they guessed 63% would obey a uniformed man. In the real study, 92% did.

How to say no

Ask two questions. Is this authority truly an expert on this topic? That moves your attention from symbols (a title, a coat, a car) to substance. How truthful can I expect this expert to be here? What do they gain from my yes? And be wary of the small confession that comes right before the big claim.

Goal 3 · Chapters 6 and 7

Prompt action

Even when we like someone and believe them, we may never get round to it. Two principles turn agreement into action: the fear of missing out, and the wish to stay true to ourselves.

06Scarcity

What's rare, or slipping away, seems precious

The idea of losing something motivates us more than the idea of gaining something of the same value.

  • Loss aversion sits at the heart of Daniel Kahneman's prospect theory. British energy customers were 45% more likely to switch supplier to avoid a loss than to save the same amount. Team members were 82% more likely to cheat to avoid losing status than to gain it.
  • Persuaders use limited numbers ("only a few left") and deadlines ("we have to delete the files within 24 hours"). A salesman's favourite: "Sorry, I sold that one to another couple 20 minutes ago."
  • Scarcity works for two reasons. Things that are hard to get usually are better, so availability is a quick proxy for quality. And losing access means losing a freedom. Reactance makes us want what we're denied. Toddlers went straight for a toy behind a high barrier and ignored the one behind a low one. Parental opposition can deepen teenage love (the Romeo and Juliet effect).
  • It applies to information. Banned information becomes more wanted and more believed, before we even know what it says. Exclusive information persuades more than the same information shared widely.
  • Telling people they're free to refuse ("but you're free to say no") roughly doubled the success of requests: the opposite of scarcity, removing the threat to freedom.

The story · New Coke

In 1985 Coca-Cola replaced its classic formula with a sweeter "New Coke" that had won blind taste tests. The backlash was enormous. One retiree founded the Old Cola Drinkers of America. Within three months the original was back. Cialdini suspects the company's own research had shown it: people who knew they'd lose the old formula wanted it far more. Nobody was looking with a detective's eye for scarcity.

How to say no

Scarcity is hard to resist by thinking, because it attacks thinking: it raises our pulse and narrows our attention. So use the arousal itself as the signal. When you feel the rush ("Only today!"), stop, calm down, and ask why you want it and what it's really worth to you.

07Commitment & Consistency

Once we take a stand, we defend it

The key is the first commitment. After it, we're far more willing to agree to anything that fits it.

  • Bettors at a racetrack are more confident their horse will win right after placing the bet than right before. Nothing about the horse has changed.
  • We value consistency for three reasons: society treats it as a virtue, it makes us look reliable, and it saves effort. Once we've decided, we don't have to think again.
  • The foot in the door: a small first yes changes how we see ourselves ("I'm someone who supports good causes"), so we agree to the bigger request. People who first predicted whether they'd volunteer for a cancer charity were 700% more likely to say yes when called.
  • Commitments stick best when they're active, public, effortful and freely chosen. Amway has salespeople write their goals down. A diet app's users who shared their calories with friends lost 50% more weight. A restaurant cut no-shows from 30% to 10% by changing "Please call if you change your plans" to "Will you please call…?" and waiting for a yes.
  • In Korean War camps, Chinese captors got American prisoners to write mildly pro-communist essays for tiny rewards like rice or cigarettes. Because the rewards were small, the prisoners couldn't blame them, and many came to see themselves as collaborators.
  • Harsh initiation rites survive because effort creates commitment: the stronger the shocks women endured to join a group, the more interesting and intelligent the group seemed to them. Fraternities refuse "help week" community service for the same reason: it would let members credit an outside reason.
  • Commitments grow their own legs: we invent new reasons to support a choice, so it survives even after the original reason disappears. That's how the low-ball works: a car dealer offers a great price, you commit, then the price "turns out" to be higher, and you still buy.

What makes a commitment stick

  1. Active. Write it, say it, do it. Volunteers who actively signed up made up 74% of those who actually showed up.

  3. Effortful. The more it cost to get in, the more we value being in.

The story · the toy race track

One January Cialdini found himself buying his son another expensive toy. A former toy-industry colleague explained the trick: before Christmas, makers advertise a must-have toy, kids beg, parents promise. Then the shops are undersupplied, so parents buy something else of equal value. In January the ads return, and parents who promised buy the original too. Cabbage Patch Kids and Furbies played the same way.

How to say no

Listen to two signals. Your stomach knots when you're being pushed into something you don't want just to stay consistent. Then say so: agreeing would be foolish consistency, and you'd rather not. Your heart helps when you're unsure the first decision was right. Ask: "Knowing what I know now, if I could go back, would I make the same choice?" Trust the first feeling that answers. Be extra careful if you live in an individualist culture or are over 50: these tactics work best on you.

Chapter 9 · Epilogue

Primitive reflexes in an automated age

John Stuart Mill, who died in 1873, is said to have been the last person who knew everything there was to know. Today most of our information is less than 15 years old, and our phones out-compute old universities. We call it the information age, Cialdini notes, never the knowledge age.

The paradox: the large brain that made us the dominant species has built a world so fast and complex that we must handle it the way animals do, by responding to single cues. In a rush, stressed, unsure or tired, we'll lean on the seven shortcuts more, not less.

That's not a problem in itself. The shortcuts are reliable, and persuaders who point to real popularity, real expertise or real scarcity are doing us a favour. The abuse is fabricating the cue. Because we need our shortcuts to keep working, Cialdini argues we should fight counterfeiters with every legitimate means: refuse their products, call out their tricks.

Honest cue or counterfeit?

Honest use
  • Pointing out that a product genuinely sells well
  • A qualified expert mentioning her training
  • Telling buyers stock really is limited
  • A sincere, specific compliment
  • An unconditional gift
Counterfeit
  • Bought reviews, canned laughter, staged queues
  • An actor in a lab coat
  • A "last chance" that comes back every week
  • Invented hobbies to mirror yours
  • A "gift" designed to make you owe

The test isn't whether a principle is used. It's whether the cue reflects the truth.

The toolkit

For every principle, a way to spot it and a way to say no, plus how to use it honestly yourself. The defences don't ask you to distrust everyone; only to notice when a shortcut has been faked.

Relationship

ReciprocationRedefine the trickTake the free sample in good faith. If it turns out to be bait, call it bait, and you owe nothing.
LikingSeparate seller and dealLiking someone surprisingly fast is the signal. Ask whether you'd buy it from a stranger.
UnityCheck the "we"Is the shared identity real, and is it being used to excuse something you'd never accept from an outsider?
Use it wellGive first, personallyMake gifts meaningful, unexpected and tailored. Ask colleagues and customers for advice, not opinions.

Uncertainty

Social proofLook for fake evidenceStaged testimonials, laugh tracks, bought reviews. And remember everyone else may be just as unsure as you.
AuthorityAsk the two questionsIs this person really an expert on this? How honest are they likely to be with me here?
In an emergencyPick one person"You, in the blue jacket, call an ambulance." It ends everyone's uncertainty at once.
Use it wellSay what most people do rightNever advertise how many people do the wrong thing. Mention a small weakness before your strength.

Action

ScarcityTreat the rush as a warningWhen "only today" makes your pulse rise, pause, calm down and ask what it's worth to you.
ConsistencyTrust your stomach and heartKnowing what you know now, would you make the same choice again? If not, say "that would be foolish consistency."
Low-ballCheck if the reason still holdsIf the deal that made you say yes has changed, your yes can change too.
Use it wellGet an active, voluntary yes"Will you…?" and a pause beats "Please do…". Frame what's at stake as something people already have to lose.
