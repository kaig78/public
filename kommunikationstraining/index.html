The big idea

Communicate with the person, not just the message

The book opens with a fable. Leo the lion roars "Who is king of the animals?" at a hare, a deer and a zebra, and they all agree. Emboldened, he even cows a rhino. Then he roars at an elephant, who taps him on the head with one foot and pushes him into the sand up to his neck.

Birkenbihl draws five lessons from it. We build a picture of ourselves by comparing ourselves with others. We act on that picture. The picture doesn't have to match reality. When the gap gets too big, communication suffers. And when communication suffers, so does our self-esteem (Selbstwertgefühl, which she shortens to SWG).

Everything else in the book hangs on that last point. In every exchange we listen, often unconsciously, for one thing: does the other person see me as OK? When we hear a no, we stop listening to the message and start defending ourselves.

First published in 1975 and revised for decades, the book is two books in one: a theory part you can read on its own, and a practice part of exercises and games, most of them for small groups.

The opening dialogue

"It's five to six, hurry up!" – "And you're still dawdling!" – "Of course, it's always my fault when we're late!" A couple heading out for the evening turn a question about the time into a fight in six lines. Neither said anything about the time again. Birkenbihl's question is why people react so "strangely", and how to react better.

The core framework

Three voices in every person

Birkenbihl borrows Eric Berne's transactional analysis. At any moment each of us speaks from one of three ego states, and each remark lands on one of the other person's three. Most of the book's advice can be read through this lens.

Parent, Adult, Child

P

Parent

Rules, morals, prejudices and habits copied from our parents. Comes in two forms: the critical Parent who scolds, and the nurturing Parent who comforts and helps.

"That's not how it's done!" · "Does it hurt?"
A

Adult

The analyst. Gathers facts, weighs them, draws conclusions. Its criticism finds errors instead of complaining about them.

"What does it cost?" · "The results show…"
C

Child

Wants, feelings, curiosity, play, sulking. Self-esteem lives here. When it feels attacked it becomes the weeping Child.

"I want that!" · "Why is it always my fault?"

She writes "childlike", never "childish": the labels describe, they don't judge. In private, let your Child out. At work, learn to tell your Child's wishes apart from Adult arguments ("This wall needs a picture to look balanced" often just means "I want a picture here").

Part I · Chapters 1–7

The theory

Each chapter opens with the same formula: "Communicating well means…" – respecting self-esteem, meeting needs, motivating properly, understanding transactions, not answering defence with defence, and checking what actually arrived.

01Self-Esteem

Self-esteem is the centre we refer everything to

Whenever the other person's self-esteem is hurt, communication suffers.

  • We can only learn our worth by comparison, so we depend partly on how others judge us. Those judgements always reach us through communication.
  • As long as we hear no judgement, or a positive one, we can focus on the message. Once we think we hear a negative one, we focus on the judgement instead.
  • Try her list: write down ten things you do and ask "why?" until you hit bottom. Everything we do, she argues, is ultimately done to keep, defend or raise our self-esteem.
  • Her experiments show how dependent we are: have four colleagues tell someone they look ill, or frown at someone naming animals ("the dog barks, the cat meows"). After three or four frowns most people lose their footing on the easiest task.
  • People who claim they never feel "not OK" are risky. They don't notice when they hurt others, and they rarely admit they were wrong.

Five factors that keep self-esteem intact

Factors 3 and 4 can only be given through communication: a kind word, praise, a sincere compliment. Factor 5 is a double one. A couple can be happy as partners and struggle physically, or the reverse, and either gap makes both feel not OK. Her advice: however angry you are, never attack someone there. Those wounds don't heal.

When the message beats the person

The hotel restaurant

An American couple ask for food just before midnight. The head waiter returns: "Unfortunately the kitchen is…" – "Don't you speak English?" – "…already closed…" – he finishes in German – "…but you can still have a sandwich." The wife is furious that a hotel like this can't even offer a sandwich. In her anger she hadn't heard the word.

The waiter focused so hard on his message that he forgot the people. Feeling ignored, the guest stopped thinking clearly. Upset people misunderstand things they would normally never miss.

Conscience

Benny, a Baptist insurance agent, may neither drink nor lie. When a client toasts a signed policy, he either drinks and feels guilty, or refuses and gets teased. Pushing someone to act against their conscience always attacks their self-esteem, and mocking it says "my conscience is better than yours".

02Human Needs

Meet people on the level where they are

Someone fighting for a lower level of needs can't hear anything about the higher ones.

  • Two openings with the same aim: "Bert, listen to what happened at work…" versus "Bert, I'd like your opinion on something. Got a minute?" The second asks for his view (recognition) and lets him choose to listen.
  • She sorts all needs into Maslow's five levels. Don't force a conversation on someone who is tired, hungry or has a headache. Someone worried about their job only hears what affects their security.
  • Levels 3 and 4 are psychological survival. Mocking someone's group or withholding recognition can trigger the same "brainless" reactions as a physical threat.
  • Strokes (Streicheleinheiten, from Berne): any attention counts, and negative strokes are better than none. A marriage is only truly over when the partners have nothing left to say to each other.
  • People who show off really need it: an insecure base makes them overdo level 4. Some people deliberately provoke anger, because being scolded at least means being noticed.

The needs tower

After an emergency landing, scientist Dr Ford stops thinking about his paper and asks about food, then water, then shelter. Only once small groups form among the survivors does he open his paper again. Pull out the lower blocks and the upper ones stop mattering.

Loved for being, or for doing?

"Being"-stroked
  • Loved simply because they exist
  • Feel accepted, set modest goals
  • Expect acceptance without effort
  • Ask "what happened?" when things fail
  • Think elites are born
"Doing"-stroked
  • Loved when they achieve something
  • Set high goals, motivated from within
  • Need praise for performance to stay OK
  • Ask "who did it?" when things fail
  • Think elites are made

Plot yourself on a line between the two: how you were stroked as a child, how you're stroked today, and how you stroke others. Balanced people's turns of phrase split about 50/50. The point is to praise each person in the currency they value.

03Motivation

Motivating means replacing an old behaviour

Speak to one of the other person's unmet needs and show them which behaviour satisfies it.

  • Only momentary behaviour changes instantly. Habits were learned, so changing them is a learning process, and learning takes time. Try putting on the other shoe first for a few weeks and count your relapses.
  • Three steps: recognise the other person's need, define its fulfilment as the goal, and describe the goal so vividly that the new behaviour follows. "Don't sell the steak, sell the sizzle."
  • The golden rule: good motivation leaves both sides satisfied. If only one side is, it was manipulation, like Jacob buying Esau's birthright for a bowl of stew. The manipulated person resents it later, and rightly so.
  • Negative motivation (Herzberg's "KITA", the kick) can work, but only with constant pressure and control. Bosses who use it and then complain that people slack off the moment they look away haven't understood it.
  • Positive motivation fails most often for two fixable reasons: "doing" people who get no praise, and praise that misses what matters to the person. Fake praise counts as three negative strokes once it's spotted.

Carrot or kick

Positive motivation
  • Shows a need being met
  • Needs no pressure
  • Needs little control: you can turn your back
  • The person keeps going on their own
Negative motivation
  • Threatens a need (job, bonus)
  • Needs lasting pressure
  • Needs constant control
  • The old behaviour returns as soon as nobody's watching

Try positive first. If you have to fall back on pressure, keep it fair: respect the person's dignity and guarantee their needs as long as they meet the demands.

Turn the threat around

✕ "If you don't eat your spinach, no playing!"
✓ "Eat your spinach now, then you can play."

✕ "Work cleanly or work somewhere else!"
✓ "Less waste means a bigger profit share for you."

// the negative version only needs your own needs;
// the positive one forces you to think about theirs

Her drill: rewrite six threats as carrots and notice which version comes to you more easily.

The gasket factory

Karl loads rubber blanks so carelessly that 20% of each batch is scrap. Foreman Weber listens and hears "nobody thanks you for doing it right" and "if good work paid more, it'd be different". So he explains the profit share and how scrap costs four times over: lost material, rerun production, inspection and disposal. Then he works out what less scrap would mean for Karl at year end, without a minute of extra work. Karl's old motive is swapped for a new one, and the new behaviour follows. Weber only needs to keep praising it now and then.

04Transactional Analysis

When the ball lands on the wrong player

Most crossed transactions happen when you aim at someone's Adult or Parent and hit their weeping Child.

  • Think of a friendly game of table tennis, where you play the ball so the other person can return it. In a crossed transaction the ball drops.
  • The weeping Child shows itself in moaning ("why does this always happen to me?"), apologising, self-blame, blaming others or circumstances, feeling accused when nobody accused you, making yourself smaller or bigger, belittling others, or fleeing.
  • Its shield is up, so arguments can't reach the Adult behind it. "Be reasonable!" makes things worse: it's a Parent remark that says "you idiot".
  • The fix: answer the weeping Child with the nurturing Parent, a reassuring stroke, until the shield comes down. Then switch back to the Adult. The wiser one gives in, and the wiser one is whoever sees what's going on.
  • Energy decides which state we speak from. Tired, stressed, sick or threatened, we fall back on ready-made Parent opinions or the Child's reflexes, because the Adult's analysis costs energy.

She adds an old prayer for the moments when someone is being especially obtuse: let me walk a mile in my neighbour's moccasins before I judge them.

05Defence Manoeuvres

Every defence says "I feel small right now"

What counts is not whether someone was attacked, but whether they feel attacked. And defence almost always provokes counter-defence.

  • Conversations full of defence turn into duels with only losers and apparent winners. Apparent victories have to be won again next time, like Leo, who needed fresh proof of his power with every animal.
  • The elephant would have looked far stronger if he had just walked on. Strength you have to prove, you don't have.
  • Following Thomas Harris, the goal is "I'm OK – you're OK". "I'm OK – you're not OK" comes across as arrogant. "I'm not OK – you're OK" leaves you feeling threatened. Either one invites defence.
  • Anger is part of defence, because it tempts us to hit back. Getting angry at a show-off proves his inflation worked.
  • Two rules come out of it: what's true is not what was said, but what the other person heard, and there's no guarantee they heard what you meant.

06Images and Fog

Every opinion is an investment the mind defends

Building an image of the world costs energy, so the organism protects it. Attack an image and you put the other person in a fog.

  • Images (Bilder) are stored ideas, opinions and pictures. Each one cost energy to make and is coloured by emotion, which adds to the investment. Every decision taken on the basis of an image raises it further.
  • Mrs Bauer believes Napoleon was French. She writes an indignant letter to a magazine that called him Corsican and gets a printed reply proving her wrong. Now she has a public decision riding on the old image, so changing it is even harder.
  • Grief is a massive image exchange: thousands of small images of a partner have to be replaced at once. That's why it takes months or years, and why sudden deaths are harder to bear.
  • Being preoccupied causes psychological fog. A driver dodging a crash hears nothing his boss says. Someone whose important image is under attack hears only fragments of your arguments.
  • A joke smashes two images together and releases the energy that kept them apart, as laughter if it's no threat to you, as indignation if it is. The more OK you feel, the more easily you laugh at yourself.
  • Your self-image is the most important image of all, and it shows without words. The more OK you see yourself, the more OK you come across.

Images are stacked like bricks

Knock out a low brick and everything resting on it is in danger. That's why an attack on a basic belief feels like an attack on the whole person, and why the defence is so fierce. Diagram drawn for this page.

The wife who wanted to work

Mrs Maurer's husband believes a wife should only work if money forces her to. Every time she raises it directly, he goes into the fog. So she starts from his own complaint that she's been on edge, mentions how cheerful she was the week she helped the writer next door sort his archive, and lets him reach the conclusion himself: "I'm beginning to see where this is going." Then she drops the subject. Next time the ground is prepared.

07Feedback Techniques

Check what arrived, and show that you accept

Responsibility for being understood lies first with the sender. If the sender forgets, the receiver should check back.

  • "Is the report done?" – "How could it be, Karsten is still at the land registry!" – "I meant the one for the tax office." One check-back ("Do you mean the building report?") would have saved the exchange.
  • Paraphrase: say the other person's message back in your own words. "May I sum up?" doesn't signal that you weren't listening, it proves that you were.
  • The language of non-acceptance: judging, preaching, moralising, warning, ordering, interfering, interrupting, controlling. It makes people defensive, or makes them keep their problems to themselves.
  • Acceptance has to be shown: eye contact, a smile, a nod, your body turned towards the speaker. An insecure person reads the absence of praise as criticism. 71% of German managers found "If it doesn't taste good, you'll hear about it" rude at dinner, and most of them ran their teams exactly that way.
  • Interfering, as when a father redesigns his son's sandcastle, says "you're not good enough". Not interfering says the opposite, as long as it isn't indifference.
  • Learn all the theory you can, she says, quoting Jung, then forget it when you sit across from someone. Tune in to the person, not the message.

08Bio-Logic / Psycho-Logic

Under stress, the thinking brain goes offline

The weeping Child doesn't refuse logic out of stubbornness. Its stress hormones have partly switched off the part of the brain that does logic.

  • Following MacLean's model, she describes three layers: the ancient reptile brain (fight or flight, fear, rage, envy; barely able to learn), the limbic system (feelings and stress hormones) and the new brain, which she calls the "thinking brain".
  • When the reptile takes over, the limbic system releases stress hormones, and what's left is less Homo sapiens than "HoRmo sapiens", a creature run by its hormones.
  • Some people fall into the reptile brain now and then. Others react from it chronically. She calls the first kind of logic bio-logic, which often turns ordinary logic into pseudo-logic.
  • She separates bad stress (distress) from good stress (eustress), which produces "joy hormones" instead.
  • Watch for people who think they're in their Adult while their hormones are in charge. That's where "Now be reasonable!" comes from.

Three brains in one head

Seen this way, everything in the chapter on defence manoeuvres makes biological sense. A person under threat can't think clearly, listen, ask or argue in that moment, however much they want to.

The practical consequence is to lower the threat first and argue second. Her game "the four never-evers" (see Practice) lets you feel the difference from the inside.

09Agreeing to Differ

Two realities, and a word for agreeing to differ

When in doubt, say nothing, ask a question first.

  • We assume there's only one reality, our own. "It's hot in here," she sighs at a party. "Nonsense, it isn't hot!" says her husband, as though only his perception counts.
  • A travel agent tells a client who rejects charter flights, "You've got that wrong." A colleague doubts that a whole-food diet could cause real weight loss. In both cases a question ("What makes you say that?") would have opened the conversation. Whoever asks, leads.
  • Following Paul Watzlawick: first-order reality is what can be measured. Second-order reality is the meaning and value we give it: taste, religion, opinion. Most arguments are about the second while pretending to be about the first.
  • Watch out for "constraints of the facts" (Sachzwänge) used by managers, politicians, teachers and parents to push their own second-order reality.
  • English has "let's agree to differ"; German had nothing like it. So she coined Zweinigung, a blend of "two" and "agreement", which says: we see this differently, and that's fine. It also helps separate our opinion from our person.

The three-bowl experiment

One hand in hot water, one in iced water, for about a minute. Then both go into the lukewarm middle bowl. The same water feels icy to one hand and warm to the other. For chronic know-it-alls she suggests a four-bowl version.

“It can be bolder to doubt what is known than to explore the unknown.”
A line by Kaspar, quoted by Watzlawick, Beavin and Jackson, which Birkenbihl proposes as a motto (my translation)

Her exercise: for a few weeks, sort remarks you hear and read into first- and second-order realities, ideally with others. That this regularly causes heated debates, she notes, should give us pause.

10Levels of Communication

The relationship decides what the content means

Every message travels on two levels at once, and when the relationship level is disturbed the content gets lost.

  • Watzlawick's rule: every communication has a content level and a relationship level, and the second defines the first.
  • At a party one woman looks at another's necklace and says "Are those pearls real". Without the tone, nobody can know if it's admiration, envy or an insult, yet seminar groups always guess at once.
  • The content level is the "head" level, run by the thinking brain. The relationship level carries tone, face and gesture, and with them pressure, which can impress or oppress.
  • When the relationship is badly disturbed, stop pushing content. Talk about the relationship instead: "I have the feeling I've hurt you. I'm sorry." Or: "I think you're angry now. Is that right?" Only once the fog has cleared can you return to content.

Two channels, one message

Content level
What is said
  • Words, facts, information
  • Run by the thinking brain
  • Gets through freely while the relationship is neutral or good
Relationship level
How it is said
  • Tone, face, posture, silence
  • Information about the information
  • Once it turns negative, it drowns out the content
11Brain-Friendly?

Most learning problems are teaching problems

If you don't understand an explanation, the fault almost always lies with the sender.

  • Her own early research grew out of being a "victim of the school system". Education level is one of many factors that shape any conversation, so learning belongs in a book on communication.
  • Learning isn't cramming. Cramming forces in facts you haven't understood, which is fine for PIN numbers. Real learning gets easier the more you know, because new things hang on what's already there.
  • Successful learning releases dopamine, which makes you want more. It raises self-esteem and makes you more intelligent and more creative, because new ideas are surprising links between existing knowledge.
  • People who learn regularly also explain better. Her motto: there's no dry theory, only dry theorists.
12Language and Thought

Words don't just describe reality, they shape it

We never send 100% of a message. The listener fills in the rest from what they know, and doesn't notice doing it.

  • In a restaurant the waiter asks "Pork?" and someone says "That's me." Fine at dinner. Not fine with in-house acronyms and jargon, where the listener can't fill the gaps.
  • A warehouse burned down because a worker threw a cigarette end into an "empty" barrel. It was an empty petrol barrel, full of explosive fumes. The word "empty" told him it was safe.
  • Take words literally to see the pictures inside them. German Ent-täuschung (disappointment) is literally the end of a deception, and so, oddly, a gain.
  • Concepts such as "justice" are human ideals rather than facts of nature, which is no reason to give them up, but a reason to be less bitter when the world doesn't follow them.
13Closing Part I

Communicating well is a skill, like driving

You can't be perfect with everyone. Make the effort where the person or the outcome really matters.

  • Good communication costs energy. If you did it perfectly all the time you'd have none left for your work and your life.
  • Honesty has limits: some motives are unconscious, the truth can hurt the other person, and it can harm you with someone who doesn't want an open exchange. Always respect the other person's psychological privacy.
  • Kahlil Gibran's image: a couple shouldn't try to merge into one temple pillar, but be two pillars that carry the roof together.
  • Learner drivers think they'll never manage clutch, mirror and indicator at once, then do it without thinking. Communication works the same way: once a behaviour becomes a habit, it costs no extra energy.

Her learning plan

1 list the aspects you want to master
2 read Part II, tick the exercises that fit
3 decide how much time per day or week
4 carry a small notebook everywhere

week 1  hand out positive strokes
week 2  watch for the four never-evers
week …  one point per week, notes several times a day
then    one week off, start again at point 1

On your second round you'll find the first point has kept improving on its own. Share the plan with a friend or set up a small practice group: their encouragement is itself a stroke, and strokes speed up any learning.

Parts II–III · Practice

Exercises and games

Reading only gets you to "yes, yes". To change a habit you have to feel the theory, which is why the second half of the book is exercises, most of them for small groups without a trainer, just someone to run the game.

The four never-evers

The attackerScolds, nags, bangs the table."Typical, nobody listens to me!"
The trivialiserDoesn't take the problem or the speaker seriously."In fifty years it won't matter."
The "be reasonable"Repeats one sentence whose hidden message is "you idiot"."Oh, be reasonable."
The topic-changerAlways drifts off the subject."Apparently it'll rain tomorrow."

Four players are a family that has been arguing for two hours about where to go on holiday. The attacker starts, three rounds each, then everyone takes their left neighbour's role until each has played all four. Nobody ever agrees on a destination, and that's the lesson. Afterwards: which role was hardest? Which do you use in real life? Who do you know who's stuck in one?

The feelings wheel

An American tool for open talks. Two people sit facing each other and silently place chips on the feelings they have about each other: outer ring for slight, inner ring for intense.

A free zone takes feelings the wheel doesn't list, such as jealousy. The rest zone in the middle is where you put a chip when you need a pause. Then you talk about the chips, one by one or going round the wheel.

Illustrative: the book's printed wheel lists many more feelings

Feelings that can't be said get passed down the line: boss to father, father to mother, mother to child. One couple discovered at the wheel that he still brought up a batch of burnt steaks from years ago. She now laughs at his superior moments, and he catches himself.

Inventoryalone

The stick-figure test

Draw yourself next to your father and your mother. Are you bigger, smaller or the same size in self-esteem? Then draw a self-portrait, full body, and answer the questions on info sheet 5.

Inner attitude2 people

One minute, eyes closed

Think about a positive (or negative) aspect of the person opposite. Open your eyes; they have to guess which. Your attitude shows without a word.

Strokesgroup

One good thing each

Everyone tells everyone else one thing they like about them. Many people find positive things harder to say than they expected, even when they feel them.

Directness2 people

The one-word method

Hold a conversation one word per sentence: "Mother-in-law." – "Mother-in-law?" – "Tomorrow." – "Fear." – "Why?" – "Time." You catch your stock phrases, and you can't wrap feelings in "facts".

Body language2 people

Back to back, face to face

Talk back to back, like a couple calling from different rooms. Then face each other in silence, then with gestures only, eyes closed with touch only, and finally with everything at once.

Nervesmirror or partner

The towel test

Cover everything but your eyes and hold eye contact. After about two minutes it gets awkward. Notice which muscles tighten and where. Then do something mildly embarrassing in public and write down your reactions straight after.

Fearin real life

As-if, or admit it

A little nervous? Act as if you were confident for a minute or two, and others respond as if you were. Very nervous? Say so. Nothing disarms like admitting what the other person was about to notice anyway.

Questions2–10 people

The sawdust riddle

"A man enters his room, doesn't see the sawdust, and shoots himself." Find out why using closed questions only; each open question costs a point. The answer is on info sheet 6.

Alter egogroup

"I am you now"

Speak as another participant: "My name is Y, I've been sitting here since yesterday wondering what this is for…" They correct you. Then one player acts out someone's own habit, like constant interrupting, until that person sees it from the other side.

Afterword

Why it has stayed a standard work

First published in 1975, the book anticipated adult-education trends of the 1980s, transactional analysis among them, and passed 117,000 copies by its 13th edition. Birkenbihl kept revising it for decades, moving the "new thoughts" into the theory and answering readers' questions in later afterwords.

Some parts show their age: the examples are set in a 1970s gasket factory, the gender roles are of their time, and a few references now feel dated. The core argument hasn't aged at all. People defend their self-esteem before they listen, and whoever notices that first is the one who can rescue the conversation.

Her closing advice is to practise in company. Communication always happens with other people, so it's no surprise the best learning does too.

From theory and practice

The toolkit

The habits that come up again and again across the book, sorted by when you'd use them.

In the moment

Read defence as a signalApologising, showing off, leaving or attacking all mean the other person feels small. Don't answer in kind.
Stroke the weeping Child firstA reassuring word from the nurturing Parent, then back to facts once the shield is down.
Ask before you contradict"What makes you say that?" beats "You've got that wrong."
Talk about the relationshipWhen the mood is off, stop pushing content: "I think I've hurt you. Is that right?"

Before you persuade

Is this image swap necessary?Every change of opinion costs the other person energy. Skip the ones that don't matter.
Reasons first, conclusion lastIf you know you'll hit a belief, explain your way towards the main point so it doesn't vanish in the fog.
Sell the sizzleFind their unmet need, make its fulfilment the goal, and describe it vividly. Both sides should win.
Agree to differOn matters of taste, faith or opinion, settle for a Zweinigung instead of a victory.
