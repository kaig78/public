Henri Nouwen'sLifesigns

Intimacy, fecundity and ecstasy in Christian perspective

A priest spending a year among people with mental disabilities in a French village asks why fear runs so much of our lives, and names three signs that we have moved out of its house and into the house of love.

The big idea

Fear is useful to somebody

Nouwen opens with an observation. The more people he knows, the more he sees how much fear decides for them. Most of us, he says, live in a house of fear most of the time, and treat it as the normal place to plan a life.

His sharpest question is who gains. Whoever can make us afraid can make us obey. Behind the fearful child, student, patient, employee or believer there is usually someone holding them in place: a father, a teacher, a boss, a bishop, a church, even a picture of God. Fear, in his reading, is one of the most effective tools of control.

The tool works through questions. The news and the culture hand us their "what if" and "how can I" questions, and once we adopt them, every answer opens a new worry.

“Fear engenders fear. Fear never gives birth to love.”
Nouwen, Introduction

So the questions we let steer us matter as much as the answers. In the gospels, he notes, Jesus almost never answered the questions he was given. He set them aside as worries about rank and control, and changed the question. Believers, meanwhile, hear talk of peace and reply "yes, but…", which shows which house they live in.

The way out is a voice that keeps sounding through scripture: do not be afraid. The house of love, he insists, is offered in the middle of an anxious world, not only after death.

The three words came from Jean Vanier, founder of l'Arche, a network of communities for people with mental disabilities. On a silent Pentecost retreat in Chicago he remarked, almost in passing, that every person is called to intimacy, fecundity and ecstasy. Nouwen later found all three in Jesus' farewell address. He wrote the book during a year at Trosly-Breuil, France, from August 1985.

The shape of the argument

Not a happy medium

Each part makes the same three moves. Fear produces two opposite counterfeits. Love is not a compromise between them but a different level altogether. And every personal sign has a worldwide side.

Each lifesign and its two fearful counterfeits

Drawn for this page from the book's structure. The grey line is where fear lives; both of its ends are fear's work. Nouwen explicitly rejects placing the lifesign at the midpoint: it sits on a different level, where the old choice stops being the guiding question.

This structure is the book's most useful idea for anyone reading it about fear. Fear does not only make us cold or only make us clingy. It does both, and the two look like opposites while serving one purpose: staying safe and in control.

The obvious fix is balance. Nouwen grants that this is sensible, then argues it leaves love on fear's ground, renegotiated forever. The real alternative comes as a gift, and from there the old question of how close, how much or how fixed stops running the show.

Part one · John 15:4

Intimacy

The first sign is home: a place where we can drop our defences. Nouwen thinks no word sums up the suffering of his century better than "homeless", whether people are driven from their countries or only living among strangers in small student rooms.

Even with so many broken homes, he notes, the word still carries warmth. Faith speaks of death as finally coming home, and he points to Rembrandt's painting of the prodigal son, the old father holding his worn-out child, as the picture of that longing.

01Intimacy and fear

Fear runs away or clings

Fear puts people at a "safe" distance or pulls them into a "safe" closeness. Neither makes a home.

  • The disciples did both within days: they fled at Gethsemane, then huddled in a closed room.
  • Nouwen admits he does the same with people he fears. He leaves the room or speaks in flat phrases, or he stays too long, laughs too loudly and agrees too quickly. Either way he feels unfree and resents their power over him.
  • Society copies the pattern. Prisons, mental hospitals and refugee camps are built out of sight; we choose safe topics and safe guests. Or we join the clique, sect or club that huddles against outsiders, which grows tempting when fear turns apocalyptic.
  • Both block what intimacy needs most: confessing our wounds and forgiving each other. Distance lets us ignore the other. Closeness gives us an excuse never to say we were hurt.
  • Vanier saw people with disabilities caught in exactly this: left in cold institutions or smothered by overprotection, rejected as aliens or held as property, never given room to grow at their own pace.
The story · How l'Arche began

Vanier spent ten years in the British and Canadian navies, studied philosophy and taught in Toronto, still unsure of his calling. In 1964, in the village of Trosly-Breuil, he invited Raphaël and Philippe, two men from an institution with no family, to live with him. He knew he could never send them back. He named the home after Noah's ark, a refuge for frightened people.

The cupped hand

Vanier's teaching gesture, as Nouwen describes it, drawn schematically. He holds out a hand as if it held a wounded bird and asks what happens if he opens it fully, then if he closes it. The cover photograph of this edition shows the same gesture.

“An intimate place is like my cupped hand, neither totally open nor totally closed.”
Jean Vanier, as quoted by Nouwen
02Intimacy and love

A home you are given, not one you negotiate

Intimacy is not the midpoint between too far and too close. It is a wider space, made by God, where that question stops steering.

  • Popular psychology treats relationships as a negotiation: closeness, but not too much; support, but with space. True enough, Nouwen says, but it leaves love under constant pressure from fear on both sides.
  • The "perfect love" that casts out fear is not compatibility or attraction but God's own love, which we are invited into. The home is made by God, not by human hands.
  • The tragedy is that we search for that home in competence, fame, success, pleasure or altered states, and never look in our own hearts. We become, in his phrase, “people who have an address but are never home”.
  • Spiritual discipline means coming home again and again to hear the "first love": you were loved, accepted and safe before anyone else could give or withhold it. Conversion is coming home. Prayer is how.
  • His model is the hesychast tradition of the Eastern church (hesychia means rest): descending with the mind into the heart, often with the Jesus Prayer. He names Basil Pennington's centering prayer and John Main's Maranatha prayer as variations.
  • At l'Arche, he learned, wounded people cannot be each other's whole home. Expecting it puts superhuman demands on others and ends in hurt. Lasting bonds rest on God's covenant; what people owe each other is a readiness to confess weakness and keep forgiving.
03Intimacy and solidarity

The innermost room is as wide as humanity

Intimacy with God does not shrink into privacy. Finding God's heart means finding everyone already gathered there.

  • We hear "intimate" as small and exclusive. Nouwen argues the reverse: through the incarnation all human flesh has been taken up, so a Christ without all people is not the true Christ. Lose this link and spirituality turns either private or narrowly activist.
  • The hardest idea, he says, is that God loves everyone with the same unlimited love and each person uniquely. In a competitive world we suspect our being chosen requires someone else's rejection. Deeper prayer frees us to admire other people's gifts without feeling smaller.
  • He separates action from activism. The activist anxiously tries to heal and restore a broken world. Christian action, in his view, expresses a unity that already exists and points to the one who heals.
The story · An American birthday at La Promesse

For the birthday of Brad, an American assistant, a foyer of fourteen residents and assistants from six countries served a "real American meal" on paper plates, with candles stuck in the hamburgers. Among those thirty people, Nouwen saw that here the poor were the hosts, and people of means had come from many countries to their table.

Part two · John 15:5

Fecundity

The second sign is fruitfulness, the sense that your life can bring forth life. Its absence is a quieter epidemic: days that are busy, dull and convinced they have nothing to give.

04Fecundity and fear

Sterility, or frantic productivity

Fear either shuts us down or drives us to manufacture. Both betray a doubt that our lives can bear fruit.

  • Sterility is withdrawal: the more threats we sense, the more we retreat, until we are out of touch with others. Its voice says leave me alone, I mind my own business, there is nothing I can do. People can feel it with children, a job and plenty of success.
  • In the rich world people feel like spare parts in a machine, bored while busy. Among the very poor, the distance from the powerful breeds fatalism. Both end in "I have nothing to offer".
  • Productivity is the opposite flight. Products are things we make, can repeat and can claim. Friends, deals and decisions can all become products, and we introduce people by their output, as if we are what we make.
  • He sees it in universities. "School" comes from the word for free time, yet students race deadlines and read great books just to tick a requirement. He sees it in a sex industry that sells potency as the way to intimacy.
  • He is not against productivity. But when our worth depends on it we become easy targets for rejection, and no amount of output gives a sense of being at home.
  • His own confession: living with people whose main achievements are dressing, eating and walking frustrates him. He believed in theory that being matters more than doing; at l'Arche he found out how far he was from living it.

Fruits are not products

Products
  • Made by us, and ours to claim
  • Repeatable and predictable
  • Need maintenance against breakdown
  • Measure our worth, so feed the fear
Fruits
  • Received as gifts
  • Neither predictable nor definable
  • Need soil, water, air and light: care, not control
  • Grow from intimate love, and call for thanks

Summary drawn for this page. Both sterility and productivity, he concludes, can betray the same thing: a lack of faith that our lives can bear fruit.

05Fecundity and love

Vulnerability, gratitude, care

Fruit appears where we stop trying to control life. Refusing new life and manufacturing it ourselves are both ways of playing God.

Three conditions of a fruitful life

1 · VulnerabilityArmed, defensive lives produce walls and missiles, not fruit. God's way is weakness: a dependent child, a powerless preacher, a man executed as useless.
2 · GratitudeThanks means admitting we depend on others, which a culture of self-reliance reads as weakness. Fruits of the Spirit can't be made or exported; they can only be received.
3 · CareA seed pulled up every day to check its progress never grows. Fruits ask for a safe environment, not management of every stage.

In John 6, Andrew sees five barley loaves and two fish and asks what that is among so many. Jesus gives thanks and shares, and twelve baskets of scraps remain. That shift from hoarding to sharing is, for Nouwen, the move from fear to love.

  • A student kept telling him she hated God. Arguments did nothing. Then, given time and trust, she told him her whole story, and later wrote that it began a new life.
  • At l'Arche he watches the residents give their hearts directly, sorrow included. They sense a house's mood before the assistants do, which makes them, in his word, barometers. Many assistants say they received more than they gave.
06Fecundity and mission

Learning to receive

As solidarity grows from intimacy, mission grows from fruitfulness, and real mission runs both ways.

  • On his first trips to Latin America, Nouwen admits, he was fighting his own sense of sterility and wanted to prove himself by fixing things. He warns that wanting to change things can itself turn violent.
  • A few weeks among the poor of Lima, Peru, changed his view. Struck by their joy, peace and gentleness despite great need, he came to see his calling as much receiver as giver.
  • He calls receiving the fruits of the poor one of the great missionary tasks. Wanting only to give, he argues, is a way of staying in control, and so of staying in the house of fear.
The story · Antonio's mother

Nouwen said Mass for Antonio, an eighteen-year-old killed in an accident, then mumbled condolences with his eyes on the floor. The mother made him stand straight and look at her. God had given them Antonio for a few years, she said, and they were grateful. She was ministering to him as much as he to her.

Part three · John 15:11

Ecstasy

The third sign is joy, and Nouwen wants the word back from the mystics. Its Greek root, ek-stasis, means standing outside a fixed, static place.

A university teacher of his lectured for a year on anxiety. Asked near the end to speak about joy, he tried for three sessions, ran out of ideas and went back to anxiety.

Joy, Nouwen suggests, is hard to describe the way health is: we detail aching legs and say nothing of healthy ones. Maybe it is the deeper, more normal state.

“Old joy is not joy!”
Nouwen, Ecstasy: Introduction

There can be old grief and old sorrow, he argues, but joy is always new, tied to movement, renewal and life.

07Ecstasy and fear

Routine, or rootlessness

Fear either freezes us in the familiar or scatters us aimlessly. Neither moves toward joy.

  • Routine soothes fear with sameness: "this is how we do things here". Children's bedtime rituals keep the dark at bay. Many routines are healthy; fear shows when any suggestion of change meets violent resistance.
  • A thirty-year-old man at l'Arche told every newcomer the same detailed story of his workshop job. An assistant explained it was how he coped with strangers. Nouwen recognised himself: friends also groan at his favourite success stories.
  • Two men rowing the Atlantic lost their bearings and kept sane with a rigid "mental playpen", even scheduling their conversation topics. Frightened people, he says, readily trade freedom for security.
  • Rootlessness is the opposite: emotions like a river leaving its bed. Lashing out, self-harm and aimless running can all answer a fear too big to face.
  • At La Forestière, a home for deeply disabled people, Edith often beats her head against hard objects. All he can do, he writes, is stay present without expecting change. Yet even there he finds a joy stronger than the agony: roots beyond rootlessness.
08Ecstasy and love

Celebration, including the sad days

Joy breaks in from outside the house of fear. It shows up as celebration, which lifts up loss as well as gladness.

  • Much joy is manufactured: happy hours, cruises, thrillers, a commercial Christmas. He likens it to the good last meal of a man on death row: tasty, but it doesn't keep him alive.
  • Celebration, for him, is not a party for special occasions but an awareness that every moment can be lifted up, including failure, farewell and death. Even death is celebrated, not because it is desirable but because life can be proclaimed in its face.
  • Every night a l'Arche household gathers around a candle and an icon. Sadness is named (Maurice's missed visit, Marie's ill mother, Pierre who won't leave his room) alongside thanks for good food or a new assistant. There are always flowers on the table.
  • Joyful people, he says, are not jokers or optimists. They see evil clearly but are not imprisoned by it. His example is Etty Hillesum, a Dutch Jewish woman writing under Nazi occupation, who let sorrows pass through her like a stream rather than clinging to them.
  • Joy is always shared. A daily Eucharist he began at two universities with one or two students drew very different people into a real community.
09Ecstasy and a new international order

Nations, too, can leave the house of fear

A joy that ignores the powers shaping whole countries is escapism. In the end, Nouwen argues, nations as well as people must choose between security and freedom.

  • In Guatemala he heard of a young Indian forced into the army and made to torture his own people. When he came home, his father let him greet his mother and sent him away: he carried death with him.
  • Ecstatic living means leaving the safe place. For a country that means a foreign policy that asks more than how our nation survives, and puts shared humanity ahead of any nationality.
  • Security obsession freezes, he says, whether it is a pistol by the bed or a Trident submarine in the port. Jesus sent his followers to all nations, and nations, not only individuals, are called to convert.
  • He asks for academies and ministries of peace, and above all a belief that rivalry between nations is as outdated as medieval rivalry between cities.
  • A new order, he admits, sounds like a dream. But Beethoven's Fifth now seems always to have existed, and someone had to compose it note by note. The world waits for new St Francises.

The hoarders' parable

Nouwen's own parable about national security, redrawn for this page; it contains no numbers. Each defensive step answers the fear the last one created. Ring = walls, dots = bombs on top of them.

The lamp · l'Arche

Nouwen does not claim l'Arche is a new world order, only a lamp on a stand. Vanier writes in a 1985 letter that the communities can't join every big political struggle because particular people, such as Innocente in Bouaké, need their constant presence.

Conclusion · Signs of Life

A love-filled home on a fear-filled sea

Nouwen ends without pretending the fear has gone. His last image is a boat in a storm.

As he finishes, the news is full of fear: the Achille Lauro hijacking, gloom about Reagan and Gorbachev halting the arms race. Homelessness, fruitlessness and static living are everywhere.

Still, the book offers a home now, not after catastrophe. Severely disabled people teach him this best: they don't brood over future disasters, and say in effect that it is good to be together now.

He refuses sentimentality. The people in the ark carry the world's fears; the boat rocks and nobody stays unafraid. But Jesus is in the boat, asleep, as in the storm story in Matthew. We keep pointing at the waves and saying "yes, but look", and he patiently keeps repeating where our home is.

The book closes with a long prayer by Etty Hillesum, written during the Nazi persecution of the Jews in Holland. She resolves not to burden today with worry about tomorrow. The jasmine behind her house has been wrecked by rain, yet she says it keeps blooming within her.

Reflective practices

Moving house, one habit at a time

Lifesigns is a meditation, not a manual, but each part points to concrete practices. These are the ones it stresses, grouped by theme, starting with fear itself.

Fear

Audit your questionsList the "what if" and "how can I" questions running your week. Ask whose questions they really are.
Ask who gainsWhen you feel afraid, ask who gets power over you through that fear.
Catch the "yes, but"Notice when you answer an invitation to love with a "realistic" objection. It shows which house you are in.

Intimacy

Cup your handWith people you care for, give neither cold distance nor smothering closeness: a space to grow.
Pray from the heartA short repeated prayer, such as the Jesus Prayer, to come home to the first love again and again.
Don't ask people to be your homeExpect confession and forgiveness from others, not freedom from every wound.

Fecundity

Fruit or product?Ask of what you value: did I make this, or was it given? Treat gifts as gifts.
Risk being knownConfess what you are most ashamed of to someone you trust. It may win a friend, not lose one.
Practise receivingLet poorer or weaker people give to you, not only receive from you.

Ecstasy

Spot fearful routinesWhich habits do you defend fiercely? Which story do you tell every stranger?
Celebrate every dayAn evening moment to name both sadness and thanks, with a candle or flowers.
Choose shared joyJoy is "ours": look for regular worship or meals that bind unlikely people.
