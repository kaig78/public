Praying Like Monks,Living Like Fools

An invitation to the wonder and mystery of prayer

A Brooklyn pastor, shaped by the 24-7 Prayer movement, on prayer as a wild relationship rather than a self-improvement tool: ten kinds of prayer, each with a story and a practice, held together by an ancient daily rhythm.

10 chapters10 practices1 daily rhythmwritten in lockdown, 2020
The big idea

Read the book for its practices

Staton writes from his kitchen table in a silent Brooklyn during the 2020 lockdown. The same crisis made comfortable agnostics start praying and made many churchgoers stop. Both, he notes, are biblical prayers: "help", and "you're no help at all".

Everybody prays: Catholics with the saints' words, Muslims on their rugs, Jews at the Western Wall, an atheist head-in-hands in a hospital waiting room. Gallup found more Americans pray in a given week than exercise, drive a car or go to work. Yet most Christians find prayer boring, obligatory or confusing.

Staton isn't interested in the dramatic beginning or end of answered-prayer stories, but in the "mystery in the middle": why ask if God is sovereign, why the long waits, why some are healed and others not. He writes to three groups: saints still knee-deep in the pool, sleepwalkers who find God admirable but boring, and sceptics who can only discover, not be taught.

Prayer, he says, is not a soft pillow or a workout for burning spiritual fat, but a wild adventure for people willing to drop their artificial identities. "Monks" for the ancient steady rhythms; "fools" because it will look foolish. And his advice: don't read the book for its content; read the content for its practices.

The middle voice of prayer

From chapter 7, borrowing Eugene Peterson's idea. Greek has a "middle voice": I take part, but the action didn't begin with me. Staton notes that in Eden this was the only voice; Eve's grab introduced the active voice and Adam's blame-shifting the passive.

Part one · Chapters 1–4

Start where you are

Ordinary places become holy ground: a field Moses walked past for forty years, Daniel's windowsill, a public middle-school parking lot.

1Holy Ground

Pray as you can, not as you can't

Prayer is learned by discovery, not information. The only non-negotiable is showing up honestly, and keeping on showing up.

The story · laps around the school

At thirteen, Staton wasn't sure he believed. A mentor dared him to walk around his school every day of the summer praying for his classmates. His newly-sixteen brother drove him; the student directory became his "book of common prayer". He fell in love with a God he wasn't sure was listening: he didn't just need God, he liked him. He started a 6:30 a.m. club, prepared by opening the Bible at random the night before ("a recipe for disaster, not revival"), and prayed name by name. The club outgrew the maths room. Twenty years later, as a pastor, he stood in that car park at sunrise with his shoes off on the icy pavement, crying.

  • Why we don't pray, beneath busyness and phones: fear of looking naive (prayer can't be mastered, only entered humbly); fear of silence (Dallas Willard: what if there's little to "just us and God"?); fear of mixed motives; fear of doing it wrong next to eloquent public pray-ers.
  • Why pray anyway: because we're overwhelmed, each drowning in "our thing"; because trust in God's character comes before faith in a particular answer; because complaints are welcome (David's psalms include rage and despair, and God still called him a man after his heart); and because the only way to get prayer wrong is to try to get it right.
  • Philippians 4 starts not with "do not be anxious" but with "the Lord is near". In the temple, Jesus calls the holiest place on earth "my Father's house". What would change if your prayers didn't begin "Dear God, sorry to bother you"?
  • Pete Greig: the most important discovery you'll ever make is that the Father likes you. Knowing about the moon isn't walking on it; reading the menu isn't eating.
  • Dom John Chapman: pray as you can, not as you can't. A minute instead of an hour. Walking instead of kneeling. Writing instead of speaking. Doubt instead of fake faith.
  • On New Year's Eve 2018 he drove his wife from dinner to circle the school at midnight, not to bend God's arm but to be with him: prayer is about presence before outcomes. "Do it again, Lord, this time in Brooklyn."
2Be Still and Know

See anything here more powerful than you?

Prayer starts with seeing, not speaking. Philip Yancey: prayer is seeing reality from God's point of view.

The story · Coney Island

CJ, a few months sober, told his AA sponsor he was in for everything except God. Days later the sponsor drove him to Coney Island on a cold November evening. They sat on the sand watching the sun set over the ocean. "See anything here more powerful than you are?" "Yeah." "Great. Start there."

  • Be still. Three inventions made stillness abnormal: the public clock (1370), turning time from a limit into a resource; the light bulb (1879), when Americans still slept about ten hours; the iPhone (2007). A 1967 Senate panel predicted a 22-hour work week by 1985; leisure has actually shrunk.
  • Dallas Willard's famous advice: ruthlessly eliminate hurry. A study of 20,000 Christians found busyness the top distraction from God, worst among doctors, lawyers and pastors ("not the pastor writing these words, of course"). Thomas Kelly found a year in Hawaii didn't fix his frantic inner life: we build environments that mirror our insides.
  • Since Eden, we've avoided the truth by never stopping. Ronald Rolheiser: not bad, just busy; not immoral, just distracted.
  • Know God. During the 2017 solar eclipse on Manhattan's 23rd Street, New Yorkers turned into children passing glasses around, while others shoved past, annoyed. If the Milky Way were the size of North America, our solar system would fit in a coffee cup. The great work of prayer is to let ourselves be loved by a God that big.
  • Know yourself. His favourite view of Manhattan was from Green-Wood Cemetery, over the tombstones. Remembering your mortality isn't depressing; it's rebellion against the first lie, "you will not die". Small, and crowned with glory.
  • Unhurried love. Jesus was intentional (withdrawing to pray) and interruptible (Bartimaeus, the bleeding woman). Russian hermits called themselves poustiniki, "being with everybody", and never latched their doors. Hurry kills love; silence breaks the attachments that make us use people.
3Our Father · Adoration

Remember who you're talking to

"Our Father in heaven, hallowed be your name" lays a threefold foundation: who God is, who you are, and who we are to each other.

The story · Wayne's question

Wayne worked for International Justice Mission, freeing people from forced labour. At a two-day IJM gathering in Washington that was really a prayer meeting, an auditorium begged God to free the enslaved in India. Years later, over tea in his Lower East Side flat, he said nothing had changed. Why wouldn't God answer that prayer? Staton doesn't answer quickly. He starts with who God is.

  • Who God is. The disciples gasped at "Father". The ancient question wasn't whether God exists, since there was a pillar of fire, but whether he's knowable. Staton's experience as a pastor: the number one obstacle to prayer is an inability to receive God's love.
  • In Genesis 3 the serpent never says "eat". He turns God's generous "any tree but one" into a stingy rule, and switches from God's personal name to a mere title, like calling your dad "Sir".
  • Who you are. In adoration we get our own name back: "saint", someone who has experienced God's goodness. His four-year-old Hank tells him daily, "You'll always love me no matter what, though, Dad." Our hearts never outgrow needing that.
  • Who we are to each other. "Our", not "my". We treat people as extras in a film where we're the lead.
  • Hallowed. Not for God's ego, but because the world isn't neutral: some other name (success, approval, comfort) is always being hallowed in us. The creatures in Revelation, covered in eyes, never get bored of God.
  • Defiant adoration. Beaten and chained, Paul and Silas sang hymns at midnight, really praying "Show yourself here". Then came the earthquake, and the jailer was baptised by morning.
4Search Me · Confession

Not a church without sin, but a church without secrets

Searching is God's part; naming is ours. Spiritual maturity means confessing more, not less.

  • Staton is unusually open. As a second grader, he retold a Goosebumps story on the school bus that left a kindergartner shaking: his first memory of guilt. In high school he punched a hole in his bedroom wall and covered it with a Bob Dylan poster. In college he used gossip for security. In his twenties he had a pornography addiction that followed him through Bible college, youth ministry and into the first year of marriage, when his wife checked his browser history. It has been over a decade since then.
  • Now it's impatience and anger. On the very day his church launched round-the-clock prayer, he yelled at his son three times and walked into the midnight prayer meeting after mishandling his wife's vulnerable moment. "I am on the confessor side, not the absolver side."
  • Confession is the slow song at a wedding party: Psalm 24 is a victory anthem, but it asks who has clean hands, and the subtext is "not me".
  • Sin is both the most controversial doctrine and the most universally agreed; Chesterton's answer to "what's wrong with the world?" was "I am". Biblically, sin is a diagnosis, not an accusation: meeting deep needs with our own resources. God's first question, "Where are you?", is an invitation to confess.
  • Jesus co-suffers. He's like a doctor treating lung cancer who had it himself, and you can't be healed while describing yourself as "generally sick".
  • Mary Karr, in her memoir, prayed for the first time kneeling at a toilet in a psychiatric hospital: "the throne" she had worshipped at as a drunk. Eugene Peterson: when God forgives, there is more of us, not less.
  • Ancient cities were built on the ruins of older ones; confession is the excavation.
The story · Herrnhut, 13 August 1727

Count Zinzendorf, in his twenties, turned his estate into a village for Moravian refugees. Five years in, they were disillusioned and bitter. During an ordinary service, as he preached on the cross, people began confessing and forgiving one another for hours, and stumbled out dizzy. Two weeks later they started a prayer meeting that lasted a hundred years and launched the modern missions movement. The eyewitnesses said the prayer was just the overflow of one night of confession.

  • C. S. Lewis' Eustace couldn't peel off his dragon skin himself. Aslan said: you will have to let me undress you. In Psalm 51 David uses four words for sin and nineteen for forgiveness.

Four layers to search (appendix 2)

The ancients' grid for confession. The deepest layer is about trust, where we look for significance, safety and wellbeing: the "fig leaves" of the false self.

Part two · Chapters 5–8

Ask, join, labour

Prayer changes the one who prays, but Staton insists it also releases power in the real world. God doesn't need intercessors; he chooses them.

5Intercession

History belongs to the intercessors

Walter Wink's line. Intercession is ordinary love plus sober humility: you love someone, their need exceeds you, and prayer fills the gap.

The story · Van

During a marriage course for pastors in Queens, his wife's phone kept buzzing: her brother Van, in his early thirties and one of Tyler's closest friends, had a torn aorta he'd mistaken for heartburn. Nashville's top surgeons said he wouldn't make it. After five hours the surgical team gave up and informally declared him dead. Then a nursing student, whose only job was handing over scissors, started praying, and the surgeon found the tear immediately. The non-Christian surgeon wept as he told the family it was miraculous.

  • Monica prayed for her womanising, sceptical son for years after a promising dream. The night he secretly sailed to Rome, she prayed he wouldn't go. In a Roman garden he heard a voice and opened the Bible: Augustine.
  • Most of us live "paralysed between wonder and mystery", praying vague prayers nobody could tell were answered. Thought experiment: if God answered every prayer you prayed last week, what would change?
  • The story of prayer in four eras. Creation: we were made to "rule" as God's image-bearers; Jonathan Sacks: we are all royalty. Fall: the authority was usurped. A friend with a brain injury after a motorbike crash could think "move your hand" while his hand stayed still: a communication breach between head and body. Promise: the government will be on his shoulders. Jesus: "all authority has been given to me", and "until now you have not asked in my name", meaning under his authority.
  • Karl Barth: to clasp the hands in prayer is the beginning of an uprising against the disorder of the world. Yet the church's worst-kept secret is that most Christians find prayer like eating celery.
The story · Diego

Diego, from the Avenue D projects, entered ninth grade at a third-grade reading level. After his father's arrest for dealing, he started drinking. Staton walked two miles along Avenue D every morning before sunrise, praying "your kingdom come" over Diego's building. One night at 1 a.m. he sat with Diego at a bus stop as Diego tried to run away to Puerto Rico. A year later Staton drove him 300 miles to university. Diego became student body president and the first in his family to graduate. Intercessors often become the answer to their own prayers.

  • In 2019 his Brooklyn church, meeting in a former synagogue, turned the small nursery into a prayer room six days a week, with butcher paper on the walls, an old kneeler, a basin and a cross. A sign read "Remove your shoes, holy ground", even though it still smelled of nappies. After a week, the walls were covered like a tattooed back, with names of friends along the baseboard. The month filled up.
6Daily Bread · Petition

World-hunger prayers and parking-space prayers

Jesus put "give us today our daily bread" right in the middle of a cosmic prayer. Gratitude is the reward for those who can stomach praying for small things.

  • In a busy car park after Christmas, his mother-in-law casually asked Jesus for a space. From the back seat, Staton silently ranted about SUVs, overstuffed closets and 690 million hungry people. A space opened; she said thank you, Jesus. He admits it's mostly hyperbole, and she was the one with the healthy soul.
  • Grace over takeaway fried rice; thanks at the top of a hike; seeing the cashier working a holiday double shift. Big-only prayers make for a cramped spiritual life.
  • Control. A good desire for a fruitful life, pursued with clenched jaws. Millennials, Staton notes, are the most justice-minded generation and also the most anxious. We trust God with our worldview but not with today. "Give us" weans us off independence.
  • "I want to hear you say it." Jesus asked a man paralysed for 38 years, "Do you want to get well?", like a paramedic asking a crash victim. God knows what we need and still wants us to ask, Spurgeon notes, even the Son (Psalm 2:8).
  • Why ask? For relationship: asking risks disappointment, and without it God is a vending machine. The friend at midnight is as ordinary as borrowing a neighbour's sourdough starter. And for empowerment: in Exodus 32 Moses reminded God of his own promises, and God "relented". Not Aristotle's "unmoved mover", but a moved mover. John Mark Comer: more friend than formula.
  • After a long day of racism, hunger and infidelity counselling, his son asked, "Can we have ice cream tonight?" Yes, buddy. God loves to give, even parking spaces.
7The Middle Voice

Join what God is already doing

Peterson: the task isn't to get God to do what I think needs doing, but to notice what he's doing and take part.

  • A friend came back from a Mumbai temple shaken: an elderly woman beside her wailed in prayer, as did everyone else, with more desperation for a god she didn't believe in than she'd ever prayed to Jesus.
  • Active prayer tries to crack God's code; the Pharisees thought one day of perfect law-keeping would bring the kingdom. Passive prayer empties itself. Biblical contemplation is about being filled.
  • Mary, a teenager, faced divorce, disgrace and possibly death under the law. She answered: "I am the Lord's servant. May your word to me be fulfilled." Thirty-three years later, in Gethsemane, Jesus prayed his mother's words.
  • God's activity is like the Mississippi's current: swim with it or against it, but you can't pretend it's a pond. Psalm 112: no fear of bad news. Staton admits he has the opposite.
  • Intimacy before fruitfulness. Mother Teresa, accepting the Nobel Prize, said her sisters were "contemplatives in the heart of the world"; the work grew out of prayer. Hebrew uses one word, tsedaqah, for personal righteousness and public justice. N. T. Wright: be in prayer, in the Spirit, at the place the world is in pain.
  • Activists who skip intimacy often end exhausted and cold toward the church. Prayer is the furnace that fuels mission.
8Laboring in Prayer

God dreams of a city reborn

There is a kind of prayer that gives birth to new life, and it's slow and unglamorous.

  • D. L. Moody, a shoe salesman with a fifth-grade education, carried a list of a hundred friends to pray for, as the oft-told story goes. Ninety-six came to faith in his lifetime and the last four at his funeral. Staton preached it, and nine months later asked who was still praying daily for a name. One hand went up, and it wasn't his. "That doesn't make us failures; it makes us normal."

Elijah in three scenes

1 · Church on fireHe rebuilds the broken altar and pours precious water on it three times in a drought, the costliest offering. Fire falls; people cry "The Lord is God!"
2 · Mountain of prayerHe crouches with his face between his knees: the posture of a woman in labour. Seven times the servant sees nothing, then a cloud like a hand.
3 · City rebornRain on a starving land. God doesn't dream of a church on fire; that's the vehicle. He dreams of a city reborn.

Birth runs through the Bible: the Spirit hovering over barren chaos, Sarah's womb, Mary overshadowed, Jesus describing his death as labour, and "rivers from within", a word that can mean womb.

  • His son Hank's birth took eighteen hours. Staton opened his footlong sub at hour eight ("What's that smell?"). His wife said "never again", and a week later wanted another baby.
  • When James and John wanted to call down fire, Jesus rebuked them. James later points us to Elijah's hidden rain prayer, not his public fire.
  • Spurgeon credited his "boiler room" of intercessors praying beneath the pulpit as he preached. One printed sermon converted a British prisoner in South America; another, printed like a newspaper advert, a shepherd in Ballarat.
Part three · Chapters 9–10

Keep knocking

Even Jesus' Gethsemane prayer wasn't answered as he asked. Unanswered prayer is part of the path, and so is a rhythm that holds you when feelings don't.

9Ask, Seek, Knock

When God is silent

"Either God is not powerful enough or God is not good enough. Either way, I'd have to diminish my view of God."

The story · Jenna and Helen

Jenna lived on prayer: groceries from a pantry, an anonymous rent cheque slipped under the door, and a microwave handed out of a car boot at midnight in a laundromat car park where she served addicts and sex workers. But for years she prayed for a baby and heard silence. Her sister-in-law Helen in Ireland, in her twenties, had cervical cancer and little chance of carrying a child. Against the odds, baby Henry was born, and Jenna too became pregnant after IVF. Months later, a massive inoperable tumour was found, hidden behind where Henry had been. Seven months of treatment failed. At Helen's bedside, Jenna heard her breathing change "like a key change" and said: it's okay, you can go. Helen died less than a week before Henry's first birthday. Back in Brooklyn, Jenna wanted to walk away from God. Two things kept her: too much good had been built on Jesus to deny, and she was too angry to leave the room.

  • Staton has seen a man left for dead wake up after prayer, and has anointed a young man in a coma who died while his family waited outside. Sometimes, he admits, silence would be easier if power were never on the table. But God isn't an operating system.
  • Ask, seek, knock trace a journey: need brings us (ask); we find the Giver more than the gifts (seek); and we end at his table (knock). Rublev's icon shows Father, Son and Spirit at a table with room to pull up a chair. The Greek tense means "keep on asking".
  • The persistent widow. Widows ranked lowest, and women couldn't testify in court, yet she gets justice, "and quickly". The unjust judge is a contrast to God, not a picture of him.
  • God collects prayers and tears. Revelation shows golden bowls of incense, every prayer kept like a grandmother's scrapbook of finger paintings, later hurled to earth with thunder. Psalm 56: tears kept in a bottle. Not one of them will be wasted.
  • Her grief counsellor asked: what reason could God give that would make Helen's death okay? None. So Jenna chose, not a belief that God willed the cancer, but trust: that God is good, present in suffering, and will make all things new. Her faith moved from the resurrection-power Jesus to the man of sorrows.
  • In Acts 12, Peter is freed during an all-night prayer meeting just after James was executed. Why? "I don't know." The church kept praying anyway.

Persistent prayer in three movements

1 · Say it like you mean itStart with the disappointment, not with grit. Forget your manners.
2 · Listen for the questionUnderneath lies a question about God's character: is he loving, listening, able to heal even this?
3 · Ask him to meet you thereKeep inviting him into that question, as miracle-worker or as companion in the dark.
10Rebellious Fidelity

Pray with the heart of a lover and the discipline of a monk

The woman caught in adultery lived her moment of mercy. The real fight came on all the ordinary days after. Fidelity, frankly, is boring.

  • Spiritual boredom often means maturing, not lapsing. Love is easy at the honeymoon and at the end, as in the last scene of The Notebook; the middle years are where it's won or lost.
  • At seventeen, Staton often stopped at a waterfront park on the way home for agenda-free walks with God. He suspects God preferred those to the "mission" prayers. We waste time with those we love.
  • The Lord's Prayer adapts the Jewish Kaddish, "basically plagiarism in college", but made personal. Bonhoeffer to a young couple: don't let love sustain your marriage; let your marriage sustain your love.
  • Pray like a monk. Daniel kept praying three times a day at his window and went to the lions for it. Jesus prayed in the temple rhythm. Acts shows Pentecost at morning prayer, the first healing on the way to the 3 p.m. prayer, and Peter's vision at noon prayer. The Didache sets prayer three times a day. When the church allied with imperial power, it lost its taste for prayer, and the desert monks went out to recover the early church's way.
  • What anchors your day: inbox, notifications, the weekend? Does it love you or try to control you? Sell you something? Leave you alive or exhausted?
  • Jazz. Improvising needs the sheet music first. In Gethsemane, Jesus fell back on the words of the prayer he'd taught; under pressure, we fall to the level of our training.
  • Herrnhut: 48 refugees in 32 homes, praying in shifts for a hundred years. Zinzendorf: "I have one passion: it is He, only He." Radicals have deep roots, from the Latin radix.
EEpilogue

Raise up David's tent

On a Monday night in February, Staton sat crying over a Bible in a sticky Irish pub, sipping seltzer to 1999 alt-rock, reading Amos' promise to rebuild "David's fallen shelter".

  • After seven years of waiting, David entered Jerusalem not in royal robes but dancing in a priest's linen undergarment, with the ark, not a throne, on the float. His own song asks "Who is the King of glory?", and the answer isn't David.
  • His first act as king: a simple tent for the ark in the city centre, staffed by 288 singers and prophets for round-the-clock prayer, open to everyone, men and women, slave and free, even foreigners, while enemies surrounded the kingdom. His reign became Israel's high point. "The presence of God was David's political strategy."
  • The church's "underground atheism" is that we believe in productivity, programmes and worship albums more than prayer. Staton's dream: houses of prayer at the heart of cities. Less gritted teeth, more a king dancing in his underwear, and Jesus grinning on a donkey half his size.
AAppendices

Jesus is praying for you now

Justification is past, glory is future, and intercession is Christ's present work, pulling the story from both ends.

  • "It is finished", yet nothing seemed to change: the temple curtain was sewn back up, and the day he healed one blind man ten more were born blind. That gap is where Christ's present prayer begins.
  • When Staton pitched Little League at twelve, his older brother Josh, who had just been overtaken by his little brother at baseball, came to every game and hung Sharpie "K" napkins on the fence for every strikeout. That's intercession: a big brother screaming support from the stands.
  • Hebrew yada means knowing by relationship, even intimacy, not facts. Staton cites Brennan Manning's cabin retreat: eighteen years preaching God's love without ever feeling it.
  • On an abbey retreat, monks from 25 to over 90 were up before him. Many monks picture the face of Jesus as they pray.
  • Psalm 22 begins "why have you forsaken me?" but ends in praise, and Jesus prayed it in our place. Staton's frequent prayer: Jesus, if you walked into the room right now, what would you want to say to me?
Practice

A daily prayer rhythm

The book's closing practice, with the other chapter practices sorted around it. Staton made a companion app with 24-7 Prayer called Inner Room.

Morning

The Lord's PrayerNot recited like a script but as a theme, line by line, in your own words. At thirteen, he laid his Bible open at Mark 1:35 on his alarm clock.
Be still firstSame time, same place, feet on the floor, palms up, three breaths, a breath prayer ("Here I am, Lord"), a timer: 2 minutes, adding 2 a month up to 10.

Midday

Pray for the lostStep out for a minute (a walk, a quiet desk, "a holy stall in the restroom") and pray for your names.
Ask small and specificThe meeting, the email, the rent: specific enough to be disappointed or surprised.

Evening

DayenuThe Passover song "it would have been enough": lunch would have been enough, but you gave choice, flavour and a colleague to share it with. Thank you, God, for overdoing it.
ExamenReplay the day with God: where was he nearest, where did he feel far?

Any time

Search meAsk God to reveal you to yourself, and say it out loud, ideally to a trusted friend too.
Release, then askOpen your hands, turn them over to let go, turn them up to receive.
Mary's prayer"I am the Lord's servant. May your word to me be fulfilled." Then say yes: go, give, forgive, rest, listen, love.
