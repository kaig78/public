The big idea

Read the book for its practices

Staton writes from a silent Brooklyn during the 2020 lockdown. The same crisis made some agnostics start praying and made many churchgoers stop. Both, he notes, are prayers: "help", and "you're no help at all".

Everyone prays: Catholics with the saints' words, Muslims on their rugs, Jews at the Western Wall, an atheist in a hospital waiting room. Yet many Western Christians barely do. Staton names reasons: fear of looking naive in a clever city, and the sense that nothing happens.

His answer isn't a technique. Prayer, he insists, is not a workout for burning spiritual fat. It's an adventure for people willing to drop their artificial identities and see beauty in mystery. "Monks" because of the ancient, steady rhythms. "Fools" because praying this way will look foolish to the world.

The book follows the shape of the Lord's Prayer, roughly: posture, adoration, confession, intercession, petition. Then it widens to joining God's work, praying for friends, and staying faithful when prayers go unanswered. Each chapter ends with a practice. Staton's advice: don't read it for the content; read the content for the practices.

The middle voice of prayer

From chapter 7, building on Eugene Peterson. Ancient Greek has a "middle voice": I'm an active participant, but the action didn't start with me. Christian prayer, Staton argues, lives there, between pleading and emptying.

Part one · Chapters 1–4

Start where you are

Ordinary places become holy ground: a bush in a field Moses walked past for forty years, Daniel's windowsill, a middle-school parking lot.

1Holy Ground

Pray as you can, not as you can't

You don't need a method to begin. You need to begin, and God meets you there.

The story · a summer around the school

At thirteen, Staton wasn't sure he believed. A mentor dared him: what would God do in your friends' lives if you walked around your school every day this summer praying for them? His older brother, newly sixteen, drove him there daily. With the student directory as his "prayer book", he wore a path in the grass. He fell in love with a God he wasn't sure was listening, then asked the principal to start a Jesus club that met at 6:30 a.m. Two decades later he sat in that parking lot, crying with gratitude.

  • The chapter's motto comes from the monk John Chapman: pray as you can, and don't try to pray as you can't.
  • Can't pray for an hour? Pray for a minute. Zone out at home? Pray while walking or running errands. Can't speak? Write it down. Can't pray with hope? Tell God about your doubt; he isn't bothered.
2Be Still and Know

Give God the first word

Prayer isn't only something happening inside you. It's meeting someone bigger than you, and that starts with being quiet.

The story · Coney Island

CJ, a few months sober in AA, told his sponsor he was in for everything except God. Days later the sponsor drove him to Coney Island on a cold November evening. They sat on the sand and watched the sun set over the ocean in silence. Then the sponsor asked whether CJ saw anything more powerful than himself. CJ said yes. That was step two.

  • Many people today are fine with meditation, mindfulness or yoga, prayer as a "centred state", but find a real conversation with God absurd.
  • Practice: sit in silence for a few minutes, and grow it slowly over months to ten minutes. Don't judge whether it's "working". Offer silence as a gift to God, not a way to get something from him. One day it becomes a lifeline.
3Our Father · Adoration

Intimate and majestic at once

"Our Father" says God is near. "Hallowed be your name" says he is holy and other. Our age loves the first and is embarrassed by the second.

The story · Wayne's question

Wayne worked for International Justice Mission, freeing people from forced labour. At a two-day IJM prayer gathering in Washington, an auditorium begged God to free the captives in India. Years later, over tea, he admitted nothing seemed to change: why wouldn't God answer that prayer? Staton doesn't hurry to answer. He starts with who God is, because adoration is the foundation for every other kind of prayer.

  • "Hallowed" means set apart, honoured. The ancients found "Father" shocking and holiness comforting; we're the other way round.
  • Practice: the prayer of examen in the evening. Replay the day with God like a film and thank him for each good thing: the first coffee, a laugh with your daughter. Then ask where his presence felt nearest and most distant. End with a short prayer for tomorrow.
4Search Me · Confession

Searching is God's part; naming is ours

Without confession, most people die without ever living as their true selves.

  • Staton opens with a childhood memory: as a second grader, proud of finishing his first chapter book, he retold a Goosebumps story on the bus and terrified a kindergartner, whose mother phoned his mother before he got home.
  • Confession isn't self-flagellation. It's letting God reveal you to yourself, so the false self loses its grip.
  • Practice: still yourself, wait, pray Psalm 139's "Search me and know me", notice what surfaces, and name it.

Four layers to search (appendix)

The ancients' grid for confession. The deepest layer is about trust: where we look for significance, safety and wellbeing. Staton calls these the "fig leaves" of the false self.

Part two · Chapters 5–8

Ask, specifically

Prayer changes the one praying. But Staton insists it also releases power to change things in the real world, and Jesus told us to ask, about big things and small.

5–6Intercession · Petition

World-hunger prayers and parking-space prayers

Intercession asks for God's kingdom to come where it's missing. Petition brings our own daily bread. Jesus wants both.

The story · Van

During a church marriage course in Queens, Staton's wife Kirsten got a call: her brother Van, one of Tyler's closest friends, had a torn aorta, and Nashville's top surgeons said he wouldn't make it. They flew in to say goodbye before a last-chance surgery more likely to kill him than save him. People prayed. Van survived, and the non-Christian doctor himself called it a miracle, close to tears.

  • Staton also tells of Monica, the mother of Augustine, who prayed for her wayward son for years.
  • The parking space. In a busy mall car park after Christmas, his mother-in-law casually asked Jesus for a spot. In the back seat, Staton silently ranted about SUVs, overstuffed closets and world hunger. A spot opened; she said thank you, Jesus. He admits the rant was mostly hyperbole, and that he was the one who had it wrong.
  • The Lord's Prayer puts "give us today our daily bread" right between cosmic lines. God treasures our small requests too.
  • Practice: be brief and specific. Don't cover for God with vague wording. Ask for the meeting, the email, the rent cheque, specifically enough that he can disappoint or surprise you.
7The Middle Voice

Join what God is already doing

Following Peterson: the task isn't to get God to do what I think needs doing, but to notice what he's doing and take part.

  • A friend, Alina, came back from a Mumbai temple shaken. An elderly woman next to her wailed in prayer, as did everyone around her, with more passion for a god Alina didn't believe in than she'd ever prayed to Jesus.
  • Active, pleading prayer and passive, emptying meditation are both common. Christian prayer adds a third way: consent, see the diagram above.
  • Practice: where you see God at work in you, thank him and ask him to finish it. Where you sense his invitation, say yes.
8Laboring in Prayer

Pray by name, for a long time

Elijah prayed seven times with his face between his knees before a cloud the size of a hand appeared.

  • D. L. Moody, a shoe salesman with a fifth-grade education, became one of the 19th century's best-known evangelists. His only strategy, according to a much-told story, was a list of a hundred friends he prayed for daily.
  • "Lost" is a word of compassion: someone looking for home without knowing the way.
  • Practice: write down a few names. Put them in your pocket, your phone or on the mirror. Pray specifically enough to know if God answers, and regularly enough that it takes endurance.
Part three · Chapters 9–10

Keep knocking

Even Jesus' prayer in Gethsemane wasn't answered the way he asked. Unanswered prayer is part of the path, and so is a rhythm that holds you when feelings don't.

9Ask, Seek, Knock

When God is silent

Behind every disappointment with prayer sits a question about God's character. Find it, and bring it to him.

The story · Jenna

Jenna prayed for groceries and got them, prayed for rent and it came. But for years she and her husband couldn't have children. In a small Brooklyn office she told Staton: either God isn't powerful enough or he isn't good enough; either way, she felt forced to shrink her view of God.

Persistent prayer in three movements

1 · Say it like you mean itStart with the disappointment, not with grit. Tell God your pain plainly, without manners. He collects our tears.
2 · Find the questionListen until you hear the question underneath: Is he loving? Listening? Able to heal even this?
3 · Invite him into itHold that question before God and keep inviting him. He sometimes opens blind eyes, and sometimes stumbles through the dark with us.

Modelled on the persistent widow in Luke 18. The aim is to ask again, not from gritted teeth or duty, but from faith that has been healed. Staton notes that Jesus, who did every kind of miracle, also lived through God's silence.

10Rebellious Fidelity

Pray without ceasing, by praying at set times

A daily prayer rhythm is an old well the church has filled in. Staton wants to dig it out again.

  • The chapter starts with the woman caught in adultery. Her place of greatest shame became the story of mercy she'd tell forever. God doesn't edit our stories; he repurposes them.
  • Daniel was thrown to the lions for kneeling at his window three times a day. The psalmist calls out evening, morning and noon. Jesus withdrew to pray regularly.
  • Epilogue. Crying over a Bible in a sticky Irish pub on a Monday night, Staton reads Amos' promise to rebuild David's fallen tent: the place where David set up the ark and worship went on day and night. He longs for churches to become houses of prayer again, marked less by intensity than by joy.
Practice

A daily prayer rhythm

The book's closing practice, with the other chapter practices sorted around it. Staton made an app for it with 24-7 Prayer called Inner Room.

Morning

The Lord's PrayerStart the day with God, slowly, line by line. It's about love, not discipline or personality.
Silence firstA few minutes of stillness before you speak. Give God the first word.

Midday

Pray for the lostA short pause for the names on your list.
Ask smallBring the meeting, the email, the decision, specifically.

Evening

GratitudeOn the commute or at the table, list what you're grateful for today instead of carrying home the leftovers.
ExamenReplay the day with God: where was he near, where did he feel far?

Any time

Pray as you canA minute counts. Walking counts. Writing counts.
Search meAsk God to show you yourself; name what comes up.
Say yesWhere you sense his invitation, consent: go, give, forgive, rest.
