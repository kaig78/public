Prologue · Terrified

Either AI is almost here, or it has barely started

In May 2014 Mitchell went along with her old doctoral adviser, Douglas Hofstadter, to a meeting with about twenty Google AI researchers. Many of them had joined the field after reading his book Gödel, Escher, Bach. Hofstadter told them he was terrified, and not of killer robots.

His fear was that the things he valued most in people would turn out to be cheap to copy. Deep Blue had already broken his prediction about chess. Then David Cope's program EMI wrote a mazurka in Chopin's style, and at the Eastman School of Music, faculty listening to it next to a little-known Chopin piece picked EMI's as the real one.

The engineers didn't share his alarm; several expected human-level AI within thirty years. Mitchell thought it more than a century away, and the gap sent her to check. This book is the result: how today's systems work, what they achieve, and how far there is to go.

The running theme

Marvin Minsky's paradox: things a four-year-old does without thinking, like seeing, talking and common sense, are harder for machines than chess or calculus.

Part I · Chapters 1–3

Background

Two rival ideas of intelligence, hand-written rules or learning from examples, and a field that has swung between hype and disappointment from the start.

01Roots

Rules versus neurons

From the beginning AI split into two camps: programs that manipulate human-readable symbols, and brain-inspired networks that learn numbers from data.

  • Founding optimism. McCarthy, Minsky, Claude Shannon and Nathaniel Rochester organised the 1956 Dartmouth workshop. Herbert Simon soon predicted that within twenty years machines could do any work a person can. Minsky called "intelligence" a suitcase word, packed with too many meanings.
  • Symbolic AI. Newell and Simon's General Problem Solver worked through puzzles like Missionaries and Cannibals using explicit states, operators and rules. The symbols mean nothing to the machine. Expert systems built this way ruled the field for three decades.
  • Subsymbolic AI. Frank Rosenblatt's perceptron (late 1950s) adds up weighted inputs and fires if the total passes a threshold. It learns by nudging its weights after each mistake on labelled examples, which is supervised learning. A 1958 press event predicted it would one day walk, talk and be conscious.
  • The first winter. Minsky and Papert's 1969 book Perceptrons proved the limits of single-layer networks and doubted multilayer ones. Funding dried up, and Rosenblatt died in 1971. Symbolic AI then overpromised too, and British and US reviews cut its money. Mitchell describes a recurring cycle of spring and winter, every five to ten years.

A perceptron is a weighted vote against a threshold

Mitchell's example is an "8 detector". Weights start random, and each error moves them slightly, most for the inputs that contributed most.

02Neural networks

Hidden layers come back

Adding hidden units and a way to share out the blame for errors turned the "sterile" multilayer network into the basis of modern AI.

  • Hidden units. A multilayer network places units between input and output. Each produces a graded activation between 0 and 1 instead of firing or not. A network with more than one hidden layer is "deep", which describes its depth, not the depth of what it learns.
  • Back-propagation. Developed by several groups around the late 1970s and early 1980s, it sends the error at the output backwards through the network to work out how much each weight should change.
  • Each side's weakness. Expert systems like MYCIN (about 600 rules) explain themselves but break outside their domain, because experts rely on unwritten common sense. Networks are the reverse. Philosopher Andy Clark described them as "bad at logic, good at Frisbee."

Mitchell's own test: handwritten digits

Perceptron, 324 inputs, 10 outputs~80%
Two-layer network with 50 hidden units94%

Both were trained on 60,000 examples and tested on 10,000 new ones. Mitchell admits she can't say what the network's roughly 16,700 weights learned that the perceptron missed.

03AI spring

Spring fever, and the arguments over "thinking"

Every AI system so far is narrow. Claims about general intelligence rest on contested tests and on extrapolating exponential curves.

  • The new spring. Google Translate, Siri and Alexa, Watson winning Jeopardy! in 2011, AlphaGo winning four of five games against a top Go player in 2016. For nearly every company, AI now meant deep learning. And once something works, McCarthy noted, people stop calling it AI.
  • Narrow and general. AlphaGo can't play checkers. Google Translate can't tell whether a review it translates liked the film. John Searle (1980) argued that a computer only simulates a mind. Mitchell's mother puts the same intuition more simply.
  • The Turing test. Turing predicted that by about 2000 a program would fool an average judge 30% of the time in five minutes. In 2014 the chatbot Eugene Goostman, posing as a thirteen-year-old Ukrainian boy, fooled 10 of 30 judges at the Royal Society. Experts dismissed it as a trick based on dodging questions.
  • Kurzweil's Singularity. Building on I. J. Good's "intelligence explosion", Ray Kurzweil forecasts a Turing-test pass by 2029 and the Singularity in 2045, based on exponential trends like Moore's law. Critics reply that hardware has grown exponentially but software has not.
  • The Long Bet. Mitchell Kapor's $20,000 wager says no machine will pass a strict version (three judges, two-hour interviews) by 2029, because most human knowledge is tacit and bodily.

The rice-on-the-chessboard fable

A sage asks for 2 grains on the first square, doubling each time. Kurzweil's supporters say AI sits on the flat part of such a curve. Mitchell asks whether it is on one at all.

Part II · Chapters 4–7

Looking & seeing

Vision is the clearest case of easy things being hard. Deep learning made a huge leap here, and its blind spots are easiest to see here too.

04Who, what, when

A network built like the visual cortex

A photo of a soldier greeted by her dog tells you a whole story at a glance. A computer has to start from millions of pixels.

  • Harder than it looked. A 1966 MIT summer project aimed to have a computer describe what a camera saw. Object recognition alone resisted decades of hand-crafted features.
  • From cat brains to ConvNets. Hubel and Wiesel found layers in the visual cortex whose neurons respond to edges, then shapes, then objects. Fukushima's neocognitron (1970s) copied the idea, and Yann LeCun's convolutional networks (1980s) added back-propagation.
  • Activation maps. Each layer holds several maps, one per feature. A unit computes a convolution of its small receptive field, and the same weights slide across the whole image. Higher layers combine lower maps into corners, then parts, then objects. A small ordinary network then outputs a confidence for each category.
  • Learned, not built in. Real ConvNets learn every weight from labelled photos and still end up with a brain-like hierarchy. One difference: the cortex has about ten times as many feedback connections as feed-forward ones; ConvNets have none.

05ImageNet

Big data meets an old idea

ConvNets barely changed in twenty years. Millions of labelled photos and fast GPUs made them win.

  • Building the dataset. Fei-Fei Li organised web images by WordNet nouns. Having students check them would have taken about ninety years. With Amazon Mechanical Turk workers, more than three million images were labelled in two years.
  • The 2012 shock. The challenge had 1,000 categories and 1.2 million training images, scored by whether the right label is among the top five guesses. AlexNet, from Krizhevsky, Sutskever and Hinton, had eight layers and about sixty million weights and jumped to 85%. Google soon hired the team, and Facebook hired LeCun.
  • Data snooping. In 2015 a Baidu team used at least 30 accounts to submit at least 200 times, against a limit of two a week, tuning to the hidden test set. Baidu was disqualified.
  • "Better than humans"? The 5% human error rate came from one person, Andrej Karpathy, who trained on 500 images and got about 75 of 1,500 wrong. Machines miss small, filtered or drawn objects that people don't, and a top-five hit counts even if four wrong guesses rank above it.

06Machines that learn

What did my network actually learn?

Deep networks learn whatever statistical cue gets the right answer, and that is often not the concept we meant to teach.

  • Not "on their own". People collect and label the data and tune the hyperparameters, a craft Demis Hassabis said only a few hundred people did really well.
  • The long tail. Rare situations are individually unlikely but collectively certain. Tesla's Autopilot reportedly confused salt lines with lane markings, and a Google car hit a bus while steering around sandbags. LeCun likens unsupervised learning, which nobody yet does well, to the dark matter of AI.
  • Shortcuts. A student of Mitchell's, Will Landecker, found his animal detector partly relied on blurry backgrounds. In 2015 Google Photos labelled two African Americans as "gorillas". One face dataset was 77.5% male and 83.5% white.
  • Adversarial examples. Tiny pixel changes turned AlexNet's school bus into an ostrich, and evolved noise got over 99% confidence. Patterned glasses and stickers on stop signs work too. There's no general defence yet.
  • Clever Hans. Like the horse that "counted" by reading its questioner's cues, a network may answer from surface cues. The root problem, Mitchell argues, is lack of understanding.

07Trust & ethics

The great AI trade-off

AI already brings real benefits. The open question is how much autonomy to give systems that are unpredictable, biased, hackable and opaque.

  • Divided experts. In a 2018 Pew canvass of nearly 1,000 technology experts, 63% expected AI to leave most people better off by 2030 and 37% did not. Andrew Ng calls AI the new electricity, but electricity was well understood before it was everywhere.
  • Face recognition. It can find missing children, and it can track people and enforce bias. Microsoft's Brad Smith asked Congress to regulate it, and Google held back from offering general-purpose face recognition.
  • Regulation. Mitchell favours a model like biomedicine's, with shared oversight, and thinks superintelligence gets too much attention and unreliability too little.
  • Moral machines. Asimov's stories show how rules of robotics backfire. In surveys about self-driving cars, 76% said sacrificing one passenger to save ten pedestrians was the moral choice, but most wouldn't buy that car. Mitchell argues that moral reasoning requires common sense first.

Part III · Chapters 8–10

Learning to play

Reinforcement learning needs only occasional rewards. In games it produced superhuman players. Mitchell asks what they actually learned.

08Rewards

Training Rosie the robo-dog

Reward sometimes, never explain. The agent learns how much future reward each action in each state is worth.

  • Operant conditioning. Animal trainers reward what they like and ignore the rest; journalist Amy Sutherland used it on her husband. Reinforcement learning does this for software.
  • The setup. Rosie is a hypothetical Aibo robot dog. Her state is how many steps she is from the ball, her actions are Forward, Backward and Kick, and she gets a reward of 10 only for kicking the ball.
  • The Q-table. At first Rosie acts at random. When a kick finally pays off, only the last state and action are credited. On later episodes, value spreads back one step at a time, discounted as it goes. In Mitchell's simulation, Rosie learned the task from any start within about 300 episodes.
  • The real world. Agents must balance exploring against exploiting. Real states are endless, so a network replaces the table, and training happens in simulations that transfer poorly to messy reality.

09Game on

From Breakout to AlphaGo

Deep Q-learning replaced the table with a ConvNet. Combined with tree search and self-play, it conquered Go.

  • Atari. DeepMind's Deep Q-Network took four screen frames as input and estimated a value for each joystick move. It learned by temporal differences, which Richard Sutton calls "learning a guess from a guess". It beat a professional tester on more than half of 49 games, scored over ten times the tester's average at Breakout, and Google bought DeepMind for £440 million.
  • Checkers and chess. Arthur Samuel, who coined "machine learning", wrote a 1950s checkers program that searched a few moves ahead, scored positions and improved by self-play. Deep Blue (1997) did the same with deeper search and special hardware, and almost no learning.
  • Why Go was different. Chess offers about 35 moves per position, Go about 250, and nobody had written a good evaluation function for Go. As late as 1997 one enthusiast said a Go win might take a hundred years.
  • Lee Sedol, 2016. Over 200 million people watched online. In game 2 AlphaGo made a move one commentator first took for a mistake and Fan Hui called beautiful. Lee won game 4 with a move AlphaGo seemed not to anticipate, but lost the match. AlphaGo Zero, using only the rules, later beat that version 100 games to 0.

Monte Carlo tree search, with a ConvNet's hunch

AlphaGo ran close to 2,000 roll-outs per turn and trained on about five million self-play games. Hunches guide the search; search results retrain the hunches.

10Beyond games

The ultimate idiot savant

Game-playing systems learn nothing they can take anywhere else, even to a slightly altered version of the same game.

  • No transfer. AlphaZero trains a separate network from scratch for each game. Humans transfer skills automatically, to the point that transfer is simply what we mean by learning.
  • Hidden guidance. DeepMind said AlphaGo Zero learned without human examples or guidance. Gary Marcus points out that its architecture, its tree search and its hyperparameters were all chosen by people.
  • Less hard than it looked? In 2018 Uber AI Labs found that simply trying networks with random weights matched or beat deep Q-learning on 5 of 13 Atari games. A genetic algorithm beat it on 7.
  • Brittle concepts. Moving Breakout's paddle a few pixels, or changing Pong's background colour, wrecked trained players. Marcus calls words like "wall" and "tunnel" overattributions. A kindergartner in the chess club learns to think. AlphaGo learns only Go.
  • Real tasks. A dishwasher-loading robot faces unclear states, hidden pots, countless actions and a curious dog. Karpathy noted that nearly every assumption AlphaGo relies on fails there.

AI meets language

A short restaurant story shows how much unstated knowledge reading needs. Deep learning handles the words impressively and the meaning hardly at all.

11Word vectors

Words as points in space

If a word's meaning is the company it keeps, meaning can be stored as coordinates. Bias gets stored the same way.

  • The restaurant test. A man orders a rare burger, gets it burnt, sarcastically says it's great and storms out without paying. Did he eat it? Answering takes knowledge of food, sarcasm, idioms and tipping that a four-year-old has and a machine doesn't.
  • Speech recognition. Deep networks gave it the biggest single jump in 20 years after 2012. Still, Mitchell's phone turns "mousse" into "moose". She guesses 90–95% of human accuracy in quiet rooms, and the last 10% may need understanding.
  • Recurrent networks. For sentiment, a network reads word by word, carrying its hidden state forward. Spotting single words fails, since "missing" can appear in praise.
  • word2vec. Google (2013) trained a network on billions of neighbouring word pairs from Google News, with a vocabulary of about 700,000 words, and kept 300 learned weights per word as its vector. France ends up near Spain and Belgium, and country-to-capital distances line up.

12Translation

Translation without a mental model

Neural translation is much better than what came before, but it is unreliable exactly where meaning depends on context.

  • Three eras. Hand-written rules came first, then statistical phrase tables in the 1990s, trained on Canadian parliamentary and UN texts. In 2016 Google switched to neural translation.
  • Encoder and decoder. One recurrent network turns a sentence into a set of activations, and a second produces the translation from them word by word. LSTM units help them hold on to earlier words. Training uses more than thirty million human-translated sentence pairs.
  • "Human parity"? The automatic score, BLEU, tends to overrate bad translations. Google's and Microsoft's human evaluations averaged ratings of isolated sentences taken from news and Wikipedia. Averages hide the terrible translations, and longer passages do worse.
  • Captions. Google's Show and Tell paired an ImageNet ConvNet with a decoder trained on about 80,000 image–caption pairs. Microsoft's CaptionBot described the soldier-and-dog photo as "a man holding a dog", which misses both the soldier and the whole point of the photo.

13Ask me anything

Answering is not understanding

Benchmarks for question answering keep being "beaten" by systems that match patterns. Tests built to need common sense show how much is missing.

  • Watson. In February 2011 it beat Ken Jennings and Brad Rutter at Jeopardy!. Some of its errors were very unhuman, like answering a "U.S. Cities" clue with Toronto. IBM later put the name on a range of unrelated services, and Mitchell suggests Watson may be AI's most notorious hype.
  • SQuAD. Stanford's 2016 reading test has more than 100,000 questions whose answers appear word for word in a Wikipedia paragraph. In 2018 systems from Microsoft and Alibaba beat the measured human score of 87%. Mitchell calls the task answer extraction, not comprehension.
  • Winograd schemas. A table won't fit through a doorway because it is too wide, or too narrow. Swap that one word and the pronoun points to the other object. The best system relied on phrase statistics, much like comparing counts of Google search results.
  • Fooling language systems. Adding an irrelevant sentence about a "Jeff Dean" made a SQuAD system give that name as the answer. A doctored cake photo was captioned as a dog and a cat with a frisbee. Audio can be altered so that people hear music while a speech recognizer hears a command.

Part V · Chapters 14–16

The barrier of meaning

Mitchell borrows a question from mathematician Gian-Carlo Rota: will AI ever break through the barrier of meaning? Her answer: not soon, because understanding rests on things nobody yet knows how to build.

14On understanding

What a driver knows at a glance

Understanding is core knowledge and mental simulation, built from abstraction and analogy.

  • Core knowledge. Infants know that hidden objects persist and that pushes cause motion. This intuitive physics comes with intuitive biology and psychology. Together they let a driver instantly read a woman on her phone crossing with a stroller and a dog.
  • Simulation. Mental models let us predict what happens next and imagine "what if I honk?". Lawrence Barsalou argues that understanding, even of a story you read, is a re-enactment of perception and action.
  • Metaphors we live by. Lakoff and Johnson show that abstract ideas borrow physical ones: we spend time, feel down, give someone a warm welcome. In one experiment, people holding hot coffee rated a stranger as warmer, although such results are still debated.
  • Abstraction and analogy. An imagined journal follows a child from recognising faces to arguing in court that a blogging platform is like a wall covered in graffiti. Hofstadter defines analogy as perceiving a common essence. Mitchell also speculates that consciousness may be models of our own models, which Hofstadter calls a strange loop.
Intuitive physicsObjectsThe man's hidden feet are still there. The stroller moves because she pushes it.
Intuitive biologyLiving thingsThe dog can move on its own, and it is sniffing something.

15Analogy

Teaching machines sameness

Attempts to hand-code common sense, or to get networks to abstract, have so far barely got started.

  • Cyc. Since 1984 Douglas Lenat's team has hand-written logical facts, such as that objects age a year every year. In 2015 it had 15 million and Lenat guessed that was about 5% of what's needed. Mitchell doubts it can work: much common sense is unconscious, so nobody can write it down.
  • Bongard problems. In 1967 Mikhail Bongard set 100 puzzles in which six boxes on each side share a concept you have to spot. People need only twelve examples. ConvNets trained on 20,000 examples per class of a simpler "same versus different" task scored only slightly above chance, while people scored nearly 100%.
  • Copycat. Mitchell's thesis program with Hofstadter makes letter-string analogies through conceptual slippage, where one concept gives way to a related one. Her current program, Situate, tries to recognise situations such as walking a dog.
  • Really far away. In a 2012 blog post Karpathy analysed a photo in which Obama secretly presses on a scale someone is standing on. Getting the joke needs physics, gaze, theory of mind, even social status. Mitchell finds the case that intelligence needs a body increasingly convincing.

Letter-string analogies: "do the same thing"

if abc → abd, then

Takeaways

The toolkit

Mitchell's guide comes down to a few habits for reading AI news, using AI tools, and thinking about your own mind.

Reading claims

Ask "on which benchmark?""Beats humans" usually means one dataset, a lenient metric, and sometimes one human.
Check the human baselineWho was tested, how, and on what? ImageNet's 5% came from one graduate student.
Watch the verbs"Reads", "understands", "learns on its own" often overstate what the system does.

Using systems

Plan for the long tailAverage performance says little about rare cases, which are where serious failures happen.
Assume it can be fooledSmall changes people can't see can flip a network's answer. Defences are still piecemeal.
Keep people involved where it mattersPartial autonomy fails when people stop paying attention, so design for how they'll actually behave.

Thinking about minds

Expect easy things to be hardWhat a four-year-old does without effort is usually the hardest part for machines.
Look for transferReal learning carries over to new situations. Checking whether a skill transfers is the best test of understanding.
Don't anthropomorphiseWe assume understanding on thin evidence, as the people who believed in Clever Hans did.
