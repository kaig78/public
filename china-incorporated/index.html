The politics of a world where China is number one

A former British diplomat who has watched China for thirty years argues that the West's stories about it, from imminent collapse to secret world domination, keep failing. He proposes a plainer one: two very different systems that have to share one world.

Note on terms & introduction

The good-versus-evil story about China has stopped working

The book opens in Wuhan at the end of 2019. Officials there, Brown says, were afraid of the anti-corruption campaigns Xi Jinping had run since late 2012 and kept bad news from Beijing until it couldn't be hidden. A Financial Times journalist called it China's Chernobyl moment. Within weeks the virus reached Europe and the US, and democracies imposed lockdowns as harsh as China's. By January 2022 nobody had come out of the pandemic looking competent.

What the pandemic did change was the tone. Washington talked of "the China virus", Chinese diplomats turned into "Wolf Warriors", and European leaders suddenly treated China as the problem of the age. When Brown wrote a short piece asking for less moralising, he got a day of online abuse. He notes that the US was once accused of losing China in the 1950s, and argues that people who now want to save China from its government risk repeating that.

His target is the binary story: an evil Party and a good, captive people. He calls it a fairy tale that has become dangerous. It hides how complex China is. It inflates China into the threat that overshadows all others. And it hands policymakers the wrong tools, such as treating Xi as a lone despot when he sits in a web of interests that Brown calls the "Leadership", with a capital L.

A note on terms: Brown uses "the Enlightenment West" for the US, Europe and their democratic allies, meaning a set of ideas about conscience and individual freedom as much as a place. "China" means the People's Republic under the Communist Party.

Chapters 1–3 · Seeing China as it is

Seeing

Before arguing about what China wants, Brown asks what is actually new. His answer is a strong China, a China with a navy, and a set of values that won't fit the Western template. The puzzle is that this power so rarely does what people expect it to.

01Three key things

Drop the story of a weak China

For two centuries outsiders have expected China to stagnate or collapse. Brown argues that the most reliable thing about such predictions is that they turn out wrong.

  • Where the "weak China" story comes from. John Barrow, a young member of Lord Macartney's 1793 embassy to the Qing court, described a stagnant system and his view stuck. Yet on Angus Maddison's estimates, Qing China may have been the world's largest economy until around 1820. Since then the West has kept predicting China's fall. Gordon Chang has done so since 2001.
  • A record of bad forecasts. Roger Irvine's 2016 study shows how wrong the big calls were. After Mao died in 1976, only one Beijing diplomat, Australia's Stephen Fitzgerald, saw an export-led opening coming. Brown admits that in the late 2000s he too expected China to move towards a multi-party system.
  • 1. Strong China. Xi speaks forcefully and China now builds its own institutions, such as the BRI, the AIIB and a BRICS bank in Shanghai. Brown is careful about cause, though. The West has weakened since 2008 at the same time, but mostly for its own reasons: the financial crisis, inequality, Brexit and Trump.
  • 2. China at sea. China fought or clashed with its neighbours in 1950–53, 1962, 1969 and 1979, when it was much weaker. Since Vietnam it has hardly used its army abroad. What is new is the navy, planned in the 1980s by Admiral Liu Huaqing. It has a base in Djibouti, opened in 2015, and still only one.
  • 3. Different values. Europe long insisted on one Truth. Brown argues Chinese tradition has mixed Buddhism, Confucianism and Daoism for centuries, and that today's slogans are hybrid in the same way but also exclusive: "with Chinese characteristics" means for China only. A creed that outsiders can't join is hard to export.

Universal versus hybrid-but-fenced

Brown's argument in a picture. A rival universal creed would mean a clash of civilisations. A hybrid creed that is for China only gives others little to convert to, which is why he doubts China wants to remake the world.

02The enigma of power

A power that haunts

China has every outward sign of great power. When Brown looks for a hidden master plan behind it, he finds mostly opportunism, clumsiness and limits.

  • The signs of power. China is the largest contributor to UN peacekeeping, holds a 3% stake in the IMF, runs eighty bilateral dialogues with the US and has a standing army of 1.3 million. In Beijing's 2015 parade, 84% of the equipment shown had never been seen in public. But the PLA hasn't fought a real war since 1979.
  • Different kinds of power. If power works the same for everyone, China must be plotting. Brown treats power as relational and shaped by culture. He borrows the philosopher François Jullien's line that Chinese power "haunts, but does not act".
  • Cyberspace suits it. Liu Xiaobo once remarked that God created the internet for China. Instead of liberating China, as the Clinton era hoped, the web gave the state face recognition and mass surveillance at home, plus a walled-off domestic internet. Meanwhile the open internet divided the West.
  • Confucius Institutes. There were 530 of them by 2019. Brown finds them a very mixed bunch and often inactive. At a 2014 conference in Portugal, Chinese officials were accused of tearing a Taiwanese sponsor's page out of the programme, which badly damaged their own cause. The bigger exposure is tuition fees: the University of Sydney went from 6,000 Chinese students in 2012 to about 25,000, who pay over a quarter of its revenue. Brown rejects the idea that these students are a spy network.
  • "China is buying us up." Brown points out that the Netherlands, Japan, Germany, the US and the UK invested more abroad for much of this period. He says the projects held up as proof of a grand plan, Hambantota in Sri Lanka, Piraeus in Greece and Bagamoyo in Tanzania, were none going well by late 2021.
  • Huawei. A 2017 law obliges organisations to help state intelligence work. With millions of lines of code, no one can rule out a backdoor, so Brown thinks Western caution is prudent. He also sees the case as a failure for Beijing: a state that couldn't loosen its grip lost markets that a more independent company might have kept.

03Values

Many beliefs, held lightly

The West has kept asking what Chinese people believe. Brown thinks the better question is how they believe: in several systems at once, each in its own sphere.

  • Europe as teacher. Europe's contact with China goes back some 700 years. China's patriotic education since the 1990s casts Britain, France and Germany as villains of the national humiliation story, and an EU that lectures on values while arguing in public strikes Beijing as all words. Brown reads the EU's 2006 strategy paper and Britain's 2009 framework as a stern teacher's report on a gifted but difficult pupil.
  • The retreat. The 2008 crash and the Arab Spring wiped out China's respect for Western competence. European officials came to Beijing looking for money. The EU's 2016 paper, published the day of the Brexit vote, put the EU's own interests first. By 2019 China was at once a partner, a competitor and a "systemic rival". China's own papers were self-interested throughout and became steadily firmer on Taiwan.
  • Marxism as a church. Brown compares Party belief to Catholicism. A theologian, a priest and someone who ticks a census box all claim the same faith with very different depth. For most business people, farmers and techies, the ideology is part of the background, something to work around.
  • A handful of sand. The sociologist Fei Xiaotong described Chinese society as a dense web of personal networks. Sun Yat-sen feared it would scatter like sand. Brown argues a common creed on the surface guards against that, and below it people believe many things at once.
  • Ricci's dialogue. The Jesuit Matteo Ricci mastered Chinese, wore a Confucian scholar's robes and died in Beijing in 1610. In The True Meaning of the Lord of Heaven, his Chinese debater gives way on every point. Brown sees that as intellectual annexation, the same assumption that only one set of values can be true.

The heights show the direction of the change and are not measured. Over the same period China's position hardly moved, so it was Europe that compromised.

Chapters 4–5 · Wants

Wants

Brown turns the usual question around. Before asking what China wants, ask what the West wants from China. Both lists turn out to contradict themselves, and in a similar way.

04What the world wants

Succeed, but not too much

The West wants a China that neither collapses nor wins. Brown asks whether any country could fill such a narrow slot.

  • Things it plainly wants. Its purchases show it: about USD600 billion in US–China trade predicted for 2021, about €600 billion with the EU, and China as the UK's sixth-largest export market and fourth-largest source of imports by 2020. In 2019 China exported USD2.641 trillion, a sixth of world trade. Walmart sourced more than a quarter of its products there. Companies still dream of the Chinese middle class.
  • The game and its meaning. China joined the WTO and accepted its rules. It never accepted the idea that playing capitalism well requires believing in liberal individualism. Brown's comparison is a team that wins the World Cup while caring only about the prize money.
  • Four presidents, one hope. Clinton said WTO entry would move China in the right direction. Bush told Tsinghua students he looked forward to national elections. Obama pressed on rights. Pence then said China had made a U-turn. Brown concludes that economic engagement was simply the wrong tool for changing a country's politics.
  • Things it doesn't want. A huge military, island-building, debt-trap deals, missiles aimed at Taiwan. China has acquired technology through espionage, Russian arms and heavy investment at home. It tested an anti-satellite missile in 2007, tested a new stealth fighter during the US defence secretary's visit in 2011, and in 2021 tested a hypersonic missile that circled the globe.
  • Nor collapse. Russia's 1990s collapse hit about 150 million people. A Chinese breakup would hit ten times as many, inside a nuclear state at the centre of global supply chains.
  • Nor too much success. A friend told Brown that without the Party, the world would face "100 million Jack Mas". A democratic China that won by the West's own rules would still push America into second place.

The narrow slot the West leaves for China

A schematic of the chapter's argument with no scale. Brown argues that what the West really wants is a humble, Japan-like junior partner that obeys the rules and never overtakes it. Underneath, he says, the problem is the West's fear of losing first place more than China's politics.

05What China wants

Power without responsibility

China wants to get rich, to have its core interests respected, and to be admired. It doesn't want to police anyone. Brown says those aims pull against each other.

  • Getting rich together. Since 1978 China has sought capital, technology and know-how. Now it wants specific things, such as better chips and large aircraft. It also needs a stable world while it deals with ageing, inequality, pollution and a health system that gets only 6% of GDP. Xi's 2017 Davos speech defending free trade was the pitch.
  • Dependence it would rather not have. China uses almost a quarter of the world's energy. Coal supplies 70% of it, and half its oil is imported, much of it through the Malacca Strait, which the US Navy dominates. In 2020 it bought USD70 billion of Australian ore, three times as much as from Brazil, despite poor relations. Each side is reluctantly dependent on the other.
  • Core interests, policed abroad. In 2009 Dai Bingguo listed them as the political system and security, sovereignty and territory, and stable development. Beijing pushes hard on these. After David Cameron met the Dalai Lama, high-level UK–China contact was frozen for over a year. Outside these issues, Brown says, China is largely silent.
  • No policeman. China's only treaty ally is North Korea, under a 1961 treaty renewed in 2021. Its aid to Vietnam and North Korea brought ingratitude, and it watched American wars in Afghanistan and the Middle East go wrong. The US withdrawal from Afghanistan in 2021 left a vacuum on China's border that it had no hard means to fill.
  • Status. China worries about losing face and complains of "hurt feelings", and online boycotts punish brands like Zara. The disappointing response to its 2008 Olympics hurt. Brown calls US–China relations a love story with a spurned partner, and says Xi is the first modern Chinese leader who doesn't share the old fascination with America.

Loud at the core, quiet beyond

Dai Bingguo's three core interests from 2009, drawn as rings. To the "three Ts" (Taiwan, Tibet, Tiananmen) Brown adds an X for Xinjiang. Pressure on these issues is real, he says, but often backfires, as when a 2017 Durham Union debate got far more attention after the embassy objected.

Two mirrors

Brown's closing image for the two chapters. The shared wants are ordinary and rational. The trouble comes from what each side wants the other to be.

Chapters 6–8 · Separation

Separation

The hardest case, the forecasts that failed, and a proposal: stop waiting for China to converge, and build a world with two tracks, with fences in some places and common ground in others.

06The dark side

Xinjiang and the limits of leverage

Brown doesn't dispute that serious abuses have happened. His question is how outsiders could change anything when each side describes the same events in incompatible terms.

  • Memory. Brown first went in 1995, when Han Chinese were barely 10% of Kashgar and the city felt occupied. He returned as a diplomat in 2002. He traces how railways, oil wealth and controls on language and mosques grew, and borrows Darren Byler's term "terror capitalism".
  • Tibet's poorer relative. Xinjiang had no figure like the Dalai Lama. The moderate scholar Ilham Tohti was jailed for life in 2014. Over 200 people died in the 2009 Urumqi riots and 35 in the 2014 Kunming knife attack. From 2018, reports said over a million people had been detained.
  • Two descriptions of one reality. Beijing describes counter-terror re-education. Rights groups, exiles and the press describe mass internment, forced indoctrination, surveillance and charges of genocide. Brown argues the officials are not pretending: they believe their own account. The one fact both sides accept is that the camps were so expensive they began to be wound down from late 2019.
  • No common ground. History doesn't help: James Millward shows the region wasn't a single unit within its present borders until the eighteenth century. Morality doesn't either, since Beijing answers with Iraq and colonial history. Language is contested too: once "genocide" is used, Beijing stops talking.
  • The quandary. The fiercer the condemnation, the less influence it brings. Cutting all ties would mean ending trade, climate cooperation and contact between people, which nobody is going to do. Brown asks whether critics want change in Xinjiang or just want to criticise.

The condemnation loop

A schematic of Brown's argument. He grants that outside scrutiny may prevent worse. But now that China depends less on the West, it can call the West's bluff, and harsher words alone don't break the loop.

07The great separation

Modern, just not the Western way

Theory said economic growth would bring democracy. China is testing that theory, and Brown argues the West has always been about ten years late in responding.

  • Another world. When Brown took Australian officials to a Starbucks in Hangzhou in 2018, the barista was baffled by their cash. China has built a fully modern way of life on its own phone payment systems and apps, and the pandemic deepened the split.
  • Explanation one: it's a con. Brown lists the predictions of collapse: Lardy on bad loans in 2001, Hutton in 2007, the "ghost city" of Ordos in 2012, and Evergrande as "China's Lehman moment" in 2021. Irvine found that forecasters kept underestimating China. By 2016 Ordos had 100,000 daytime residents.
  • Explanation two: it must democratise. Henry Rowen predicted democracy by 2015. In 2012 Larry Diamond gave it a generation. Brown's own 2011 book Ballot Box China expected the same. In his 2010 report for the World Movement for Democracy he wrote that only the Party could solve the Party's problems, and only when it chose to.
  • 1989. Days after condemning the Tiananmen crackdown, the first President Bush reached out to Deng's leadership. Brown argues this was the West's one real chance to back change, and it chose stability and business. Russia's chaotic 1990s then convinced Beijing it had been right.
  • Dual circulation. Announced in 2020, it is Brown's reading of China telling the West it has had enough. It has two prongs: home consumption, stuck at about a third of GNP, and home-grown technology. R&D spending reached USD378 billion in 2020, with rises of at least 7% a year planned to 2025.
  • Not a new Cold War. The USSR exported its ideology and stood outside the Western economy. China does neither, and climate change ties everyone together. Russians remember the first Cold War "victory" as bitter, so China won't sign up for the sequel.

Forecasts against what happened

The forecasts are the ones Brown cites in ch. 7, placed by the year they were made. Brown doesn't claim China will never democratise or never face a crisis. His point is that confident timelines have kept failing, and that he got it wrong too.

Always five to ten years too late

At a 2018 conference, a former security analyst said the West was a decade too late to stop Huawei, because its markets and technology no longer mattered much to the company. Brown makes this a rule for three decades of Western policy: by the time the West acts, the horse has bolted.

08The dual track world

From a bungalow to a tower

The US and China have decided they don't like each other, and neither can leave. Brown proposes accepting that and building a world with fences in some places and common ground in others.

  • We are not the authors. In 2020 Mike Pompeo said US engagement had rescued China's economy. Brown answers that engagement was never a gift. Mao's China reached out first, by putting Edgar Snow on the Tiananmen rostrum in 1970, and US firms, starting with Coca-Cola's 1979 joint venture in Tianjin, came for profit. He thinks the resentment is really about China succeeding at capitalism, not failing at democracy.
  • Four things to accept. There was never a real choice about engaging a country with a fifth of humanity. Forecasts deserve humility. The story that economic reform would bring democracy was written by the West, and nobody asked China. And the West has to decide which problem it faces: an enemy out to conquer, or an exceptionalist that simply dissents.
  • The end of saving China. Brown remembers his own pity for China in the 1990s and 2000s. After 2008 Europe went to Beijing for money, and in 2020 China sent face masks to Italy. The question of who is helping whom has reversed.
  • Moving house. America's unipolar moment is like living in a bungalow. Next comes a building with several floors, the US on one and China on another, and plenty of stairs to fall down. It will be messy and pluralist, with more need for diplomats than ever, and China will find that others' hopes weigh on it as much as their fears.

Takeaways

A toolkit for thinking about China

Brown's arguments turned into habits you can use when reading the next headline about China.

Reading China

Distrust confident forecastsCollapse and democracy have both been predicted for decades. Expect China to surprise you either way.
Ask for the evidenceSeparate what China does openly and loudly from claims about a hidden hand that can never be seen.
Ask how people believeExpect several belief systems held at once, each in its own sphere. Party doctrine is often background.
Look for the limitsPorts, institutes and island-building often show frustration as much as strength.

Dealing with it

Know the core listThe system, Taiwan, Tibet, Xinjiang, Hong Kong and the seas. Expect pressure there and indifference elsewhere.
Be cautious where it countsOn sensitive technology, assume the worst. That doesn't mean the worst applies everywhere.
Aim for change, not just condemnationBefore choosing words, ask whether they will change anything for the people affected.
Protect the shared courtyardClimate, pandemics, nuclear risk and AI need China whatever else happens.

Checking ourselves

Notice who wrote the story"Growth brings democracy" was the West's script. China never agreed to it.
Name what you wantSucceed but not too much, change but stay junior: check whether your own demands contradict each other.
Drop Cold War 2A rematch frame promises a win that interdependence rules out.
Act earlier, or acceptLeverage fades fast. Use it while it exists, or work with the situation as it is.
