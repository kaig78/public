A Primer

Donella Meadows argues that most stubborn problems aren't caused by villains or bad luck. They come from the way the system is put together, so that's where the fix has to go.

The big idea

The system causes its own behaviour

Meadows opens her teaching with a Slinky. She holds it between two hands, pulls the lower hand away, and the spring bounces. Ask what made it bounce and students say the hand. Then she does the same with the Slinky's box, and nothing happens. The bounce was in the spring all along. The hand only released it.

That's the book's main claim. A system is a set of parts connected so that together they produce their own pattern of behaviour over time. Outside events can trigger that pattern, but the same push applied to a different system gives a different result. Recessions, lost market share, oil shocks and addiction keep coming back because the structures that produce them stay in place.

Meadows doesn't think this replaces ordinary analysis. She calls it another lens, like a microscope or a telescope, and it shows things the others miss. To use it you need a small vocabulary: stocks, flows, and the feedback loops that connect them.

She finished a draft in 1993. When she died in 2001 it was still unpublished. Diana Wright of the Sustainability Institute edited it and published it in 2008, keeping most of Meadows's early-1990s examples.

The parable: the blind men and the elephant

In the Sufi tale, each blind man feels one part of the elephant: an ear, the trunk, a leg. Each is sure he knows what an elephant is, and each is wrong. Meadows uses it to make her first point. Knowing the parts of a system won't tell you how the whole behaves.

Part One · Chapters 1–2

Structure and behaviour

First Meadows takes a system apart into its pieces. Then she puts the pieces back together as feedback loops and runs simple models, so you can see which shapes of behaviour each structure produces.

01The Basics

Elements, interconnections, purpose

The parts are the easiest thing to see and usually matter least. The rules connecting them matter more, and the purpose matters most.

  • A system has three ingredients: elements, interconnections, and a function or purpose. Sand scattered on a road isn't a system, because adding or removing some changes nothing. Add or remove players on a football team and the team stops working.
  • Change the parts one at a time and see what happens. Swap every player and it's still a football team. Change the rules to basketball and it becomes a different game. Keep the players and the rules but make the goal losing instead of winning, and the system changes completely.
  • You learn a system's purpose by watching what it does, not what it says it wants. A government that claims to care about the environment but spends almost nothing on it doesn't have environmental protection as its purpose.
  • A stock is anything that accumulates: water in a tub, wood in a forest, money, self-confidence. Flows fill it or drain it. A stock is the memory of all the flows that have passed through it.
  • The bathtub rules: if inflows are greater than outflows, the stock rises. If outflows are greater, it falls. If they're equal, the stock stays level even though water keeps moving through it. People tend to watch inflows and forget outflows, but using less oil does the same job as finding a new oil field.
  • Stocks change slowly, even when flows change suddenly. That makes them buffers and sources of momentum. It's also why populations, forests and polluted groundwater can't be turned around quickly. Stocks also decouple inflows from outflows, which is why reservoirs, bank accounts and inventories exist at all.
  • A feedback loop forms when the level of a stock changes the flows into or out of it. When behaviour persists over time, look for a loop.

Balancing loop: a cup of coffee

The bigger the gap between the coffee and the room, the faster it cools. Hot coffee and iced coffee both head for room temperature, quickly at first and then more slowly.

Reinforcing loop: money in the bank

The more money there is, the more interest it earns, and the interest adds to the money. At low rates it looks like a straight line for a while, but it keeps speeding up. The same loop drives population, capital and epidemics.

The habit it builds

Meadows warns that once you start seeing feedback, you see it everywhere. When the news says the Federal Reserve moved the economy, you start asking how the economy moved the Fed. When someone says population growth causes poverty, you ask whether poverty causes population growth.

02The Systems Zoo

A few structures, a few behaviours

Systems that look nothing alike behave alike when their feedback structures match. A population and an industrial economy are the same kind of animal.

  • Thermostat, two balancing loops. The furnace pulls the room toward the setting while heat leaks out toward the outdoor temperature. The room levels off slightly below 18°C, because the leak never stops. The general rule: a goal has to allow for whatever is draining the stock. Paying off a credit card means covering new charges and interest as well as the balance.
  • Feedback only affects the future. A flow can't respond directly to another flow. It responds to a stock, and always with some delay. Meadows says many economic models go wrong by assuming instant responses.
  • Population, one reinforcing and one balancing loop. In 2007 there were 6.6 billion people, with about 21 births and 9 deaths per 1,000 people a year. If those rates held, the population would more than double within a child's first 60 years. Which loop dominates decides whether the stock grows, shrinks or levels off. When loop strengths change over time, the behaviour changes too. Systems thinkers call this shifting dominance.
  • Three questions to ask of any model's scenario: Are the driving factors likely to unfold this way? Would the system really respond like this? And what drives the driving factors? Models explore "what if". They don't predict.
  • Capital has the same structure as population. Investment plays the role of births and depreciation the role of deaths. With output equal to a third of capital and 20% of output invested, capital that lasts 15 years only just keeps up with wear. Capital that lasts longer grows. Again, you can grow a stock by slowing what drains it.
  • Inventory, with delays. A car dealer aims to keep ten days of sales on the lot. She averages sales over 5 days before reacting, closes a third of any gap with each order, and waits 5 days for deliveries. When demand rises 10% for good, the inventory starts to oscillate. These aren't her mistakes. Her information arrives late and her actions take time to have an effect.

Cars on the lot over 100 days. Shortening how long the dealer averages sales (from 5 days to 2) barely helps. Reacting faster makes the swings much worse. Reacting more slowly damps them. Meadows calls this "high leverage, wrong direction": the fixer finds the right lever and pulls it the wrong way.

  • Join many such dealers, factories and suppliers together, each reacting with its own delays, and add reinforcing loops through jobs and share prices. Meadows argues that this, not presidents, is the main cause of business cycles.
  • Capital drawing on a nonrenewable resource. An oil company starts with enough oil for 200 years at its initial rate and reinvests profits so its capital grows 5% a year. Extraction peaks after about 40 years and then collapses, because the remaining oil gets harder to reach. Doubling or quadrupling the oil field moves the peak only about 14 years each time.
  • If the price rises as oil gets scarce, capital grows higher before it falls, and the remaining oil runs out faster at the end. Cheaper extraction technology has the same effect. The real choice, Meadows says, is between getting very rich fast and getting less rich for longer.

  • Capital drawing on a renewable resource, such as a fishery. Fish breed fastest at middling densities and slowest when crowded or scarce. With modest fishing technology the fleet levels off at a harvest it can sustain. A little more efficiency, such as sonar, produces oscillations. A lot more lets boats keep fishing at very low fish densities, and both the fish and the industry collapse.
  • Nonrenewable resources are limited by the size of the stock. Renewable resources are limited by the rate of regrowth. Harvest faster than the resource regrows, push it below its threshold, and it becomes, in practice, nonrenewable. New England's logging industry is on its third cycle of growth, overcutting, collapse and regrowth.
Part Two · Chapters 3–5

Systems and us

Why well-built systems hold up so well, why they keep surprising the people inside them, and the recurring structures that turn sensible decisions into outcomes nobody wanted.

03Why Systems Work So Well

Resilience, self-organisation, hierarchy

These three properties let systems last. They're hard to see, so they're often traded away for short-term productivity or stability.

  • Resilience is the ability to recover after a shock. It comes from many balancing loops working through different mechanisms, at different speeds, and backing each other up. Loops that can rebuild other loops add a further layer, and loops that can learn and evolve add another.
  • Resilience isn't the same as stability. A resilient system can swing a lot, and a perfectly steady one can be brittle. Meadows pictures resilience as a plateau with soft walls. As resilience is lost, the plateau shrinks until one familiar move sends the system over the edge.
  • Examples of resilience traded away: growth hormone that raises a cow's milk yield but leaves the cow less healthy, just-in-time supply chains that cut costs but break when fuel or traffic is disrupted, and single-species European forests that turned out to be vulnerable to air pollution.
  • Self-organisation is a system's ability to make its own structure more complex. Very simple rules can produce enormous complexity, as the Koch snowflake and DNA show. Self-organisation needs freedom and some disorder, which is why power structures, schools and economic policy often hold it back.
  • Hierarchy is systems nested inside systems. Links are dense within each subsystem and sparser between them, so no level is overwhelmed with information. Hierarchies grow from the bottom up, and Meadows says the upper levels exist to serve the lower ones.
  • Hierarchies fail in two directions. In suboptimisation, a part pursues its own goal at the expense of the whole, like a cancer cell or a student who cheats. Under too much central control, the top stops the parts from looking after themselves.

04Why Systems Surprise Us

Our models are good, but never good enough

Everything we think we know is a model. Models fit the world well enough to get by, and badly enough to keep surprising us.

Three levels of seeing

EventsA team wins, a river floods, the Dow hits 10,000. Gripping, but explaining one event with another tells you almost nothing about tomorrow.
BehaviourPatterns over time: a winning streak, floods and droughts both getting worse. Most economic analysis stops here, linking flows to other flows statistically.
StructureThe stocks, flows and loops that produce the behaviour. Only this level tells you how to change it.

Statistical models that link one flow to another work until the structure changes: someone opens a window, the insulation is improved, the oil runs out. They're decent at short-term forecasts and of little use for improving the system.

  • Nonlinearities. Effects aren't proportional to causes. Traffic speed holds up until density reaches a tipping point. Soil erosion barely hurts yields until the topsoil is thinner than the root zone. Nonlinearities change which loop dominates, and that can flip a system into a completely different mode.
  • Boundaries that don't exist. The clouds at the edges of a diagram stand for stocks we've chosen to ignore. Draw the boundary too tight and you get surprises, like the new highway that attracts housing until it's as jammed as before. Draw it too wide and the analysis gets buried in detail. The right boundary depends on the question you're asking.
  • Layers of limits. Liebig's law of the minimum says the scarcest input sets the limit, and adding more of anything else won't help. As a system grows, it changes which input is scarcest. Meadows argues that limits to growth are certain. The only choice is whether a system sets them itself or has them imposed.
  • Delays everywhere. Livestock and crop cycles run about 4 years for pigs, 7 for cows and 11 for cocoa. A new car takes 3 to 8 years to design, sells for about 5, and stays on the road 10 to 15. Jay Forrester's advice: ask everyone how long a delay will be, make your best guess, then multiply by three.
  • Bounded rationality, Herbert Simon's term. People make sensible decisions with the partial, delayed information they can see from where they stand. Put anyone in a fisherman's position, with a mortgage on the boat and no count of the fish, and they'll overfish too. Replacing the person rarely helps. Redesigning the information and incentives does.
Interlude: spruce budworm

Budworms have killed fir and spruce in cycles for at least 400 years, which kept fir from crowding out other trees. Spraying began in the 1950s and killed the budworm's predators as well. It kept fir dense and left the forest on the brink of an outbreak all the time. By 1980, New Brunswick was spending $12.5 million a year on spraying, and the insect was still killing trees across as much as 20 million hectares a year.

Interlude: Dutch electric meters

In a Dutch suburb of identical houses, some had their electricity meter in the basement and some in the front hall. During the 1970s energy crisis, the front-hall homes turned out to use about a third less electricity. The residents could see the wheel turning as they walked past. The houses were the same. What differed was the feedback.

05System Traps . . . and Opportunities

Eight structures behind familiar failures

Systems thinkers call these structures archetypes. Blaming people, firing them or pulling policy levers harder won't fix them. Changing the structure will, so each trap is also an opportunity.

BPolicy resistance

Several actors pull one stock toward different goals. Any push meets an equal counter-push. Romania banned abortion in 1967; the birth rate tripled, then fell back as illegal abortions tripled maternal deaths.

Let go, bring all the actors in, and look for a bigger goal they can share. Sweden's 1930s policy aimed at every child being wanted and cared for.

RTragedy of the commons

Each user gains fully from using a shared, erodable resource and bears only a small share of the damage. Feedback from the resource to the user is weak or comes too late. Garrett Hardin described it in 1968.

Educate, privatise, or regulate through "mutual coercion, mutually agreed upon" (Hardin's phrase): traffic lights, parking meters, garbage fees.

RDrift to low performance

The goal follows perceived performance, and bad news is believed more readily than good. Each disappointment lowers the bar a little, like the boiled frog. Meadows's own example is a jogging programme that faded away.

Keep standards absolute, or set them from the best past results instead of the worst.

REscalation

Each side sets its goal a little above where it sees the other: arms races, price wars, smear campaigns, ever-louder advertising. It grows exponentially and ends when someone breaks down.

Stay out of it. If you're already in, disarm unilaterally or negotiate new balancing loops.

RSuccess to the successful

Winners get the means to win again: Monopoly, a Christmas-lights contest whose $100 prize kept going to the same family, competitive exclusion in ecology. The trap sorts people into permanent winners and losers.

Diversify, cap any one winner's share, level the playing field, and give rewards that don't tilt the next round.

RShifting the burden (addiction)

An intervention relieves the symptom, and the system's own ability to cope withers away. Then more intervention is needed. Pesticides, subsidies, fixed oil prices.

Avoid it in the first place. As an intervenor, strengthen the system's own ability and then step back. As a dependant, rebuild that ability now.

±Rule beating

Obeying the letter of a rule and defeating its purpose. Vermont's Act 250 led to lots of just over ten acres. Europe's feed-grain limits brought in cassava from Asia instead.

Read rule beating as feedback. Design rules so people's creativity goes into meeting their purpose.

±Seeking the wrong goal

The system delivers exactly what it is measured on. Counting IUD insertions in India led doctors to fit them without patients' consent. Meadows argues GNP counts effort and throughput, not welfare.

Choose indicators that reflect real welfare, and don't confuse effort with results.

R = the trap is driven by a reinforcing loop · B = balancing loops locked against each other · ± = a goal or rule set badly. Grouping drawn for this page.

Interlude: sailboat design

Racing rules were written to keep competitors evenly matched. Designers then built boats to win under those rules. Today's racing yachts are extremely fast and nearly unseaworthy, so finely tuned to the current rules that any change would make them useless. Meadows uses them as an example of a system optimised so hard that it has lost all resilience.

06Leverage Points

Twelve places to intervene

People inside a system often sense where the leverage is. They often push it the wrong way.

  • Jay Forrester's world model found that growth itself was a key leverage point. Leaders treat growth as the answer to almost every problem. Meadows says they are pushing hard in the wrong direction. His 1969 urban study likewise pointed to less subsidised low-income housing, not more.
  • Meadows drafted the list during a meeting on global trade, frustrated, and offers it with humility. She calls the order loose, and every item has exceptions. Read it from the bottom (weakest) to the top (strongest).

  • Parameters matter when they push the system into a range where something higher on the list kicks in, such as interest rates or birth rates that set the gain of a reinforcing loop. That's rarer than people think.
  • Missing feedback is one of the most common reasons systems fail. Meadows proposes making feedback compelling: put a town's water intake downstream of its own outflow pipe, or require politicians who declare war to serve at the front.
  • Changing the players is low leverage, except at the very top. One leader who sets a new goal can turn millions of people in a new direction.
  • Paradigms change the way Thomas Kuhn describes. Keep pointing out the old view's failures, speak confidently from the new one, put its supporters in visible places, and spend your time on the open-minded middle.
07Living in a World of Systems

You can't control it. You can learn to move with it

Meadows and her MIT colleagues expected systems thinking to deliver prediction and control. It didn't. What it offered instead turned out to be worth more.

Meadows admits that the modellers didn't follow their own advice. They lectured on addiction and couldn't give up coffee. They understood eroding goals and still let their jogging slide. Knowing how to fix a system turned out to be a long way from fixing it.

Self-organising, nonlinear feedback systems are inherently unpredictable, she concludes. The future can't be forecast precisely, but it can be imagined and designed for. The final chapter is a list of practices her colleagues picked up, some knowingly and some not. Most of them are in the toolkit below.

Two examples stand out. When the Toxic Release Inventory made companies' emissions public from 1988, reported emissions fell 40% within two years, with no fines involved. And the 1987 Montreal Protocol built in monitoring and review, so in 1990 the phase-out could be sped up when the ozone damage proved worse than expected.

"We can't control systems or figure them out. But we can dance with them!"

Donella Meadows, ch. 7
The last trap

Meadows ends by applying drift to low performance to morality. When bad behaviour is treated as normal and goodness as an exception, expectations fall. Her remedy is the same as for any eroding goal: don't weigh bad news more heavily than good, and keep the standard absolute.

From the book's guidelines and trap exits

The toolkit

Meadows ends with 15 habits for living in a world of systems. Most of them are here, together with a few lessons from earlier chapters, grouped by when you'd reach for them.

Before you act

Get the beat of the systemWatch first. Collect its history and graph real data over time. Memories are poor at timing, and people often frame the problem as the lack of their favourite solution.
Expose your mental modelsWrite them down, as diagrams, lists or equations. Keep several explanations open until the evidence rules some out.
Ask what drives the driversFor any forecast: would the system really respond this way, and what sets the inputs?
Redraw the boundary for each questionLook beyond the clouds. Where does the waste go, and where do the inputs come from?
Defy the disciplinesFollow the system across fields. Learn from economists and chemists without being limited by their lenses.

When you intervene

Honour, respect and distribute informationDon't distort, delay or withhold it. Putting the meter in the hallway often beats passing a new law.
Make feedback policies for feedback systemsPolicies that adjust with the state of the system, like Carter's proposed import-linked fuel tax, and that include ways to learn and revise.
Locate responsibility in the systemDesign things so decision makers feel the results, the way a pilot rides at the front of the plane.
Listen to the wisdom of the systemStrengthen what already works, often at the bottom of the hierarchy, before charging in with outside fixes.
Slow the loops before you strengthen the brakesReducing the gain on a growth loop usually beats adding more correction.

As a way of being

Pay attention to what's important, not just what's measurableBe a quality detector. Leaving something out because it's hard to quantify gives you a worse model.
Use language with careKeep words concrete and truthful, and learn words like resilience, carrying capacity and feedback.
Go for the good of the wholeDon't optimise one part while ignoring the whole system.
Stay humble, stay a learnerTake small steps, keep monitoring, and admit errors openly.
Expand time horizons and the boundary of caringWatch both the next step and the far horizon. In an interconnected system, the parts can't succeed if the whole fails.
