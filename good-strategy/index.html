Introduction · Overwhelming obstacles

A strategy is a way through a difficulty

At Trafalgar in 1805, Nelson was outnumbered. Instead of fighting in parallel lines, he drove two columns straight into the enemy line, betting that its formation would break and his more experienced captains would win the close fighting. The French and Spanish lost 22 ships. The British lost none.

Good strategy, Rumelt argues, usually looks this simple: find the one or two pivot points and concentrate on them. What often passes for strategy is ambition instead. Lehman Brothers' 2006 plan, for example, was to grow faster than rivals by taking deals they turned down. A web CEO once told him strategy is "never quitting until you win."

His definition is narrower: a coherent response to an important challenge. Executives who say they have an "execution problem" have usually confused strategy with setting goals. The actions are part of the strategy, not a detail for later.

The core idea is the kernel: a diagnosis, a guiding policy and coherent actions. Bad strategy is not just a missing kernel. It has its own logic, and it is spreading.

Part I · Chapters 1–5

Good and bad strategy

Rumelt says two sources of strength get overlooked: coherence, meaning policies that support each other, and new insight that changes what counts as strength. Bad strategy crowds both out, and chapter 5 gives its antidote.

01Unexpected

Focus surprises people

Most organisations spread their resources, so a rival that actually concentrates catches everyone off guard.

  • Apple, 1997. Apple was two months from bankruptcy. Jobs cut 15 desktop models to one and all portables to one laptop. He dropped printers and five of six national retailers, moved manufacturing to Taiwan and cut inventory by more than 80%. It was Business 101, and nobody expected it.
  • Waiting for the window. Asked in 1998 how Apple could escape its niche of under 4% of the market, Jobs didn't name a target. Rumelt had interviewed 26 European electronics executives. Each could explain how their industry's leader had won, by being first through a new window of opportunity, but none was looking for the next one.
  • Desert Storm. A direct attack on Kuwait was estimated to cost 2,000 dead and 8,000 wounded. Schwarzkopf instead secretly shifted 250,000 troops west for a "left hook", and the ground war lasted 100 hours. The move was the Army's textbook envelopment. The surprise was that a big organisation actually focused, overriding the air force, marines and coalition partners to do it.
"I am going to wait for the next big thing."
Steve Jobs to Rumelt, summer 1998
02Discovering power

Strength is often a matter of viewpoint

The most powerful strategies come from seeing an advantage that others have overlooked.

  • David and Goliath. A list of strengths and weaknesses made before the fight favours Goliath. The sling reveals a different asymmetry: force delivered precisely from a distance, against an unarmoured forehead.
  • Wal-Mart's real unit. The accepted rule was that a discount store needed a town of at least 100,000 people. Walton didn't break the rule. He redefined the store. The basic unit became a network of about 150 stores around a distribution centre, with shared trucks, data and managers.
  • Pieces you can't copy one at a time. Kmart also installed bar-code scanners. But its store managers chose their own vendors and prices, so the data couldn't drive an integrated logistics system. Kmart filed for bankruptcy in 2002.
  • Competing with the Soviets. Andy Marshall and James Roche's 1976 paper proposed using US technology to impose disproportionate costs, for example with quieter submarines, instead of answering each Soviet threat. Rumelt believes it helped overstretch the USSR.
03Bad strategy

Four ways to spot it

Bad strategy uses fluff, avoids naming the problem, presents goals as strategy and sets objectives that miss the point or can't be met.

  • Fluff. A bank announced a strategy of "customer-centric intermediation", which in plain words means being a bank. In 2000 an Arthur Andersen unit praised Enron's bandwidth trading with a diagram of "evolving" markets. It skipped the fact that bandwidth had no spot price and near-zero marginal cost.
  • Not facing the problem. International Harvester's 1979 plan promised more market share in every division. It never mentioned work rules, terrible labour relations or margins half its rivals'. Between 1979 and 1985 it lost more than $3 billion and closed 35 of its 42 plants.
  • Goals presented as strategy. A graphics firm's "20/20 plan" called for 20% growth and 20% margins, against a history of about 12%. Its "key strategies" were wishes. Rumelt compares this faith in one more push to Passchendaele.
  • Bad objectives. One city's plan had 47 strategies and 178 action items, and item 122 was to make a strategic plan. A Los Angeles schools plan sensibly focused on its 34 weakest schools, then asked for "transformational leadership" without asking why leadership had failed.
Logan's "key strategies"
  • Be the graphics firm of choice

  • Keep margins at 20% or more

04Why so much

Choosing hurts, so people avoid it

Bad strategy isn't a calculation error. It comes from avoiding the hard work of choosing, which templates and positive thinking make easy to skip.

  • DEC, 1992. DEC's executives were split between "Boxes", "Solutions" and "Chips". Ken Olsen asked them for consensus and got a statement about being committed to quality and leadership in data processing. His successor chose Chips, five years too late. Compaq bought DEC in 1998.
  • Saying no. In 1985 Gordon Moore told Andy Grove that a new CEO would take Intel out of memory chips. Even then the exit took more than a year. By 1992 Intel was the world's largest chipmaker.
  • Charisma in a can. In the 1980s, charismatic leadership was recast as "transformational" leadership anyone could learn. It became a template of vision, mission, values and "strategies" that says nothing, as in NEC's lofty vision from a firm earning under 2% on equity.
  • New Thought. Rumelt traces the belief that thinking about success produces it from 1880s writers to The Secret (2007). He wouldn't fly in a plane designed by people who never considered how it could fail.

Three executives, three options, no stable majority

Each option beats one rival and loses to the other, so any majority can be overturned. Rumelt uses Condorcet's paradox to show why demanding consensus from a divided group produces mush. Someone has to choose.

05The kernel

Diagnosis, guiding policy, coherent action

A strategy is an argument that runs from the facts to a diagnosis, then to a policy and to coordinated actions. If any of the three is missing, it fails.

  • Diagnosis. The question is "what's going on here?" A diagnosis is a judgment that simplifies the situation, and the best one points to something you can act on. In 1993 Lou Gerstner rejected the plan to break up IBM. He judged that IBM's problem was failing to use its integration, not having too much of it.
  • Guiding policy. A guiding policy works like a guardrail: it rules out many moves without spelling out each step. Wells Fargo's vision is an ambition. Its cross-selling policy is a way of competing.
  • Coherent action. Ford under Jacques Nasser bought Volvo and Jaguar for their brands, then put them on a shared platform to save cost. The two moves worked against each other. Coordination has a cost, because it cuts into specialisation, so impose it only where the gains are large.

The kernel, applied to a corner grocery

Stephanie, a friend of Rumelt's, faced a long list of separate questions about her corner grocery. Choosing one customer group turned them into decisions that reinforce each other. Nothing proves it was the best policy, but without one the decisions would pull against each other.

Part II · Chapters 6–15

Sources of power

A good strategy makes effort go further. These ten chapters cover where that extra force comes from, and the inertia and decay that drain it. Chapter 15 pulls them together using Nvidia.

06Leverage

Anticipate, find the pivot, concentrate

Leverage comes from predicting what others will do, finding the point where a small push releases larger forces, and putting your effort there.

  • Anticipation. Toyota spent more than $1 billion on hybrids during the SUV boom, betting that rivals would license its system. At Shell, Pierre Wack saw the real oil risk as a price spike that lures you into investing just before a crash.
  • Pivot points. Japanese shoppers tire of products fast. A 7-Eleven shows 50 soft drinks with 70% turnover a year. 7-Eleven Japan built quick-response teams to turn local taste data into new private-label products.
  • Concentration. Below a certain level, effort does nothing. With about $65 million a year, Harold Williams at the Getty Trust chose not to bid up art prices. He aimed to change the study of art itself, a goal his budget could decisively affect.
07Proximate objectives

Give people a problem they can solve

A leader's job is to take on much of the ambiguity and give the organisation a target within reach.

  • The moon was proximate. Wernher von Braun advised that a crewed landing needed rockets about ten times more powerful than either side had. That would let America's larger resources decide the race. Kennedy made his speech a month later.
  • Phyllis's moon. Nobody at JPL knew whether the lunar surface was powder, needle-like crystals or boulders. Phyllis Buwalda specified hard, grainy ground with gentle slopes and small boulders, much like the Southwestern desert. Engineers could build to that, and Surveyor landed.
  • More uncertainty, closer objectives. A regional business school swapped its list of initiatives for one aim: better jobs for graduates, starting with ten target firms.
  • Skills come in layers. A rescue pilot told Rumelt you learn to fly first, then to fly at night, then in formation. Organisations climb in the same order.
"The engineers can't work without a specification."
Phyllis Buwalda, JPL Future Mission Studies
08Chain links

When the weakest link sets the limit

In a chain-linked system, improving one link adds cost and no value. The same logic that keeps such systems stuck also makes them very hard to copy when they work.

  • Getting stuck. When each link is managed separately, nobody gains from improving their own, so the system stalls. GM from 1980 to 2008 is Rumelt's example: a better transmission doesn't help while door panels rattle.
  • Getting unstuck. Marco Tinelli's Lombardy machine company had poor quality, weak salespeople and high costs. He ran three campaigns in sequence, suspended the usual local measures, and took the short-term losses himself.
  • Excellence. IKEA's policies are chain-linked: its own flat-pack designs, giant suburban stores, catalogues instead of salespeople, and its own logistics. Copying one piece adds cost, and Rumelt notes nobody has copied the whole in over fifty years.

Raise one link and the system doesn't move

Bar heights are illustrative. The book doesn't say how long the sales campaign lasted. Tinelli scheduled cost last so that cost cutting would fit the machines rather than define them.

09Design

Strategy is designed more than chosen

Many strategies are designed rather than chosen from a menu. The fewer resources you have, the more tightly the parts must fit.

  • Cannae, 216 BC. Hannibal had about 55,000 troops against 85,000 or more Romans. He let his centre fall back while his cavalry circled behind them. The Romans were packed so tightly they couldn't fight, and at least 50,000 died. The battle was planned in advance and turned the enemy's own behaviour into the trap.
  • Voyager on a weight budget. At JPL, Rumelt found that a 3,000-pound Jupiter probe could be bolted together from standard subsystems. A 1,200-pound version forced every subsystem to be traded off against the others.
  • Learn from upstarts. Xerox's patents made strategy almost unnecessary, and that comfort bred laxity. Tightly designed attacks come from challengers like Canon. Paccar is a durable example: it designs around owner-drivers, makes only premium heavy trucks, and hasn't lost money since 1939.

Fewer resources demand tighter design

With fixed resources, a bigger challenge calls for tighter integration. With a fixed challenge, better resources reduce the need for it. Tight designs are also narrower and more fragile.

10Focus

Working out a strategy from its policies

To find a firm's real strategy, list the policies that differ from industry norms and ask what they have in common.

  • A bad industry. The big can makers were captive suppliers to giant buyers and earned 4–5% on assets. Crown was 50–60% more profitable and returned 19% a year to shareholders over 35 years.
  • Follow the policies. The official explanation was hard-to-hold products like aerosols and soft drinks. Rumelt lists the policies that differ from the norm instead. Technical help targets small customers. Fast response targets rush orders. Small plants that never depend on one buyer keep spare lines ready.
  • The common thread: short runs. Crown's cost per can is higher and its prices are higher still. Several customers per plant means no single buyer can squeeze it.

Captive supplier versus focused supplier

The majors avoid short runs because changing over a can line is expensive. Crown accepted that cost and gained bargaining power.

11Growth

Growth for its own sake

Healthy growth is the reward for an advantage. Buying growth tends to destroy value.

  • Crown after Connelly. From 1989 William Avery made twenty acquisitions, making Crown the world's largest container maker. Its skill had been short runs, not cost cutting. Plastic-bottle growth hit its ceiling, prices fell, and the stock dropped from $55 to $5 between 1998 and 2001.
  • Why leaders buy anyway. Buyers usually pay about a 25% premium plus fees, and advisers earn the fees. A banker pushing a Telecom Italia merger ended up arguing that more cash flow lets you do a bigger deal. The board said no.

12Advantage

Owning an advantage doesn't make you richer

Every advantage applies only in particular conditions. Wealth comes from increasing an advantage, not just from holding it.

  • Don't wrestle the gorilla. A venture capitalist told a start-up with a clever new fabric to license it rather than build a clothing company: different game, different strengths. Rumelt argues the US is wrestling the gorilla in Afghanistan.
  • The silver machine. Rumelt and Steven Lippman imagined a machine making $10 million of silver a year at zero cost. If you buy it for $100 million at 10% interest, you earn an ordinary return. Owning an advantage isn't enough. It becomes "interesting" only when you can increase its value.
  • Interesting moves. Stewart Resnick paid to build demand for almonds and pistachios. The seven to ten years new trees need gave him time to build processing scale small growers can't match. He then repeated the playbook with POM pomegranate juice.

13Dynamics

Riding waves of change

Large outside shifts create new high ground. The gains go to people who understand the second-order effects, not just the obvious ones.

  • Seeing the swell. In 1996 a French CEO told Rumelt that Cisco's lead came from clever code written by a tiny team, not from scale. Behind it was the microprocessor. Software won because mistakes are cheap to fix, and once every component had its own chip the computer industry broke into separate horizontal layers.
  • Cisco's waves. Cisco rode software, corporate networking, the vendor-neutral IP protocol and, from 1993, the public Internet. It took two-thirds of corporate networking while incumbents protected their own protocols.
  • Guideposts. Watch for rising fixed costs, deregulation, forecasting biases and incumbent inertia. Sketch the attractor state, the most efficient shape the industry is moving towards.
14Inertia & entropy

Stalled and decaying organisations

Organisations resist change, and without maintenance they drift into disorder. Both open gaps that rivals can exploit.

  • Inertia of routine. After deregulation, Continental still "forecast" airfares with a cost-plus formula that only regulators had made true. Long-haul fares fell 27% in 1984–85.
  • Inertia of culture. Bell Labs quoted three million dollars and two years for a simple demo, and Rumelt wrote it himself in three weeks. To change a culture: simplify, break up units, sort them, and replace the people who set each group's norms.
  • Inertia by proxy. Phone companies held back DSL to protect their profitable T1 lines. When they finally responded in 2000, none of the upstart carriers survived.
  • Entropy. Sloan gave each GM brand its own price band in 1921. By 2008 GM offered nine cars at $25,500 against Toyota's two.

Denton's hump chart: half the stores cancel out the other half

Rumelt ranked the 28 outlets by "gain to operating" and added them up one by one. The best outlet alone, at $1.05 million, beat the whole chain. After Denton's dropped five outlets and copied its best ones for two years, the figure rose from about $100,000 to over $5 million.

15Putting it together

Nvidia, from nowhere to the top

Nvidia's rise used almost every source of power in the book: a wave of change, a clear policy, tightly designed actions and rivals that couldn't respond.

  • The wave. Online 3-D games like Quake made fast graphics a matter of life and death for players. Nvidia's first chip, the NV1, had flopped.
  • Policy and actions. Nvidia focused on PC 3-D graphics using SGI's pipeline approach and Microsoft's DirectX, and set out to ship a new chip every six months. It made that possible with three staggered teams, heavy simulation and one unified driver for every chip.
  • Rivals. 3dfx overreached and closed in 2000, and Intel's slower process couldn't keep up. SGI set a goal of 50% growth and went bankrupt in 2006. Rumelt calls Nvidia's decision not to buy ArtX, which revived ATI, a blunder.

Three overlapping teams, a new chip every six months

Against an eighteen-month rival, Nvidia would have the better chip about 83% of the time. From 1997 to 2001 its performance rose 157% a year on average.

Part III · Chapters 16–18

Thinking like a strategist

Frameworks matter less than habits of mind: treating strategy as a testable bet, questioning your first idea, and checking your own assessment against the crowd's.

16Science

A strategy is a hypothesis

Strategy works at the edge of what is known, so treat it as an informed guess and running the business as the experiment that tests it.

  • No crank to wind. Deduction only repeats what is already known. Competition pushes you to the edge of knowledge, where the uncertainty is also the opportunity.
  • Anomalies. In 1983 Howard Schultz noticed that Milan had 1,500 espresso bars. Expensive coffee was the mass market there, while Americans drank cheap coffee. That mismatch became a hypothesis.
  • Testing and revising. His first bar copied Milan exactly. Customer response led him to add chairs, paper cups and nonfat milk. By 2001 Starbucks had 4,700 outlets. Owning every stage let it capture what it learned.
17Using your head

The obstacle in every case: your own short sight

Being strategic largely means being less short-sighted than your unreflective self, which takes deliberate tools.

  • Make a list. In a story Rumelt heard, Frederick Taylor told Andrew Carnegie to list the ten most important things he could do and start on number one. The value is in making the list: finding where important and actionable overlap.
  • Quick closure. In a class on TiVo, almost every executive grabbed the first idea that came to mind and then defended it. A first answer feels like relief in a messy situation.
  • Tools. The kernel turns a single insight into a full argument. Problem-solution works back from a policy to its obstacle. Create-destroy means attacking your own options. A panel of experts means imagining critiques from people you respect. Write your judgments down before you know the outcome so you can learn from them.
18Keeping your head

Look past the crowd

Do your own analysis of the fundamentals. Social herding and the belief that "this time is different" hide what simple analysis would show.

  • Global Crossing. Its first Atlantic cable cost about $1.5 million per unit of capacity, and it sold that capacity for $8 million. At one point the market valued the company at $38 billion. The industry was a pure commodity with sunk costs and huge planned capacity. The company went bankrupt in December 2001.
  • 2008's errors. Rumelt lists engineering overreach, the "smooth-sailing" fallacy, incentives that rewarded risk, social herding, and the inside view: believing your case is special. US household debt went from 60% of after-tax income in 1984 to 130% in 2007.
  • The outside view. Twenty-one advanced economies had 28 severe house-price boom-bust cycles in fifty years, and the US had land-credit panics in 1819, 1837, 1873 and 1893. Rumelt argues that easy-credit ownership policies kept repeating the pattern.

Takeaways

The toolkit

Rumelt offers no template to fill in. But his cases suggest habits that apply to a company, a school board or your own career.

Diagnose

Ask "what's going on here?"Write down what matters most before deciding what to do.
Name the obstacle, not the targetUnderperformance is a result. The challenge is whatever causes it.
Look for the anomalyFacts that don't fit the accepted story are where new strategies start.
Study the competition's inertiaEvery big success involves rivals who couldn't respond.
Take the outside viewAsk how similar cases turned out elsewhere.

Decide

Check for the four hallmarksFluff, an unnamed problem, goals presented as strategy, and objectives that miss or can't be met.
Choose, and say noIf everyone agrees, probably nothing was chosen.
Find the pivot pointPut effort where a small push releases larger forces.
Prefer focus to compromiseGood strategies tend to commit fully to one approach rather than average between several.
Treat growth as an outcomeGrowth should follow from an advantage. Don't buy it and call that strategy.

Act

Make actions coherentEach action should support the others.
Set proximate objectivesAbsorb the ambiguity yourself and hand people a problem they can solve.
Fix chain links in sequenceTake on bottlenecks one at a time and absorb the cost.
Weed the gardenBuild a hump chart. Units that hide cross-subsidies are a sign of entropy.
Write your judgments downCommit before results arrive, then compare.
