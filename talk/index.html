TALK

The science of conversation and the art of being ourselves

Alison Wood Brooks treats everyday talk as a skill with a research base. You can't script it, but you can prepare for it, get better at it and repair it when it goes wrong.

Introduction

We do it all the time, and we're amateurs

Brooks's toddler seemed to say "love you" for the first time and kissed her nose. Brooks welled up, walked down the hall, then realised the word was "lick". The child had licked her. Most misreadings are less funny. Years earlier Brooks told a close friend over soup dumplings that she had doubts about the boyfriend. The talk seemed to go well. Two days later a photo of an engagement ring arrived.

Brooks's claim is that conversation is one of the hardest things people do. Two things make it hard. The context keeps shifting: who is there, what is on the table, whether someone just dimmed the lights. And our purposes are many, often hidden, and often in conflict with each other and with our partner's. Every turn is a run of tiny choices that two or more minds have to coordinate without discussing them.

We also get almost no feedback. Nobody tells you which joke broke the ice or which topic change stung. Brooks had an unusual advantage: an identical twin, Sarah, whom she could watch make nearly the same choices from close range, and who told her bluntly when she got them wrong.

She went on to run a lab at Harvard Business School that records and transcribes real conversations at scale, including speed dates, parole hearings, sales calls and doctor visits. The book grew out of her course, TALK: How to Talk Gooder in Business and Life, designed in 2019.

Chapter 1

The game

Conversation used to be a high art with written rules. Now it's closer to jazz: you learn the patterns and improvise with whoever turns up.

01Coordination

Every turn is a guess about another mind

Think of talk as a coordination game: each player chooses without being able to ask, and the outcome depends on everyone's choices.

  • From salons to strangers. In the eighteenth-century Age of Conversation, talk meant elevated exchange among the cultivated. Kant's dinners had rules: usually five to eight guests, a menu of topics running from news to serious matters to jesting, and no know-it-alls. As cities filled up, Adam Smith saw strangers having to work out the rules as they went.
  • Schelling's focal points. Asked where to meet a stranger in New York without any contact, a surprising share of Thomas Schelling's subjects picked Grand Central's information booth at noon. Conversation is the same puzzle repeated relentlessly, a stream of what Brooks calls micro-decisions.
  • Words do things. J. L. Austin showed that speech isn't only description. We request, promise and apologise with it. So you always have at least one purpose, and so does your partner. Finding them takes self-reading, mind-reading and room-reading.
  • Grice's rules, and why we break them. Paul Grice argued that talk rests on cooperation and proposed four maxims: be truthful, concise, relevant and clear. Real talk breaks all of them, often for good reasons. Filler words such as um warn a partner that you're uncertain, and white lies can be kind.
  • Words became data. Erving Goffman eavesdropped in casinos and asylums. Conversation analysts typed up tapes by hand. Automatic transcription and language processing now let researchers count words across thousands of people and link them to outcomes. One result: men and women are equally talkative, at about sixteen thousand words a day.

The conversational compass

Every quadrant holds legitimate goals. The dashed line shows the point of the map: the farther apart two goals sit, the harder they are to pursue together. Try persuading a friend to babysit while also laughing as much as possible. Your partner has a compass too, and any turn can reset it. Chapters 2–5

The maxims

Topics and Asking steer you toward good ground. Levity keeps everyone engaged while you're there. Kindness makes sure people feel heard and are heard. In practice each one feeds the others.

TChapter 2 · Topics

Good topics are made, not found

Thirty seconds of preparation beats talent, and the skill lies in managing a topic more than in choosing one.

  • You can't rate a topic in advance. With Mike Yeomans, Brooks had a thousand people rank fifty topics by expected enjoyment. "When did you last cry in front of another person?" came second to last, yet people often enjoyed discussing it. Topics are co-created, so breakfast can be dull with one uncle and wonderful with another.
  • The Myth of Naturalness. We see a smooth talker and assume it's effortless. So 53% of people call topic prep unnecessary, 34% expect it to feel forced, and 50% think it will reduce enjoyment. Only 12% expect it to help.
  • Why prep works. In the moment, instinctive system 1 thinking grabs whatever is nearest: the rain, the appetiser, your own news. Writing topics down beforehand offloads the work. In Brooks's studies, even thirty seconds of forethought meant shorter pauses and fewer stumbles, especially between topics. In class, conversations with prepped topics rated far higher, and by the end over 90% of students ranked prep as a top-three takeaway.
  • Small talk is a doorway. Hugo and Josie, four-minute speed daters, spent eight turns on the weather and both rated the date terribly. Brooks blames the failure to move on, not the small talk. Concrete questions and personal disclosures move a conversation up, since disclosure tends to draw disclosure back.
  • Switch more than feels polite. Pairs told to get through twelve topics in ten minutes covered nine, against five for pairs left to their natural pace. They gave their talks 6 out of 7 for enjoyment, against 5, and lost no depth. In 1,600 long online conversations, 20% felt they had covered too few topics and 11% too many.
  • Three signs the juice has run out. Longer shared pauses, more polite laughter, and people repeating themselves. Mirror questions are weak evidence of interest because they're often reflex. A partner calling back to a topic is strong evidence.

AChapter 3 · Asking

Be interested, not interesting

Questions move the pieces, and the danger in most conversations is asking too few.

  • Askers are better liked. Strangers in fifteen-minute chats averaged 6.5 questions. When Brooks's team told some to ask more than nine and others fewer than four, the high askers were liked significantly more. On speed dates, one extra question per date across twenty dates won one more second date.
  • Nobody notices the link. High askers didn't think they were liked more, and outside readers of the transcripts missed the connection too. That's why the habit doesn't correct itself. Matchmaker Rachel Greenwald calls the worst cases ZQs, for Zero Questions.
  • Sensitive questions are safer than we fear. In five experiments with over 1,400 people, researchers found no evidence that asking about salary or affairs did more harm than benign questions, among friends or strangers. Risk rises with broken trust, live conflict, power gaps and audiences. Brooks was fine being asked about pregnancy quietly by a friend, and not having it shouted across a room of thirty colleagues.
  • Follow-ups do the work. In her analysis of 398 strangers, four types emerged: introductory, mirror, topic-switching and follow-up. Follow-ups drove nearly all of the likability gain, since they show you were listening. Mirror questions added none. Follow-ups also drift, so they double as stealth topic switches.
  • Borrow from the masters. Gross tries one or two follow-ups, then jumps if there's no treasure. Oprah Winfrey asks about feeling. Barbara Walters moved people through past, present and future.
  • Is there a limit? In speed dates and stranger chats, Brooks's team couldn't find one. In sales calls analysed by Gong, more than twenty questions per ten minutes did worse than fifteen to twenty, though still better than fewer than ten.
  • Open beats closed for connection. Open-ended questions draw twice as many words. In negotiation role-plays, instructing people to ask more of them produced more information, more liking and more trades. Don't spoil them by offering candidate answers.

LChapter 4 · Levity

Find the fun, don't try to be funny

Levity is any move that adds positive energy. It doesn't need a joke, and most laughter isn't about jokes anyway.

  • Tom and Cassie. Out of a Stanford dataset of eleven hundred four-minute dates, one pair stands out. They joked about being bad at speed dating and turned her home state into a guessing game. None of it was very funny. Both wanted a second date.
  • It helps with everything else. Andrew Oswald's team found happier people 12% more productive. Teresa Amabile linked happiness to creativity. Leslie John found people 1.9 times more likely to reveal personal information on a whimsical website than on a serious one. Levity signals that it's safe to play, and safety lets people take risks.
  • We use it less with age. 84% of 23-year-olds report smiling and laughing a lot the day before. That falls to 68% at 50 and 61% around 80.
  • The fear is overblown. Brooks, Schweitzer and Bitterly found that people overestimate how often humour goes badly. Managers assigned to make one joke were over 9% more likely to be voted into leadership. Even flopped jokes raised perceived confidence. Naming the flop is a fine recovery.
  • Aim up, not at people. Affiliative humour raises psychological safety, and aggressive humour lowers it. Self-deprecation worked for Ric Keller, who likened himself to Elizabeth Taylor's seventh husband. Being too benign costs you a laugh. Being too cruel can cost a relationship.
  • Moves anyone can try. Fuss over a tiny detail, as Seinfeld does. Compare unlike things. Call back to old jokes: two friends still ask whether events come with a "welcome beverage", after a spam offer for a million-dollar holiday. Keep a stable of stories, retiring any that nobody co-narrates. Get weird: Mike once asked a dull table which body part they'd remove from the homunculus.
  • Compliments work even when suspect. Flattery sold more in an experiment where readers knew it was an advert. Students sent to compliment strangers underestimated how good it felt for the receiver. Praise what people did rather than how they look.

Levity's job on the chart of emotions

Boredom is the quiet threat: fewer glances, longer pauses, flatter words. Levity lifts a conversation diagonally out of it.

What people regret

Asked for ideal traits in a partner, people may not even mention fun. Asked how their favourite people make them feel, they always say happy. Jennifer Aaker reports that the most common deathbed regret she heard was not spending more time laughing with loved ones.

KChapter 5 · Kindness

Put their needs first, and show you heard

Kindness means working out what a partner needs and helping them get it. Your own egocentrism is what stands in the way.

  • A model conversation. In 2019, two months after his mother's death, Anderson Cooper sat down with Stephen Colbert to talk about grief. They asked fourteen questions in twenty minutes and laughed every few minutes. Then Colbert gently disagreed with Cooper's wish for a visible scar, and answered a hard question with one word: yes. Brooks locates the conversation's power in each man's focus on the other.
  • The illusion of understanding. Piaget thought children outgrow extreme egocentrism around seven. Boaz Keysar's experiments suggest adults barely do. People even believed they'd caught tone in a language they didn't speak.
  • Respect has a grammar. Stanford researchers coded over 36,000 officer utterances from 981 Oakland traffic stops. Brooks draws three rules from it. Make people feel seen and known, good to be with, and worthy of care. The study's central finding, she reports, was that officers used more respectful language with white drivers than with Black drivers.
  • Rudeness is catching. Trevor Foulk's work shows negative micro-behaviours spreading like a cold, even to bystanders, though a civil encounter can overwrite the effect. An executive once asked Brooks whether her class was worth his time, and she was furious. Months later she learned he was seriously ill and rationing his energy. Hence: give the benefit of the doubt.
  • Our minds wander. Brooks's team found people reporting 76% attention and 24% wandering in chats with strangers, likely an underestimate. Only a third of participants noticed a nonsense sentence dropped into a real conversation. Attention did rise when people were paid for it.
  • Responsive beats active. Nods and smiles can be faked. Words can't: follow-ups, paraphrases, credit, call-backs. Generic mm-hmms even made storytellers worse in Janet Bavelas's work. Off-topic interruptions are rude, but on-topic ones signal engagement, as when Colbert and Cooper finish each other's sentences.

Chapters 6–8

The tests

After a half-chapter pause to breathe, the book turns to what stretches the maxims: more minds, real differences, and harm that needs repair.

06Many Minds

Groups are a different beast

More people means more possibility and more chaos. Someone has to watch status and take care of the group.

  • Join the pair. Raj, a student with party panic, tried walking up to two people talking. They were glad of it. Groups give everyone breathing room, since you don't have to speak at every turn.
  • Hidden hierarchies. Every group ranks its members without saying so, and the order can shift by topic. High-status talkers take more airtime, which raises their status further. Larger groups tend to concentrate the talking in fewer people.
  • Gender as a case study. Victoria Brescoll found that female US senators speak less than male ones, whatever their power, and that the fear of backlash behind this was accurate. Observers of both sexes judged outspoken women more harshly. Brooks argues that the higher-status members should help, starting by talking less.
  • Pulling in from above. As a graduate student, Brooks was being boxed out of a conversation on a stoop until an eminent professor told the newcomer that Alison grew up sailing and did cool research. Status tends to reduce perspective-taking, so it takes effort. High-status members can credit others by name, admit failures, and share eye contact evenly.
  • Moving up from below. Adam Galinsky's phrase is that status determines your range: low-status members have fewer acceptable moves. The workable ones are small. Ask a question, where ignorance is an asset. Paraphrase. Nudge a topic. Compliment. Smile.
  • Be a steward. The term is from Lucid Meetings founder Elise Keith. At a Wigilia supper for forty-two loud relatives near Detroit, tiny Great Busia stood, raised a piece of bread, and sent everyone into quiet two-minute pairs for exchanging wishes. Many wept. Then the rumpus resumed.

Why groups get hard so fast

07Difficult Moments

Aim to learn, not to win

Disagreement doesn't have to hurt. Find the layer where the difference lies, stay receptive, and you may persuade more by trying less.

  • The dreaded exercise. Late in the course, students pair up on the hot-button issue where they're furthest apart. They arrive filled with dread. Around 40% then name it their favourite of more than twenty exercises.
  • The receptiveness recipe. Julia Minson, Mike Yeomans and Hanne Collins studied the language of civil disagreement across hundreds of thousands of conversations. Receptive speakers saw fewer blow-ups and persuaded more. Partners tend to match it.
  • The learning mindset. We underestimate how much others are willing to learn, then treat them accordingly, and conflict follows. Letting go of persuasion lets beliefs update. In Brooks's example, brothers arguing over their mother's care each find out what the other has quietly paid for and fears.
  • Identity is the hot core. In 2013, new to teaching, Brooks showed a photo of Tom Brady and joked that the "ladies" should enjoy the view. Three LGBTQ+ students came to see her about it. She was stung, thought it over, and apologised. Their approach, and her response, left the class closer.
  • Get perspective, don't take it. Across 25 studies by Eyal, Steffel and Epley, trying to imagine another's view never helped accuracy. Asking did. The Golden Rule misleads here, as Brooks sees it, because people differ.
  • Don't avoid it. Brooks cites Richeson and Shelton's finding that would-be allies are often the most avoidant. Yet Kiara Sanchez found that talking about race brought Black and white friends closer six months on, and Broockman and Kalla found that ten-minute talks with transgender canvassers in Florida reduced transphobia.

Where a difference lives

Moving down, differences get harder to see and slower to shift. Tiny surface remarks can shoot straight to the core. The first job in a hard moment is working out which layer you're on.

Cooling down: go across, not diagonal

Brooks's research finds it easier to relabel anxiety as excitement than to calm down, because your heart can keep racing. You change the label, not the body.

Other ways to lower the heat

  • Change the setting. Stand, walk outside, move from email to a call.
  • Name it. Labelling a partner's feeling can build trust. A colleague of Brooks asks whether a class should hit refresh.
  • Find the source. People who knew why they felt something regulated it better and were happier, in a study of over five thousand momentary reports.
  • Leave. Sometimes only time helps. A row over a word-game clue at a campfire split one family for years.
The drama nobody could hear

Emma and her sister Samantha had barely spoken in two years. For an assignment she called during Samantha's drive. A scornful remark about her outdoors club stung, but instead of defending herself Emma agreed, then asked about Samantha's travel plans. A transcript would look dull. By the end Samantha had a graduation invitation, and she accepted it.

08Apologies

Don't make it about you

Apologising is nearly always better than not. A good apology shows you see the harm, and a promise to change points to what comes next.

  • It's never about the papers. On Couples Therapy, Tashira and Dru fight over a pile of paperwork. Her clipped apology makes things worse, and his missing apology about housework has its own cost. Small moments stand for unmet needs. Their therapist, Orna Guralnik, says apologies signal two things: that you see through your partner's eyes, and that you accept responsibility.
  • What the evidence says. Brooks writes that no study of conversations has found not apologising to be better. Those who apologise end up with higher status. When hospitals allowed or required doctors to apologise, litigation became less likely.
  • Blame needn't be yours. On rainy days, a research assistant asked strangers at a Philadelphia station to lend him a phone. A superfluous apology for the rain took compliance from 9% to 47%. Karina Schumann found that frequent apologisers didn't devalue their apologies. Only low-quality ones hurt.
  • The cardinal sin. BP's Tony Hayward called the Deepwater Horizon spill a "disruption", after a blowout that killed eleven workers. Then he added the line below. Good apologies stay on the recipient's experience. Tashira's complaint about the laundry is the small version, known in the Brooks house as snowballing. Asking for forgiveness also tips the focus toward you.
  • Sooner is better, and late still counts. You can acknowledge someone's pain before you know who's at fault. Brooks's son Kevin broke her nose as a toddler and apologised, unprompted, at seven, after reading Diary of a Wimpy Kid.
  • Feedback is part of repair. Only 3% of people told a survey-taker about food or lipstick on her face. Be swift and private. State your good intent. Start positive, then separate the critical part clearly. Look ahead rather than back.

Epilogue

You're better at this than you think

The goal is variety and small improvements, not constant greatness.

Visiting a friend who had been in chemotherapy, Brooks found she'd made a topic list labelled with Whitney Houston song titles. Some topics went stale, and the sadness was hard to voice. It was still one of the best conversations of Brooks's life.

That points to a third pillar of well-being. Shigehiro Oishi and Erin Westgate add psychological richness to happiness and meaning. A good life includes variety, bitter parts too. Brooks's own research finds that talking with a wider mix of people makes us happier.

Then a surprise from Christopher Welker and colleagues in 2023. We usually rate ourselves above average at things. Conversation shows a below-average effect. We credit others with the Myth of Naturalness and ourselves with what Brooks calls the Myth of Suckiness.

She closes by admitting the TALK maxims aren't really maxims. They're reminders, and sometimes the best talk comes from breaking them on purpose. She asks for small things: one more follow-up, one prepared topic, one laugh, one call-back. Erving Goffman, she notes, credited conversation as the spark that "lights up the world".
