Attached

Are you Anxious, Avoidant or Secure? How the science of adult attachment can help you find, and keep, love
Amir Levine & Rachel Heller

A psychiatrist (Levine, Columbia) and a psychologist (Heller) translate decades of research on adult attachment into a practical dating and relationship guide. We are wired, as babies are, to depend on a few special people, and we differ in how we handle that need: secure, anxious or avoidant. Knowing your style and your partner's explains baffling behaviour, shows whom to date, and gives concrete skills for communicating and fighting the secure way.

Tarcher, 2010; new edition 2019 · 12 chapters in four parts + epilogue · ~60,000 words

Chapter 1 · Decoding relationship behaviour

Tamara and Greg

Love conquers all? The authors' friend Tamara fell for Greg: handsome, flattering, full of promises (“You can come and work over at my place”). He also said he'd never had a stable relationship and always tired of girlfriends. Soon his work week was “crazy”, the signals got mixed, and she became anxious, cancelled plans with friends in case he called, and her work suffered. She knew she'd be happier without him but couldn't leave. Attachment research described both of them “down to the last detail”: Greg pushed her away because they were getting closer, not because she wasn't good enough.

Secure · just over 50%

Comfortable with intimacy, usually warm and loving. Not overly worried about the relationship or about rejection.

Anxious · about 20%

Crave intimacy, are preoccupied with the relationship, and worry that the partner won't love them back.

Avoidant · about 25%

Equate intimacy with a loss of independence and keep trying to minimise closeness.

The remaining 3–5% combine anxiety and avoidance. Styles differ in how people see intimacy, fight, approach sex, voice needs and what they expect of a partner. They come partly from childhood, partly from genes, and partly from adult relationships: on average about one in four people change style over four years, mostly without noticing. None of them is pathological; each was an evolutionary bet. In danger, avoiding attachment or clinging hard both made sense, while in peaceful conditions secure investment paid off. Tamara later met Tom, who was secure, and the drama simply stopped.

John Bowlby's insight: evolution programmed us with an attachment system that keeps us close to a few precious people. When the bond feels threatened, it fires protest behaviour, the infant's frantic search for its mother. Imagine news of a plane crash on the night your partner is flying to London: the sinking feeling is your attachment system, and the frantic calls to the airline are protest behaviour.

Chapter 2 · Dependency is not a bad word

The dependency paradox

Karen and Tim. On a reality race show, Karen needed Tim to hold her hand under pressure; Tim found it too close. After they lost at the finish line, Karen blamed herself: “I was too needy.” Neither mentioned that Tim had nearly quit at the bungee jump, which cost them the lead. Had he held her hand early, he'd have spent less time “putting out fires”, and with her support he might have jumped sooner.
Most people are only as needy as their unmet needs.Chapter 2

Only decades ago, experts warned against “too much mother love” (John B. Watson, 1928): leave babies to cry, feed them on schedule, let hospitalised children see their parents only through glass. Ainsworth and Bowlby showed that a bond is as vital as food. Today, popular advice and the language of “codependency” make the same mistake about adults: happiness should come from within, keep the focus on yourself, set boundaries, needing your partner is addiction.

One physiological unit

Partners regulate each other's blood pressure, heart rate, breathing and hormones. In James Coan's fMRI study, married women awaiting a mild shock showed strong stress activity alone, less while holding a stranger's hand, and barely any while holding their husband's, especially in the happiest marriages. “Dependency is a fact; it is not a choice or a preference.”

A secure base

In Mary Ainsworth's “strange situation”, one-year-old Kimmy explores a room of toys while her mother is there, falls apart when she leaves, and returns to play once she's back. Adults work the same way: partners who supported each other's goals in Brooke Feeney's lab felt better and rated their goals as more achievable. In an unhappy marriage, your partner's presence even raises your blood pressure.
If you want to take the road to independence and happiness, find the right person to depend on and travel down it with that person.The book's one-sentence summary of attachment theory

Chapter 3 · What is my attachment style?

Two dimensions

The book's 42-item true/false quiz is adapted from the research questionnaire ECR-R (Fraley, Waller & Brennan). Underneath, every style is a position on two axes: how comfortable you are with closeness, and how anxious you are about your partner's love.

Anxious baby

Extremely distressed when mother leaves; on reunion happy and angry, slow to calm, pushes her away.

Secure baby

Visibly upset at the separation; eager greeting, quickly soothed, back to play.

Avoidant baby

Acts as if nothing happened and ignores mother on return, yet its heart rate and cortisol are as high as the crying babies'.

Five golden rules

  1. Does s/he seek intimacy and closeness? The master question. No means avoidant; yes means secure or anxious. Personality doesn't tell you; a cocky person can crave closeness.
  2. How preoccupied with the relationship, how sensitive to rejection? High on both suggests anxious.
  3. Don't rely on one symptom. Look for a coherent pattern. (Not introducing you to her children is sensible at first; after two years, ask what it means.)
  4. Watch the reaction to effective communication. Secure partners accommodate; anxious ones open up; avoidant ones say “You're too sensitive”, “Stop analyzing everything!”, “Geez, I said I was sorry.”
  5. Notice what is not said or done. At midnight on New Year's Eve Rob said he hoped it was “the first of many new years for us together”. She kissed him and said nothing. Two months later they split.

The workshop cases repay study: Paul, 37, is still looking for the woman whose smile he can already “see”, and dropped Amanda for suspected cosmetic surgery. That is avoidant: an idealised phantom, blaming circumstances, devaluing someone as closeness grows. Anxious people blame themselves for being alone; secure people are realistic; avoidant people blame circumstances. Logan, who has dated only three people and said “I love you” early without keeping score, is secure.

Chapter 5 · The anxious style

Living with a sixth sense for danger

Emily. She was a psychiatry resident so composed that her analyst expected her training analysis to finish in record time. Then she met David, an aspiring actor full of mixed signals. Soon she was checking her pager and phone every few minutes while running around the reservoir, and had invented an online persona to chat him up. Her analyst diagnosed “masochistic borderline personality traits”. It was simply a constantly activated attachment system. Once she married a reliable man, she was her old resilient self again.

Anxious people really do notice more. In “morph movie” studies, they spotted a face's change of emotion earlier than others did. But when they reacted instantly they misread it; forced to wait a little, they outperformed everyone. Lesson: wait before you act, and your sensitivity becomes a gift. Brain scans show why calming down is hard: thinking of loss lights up emotional areas more, while regulating areas (the orbitofrontal cortex) are under-recruited.

Activating strategies

(thoughts that push you toward the partner) can't concentrate on anything else · remember only their good qualities · put them on a pedestal and run yourself down · anxiety that eases only on contact · “I'll never find anyone else” · “He can change”, “All couples have problems”, “If she leaves me, she'll turn into a great partner, for someone else.”

Protest behaviour

(actions to win attention back) calling and texting over and over · withdrawing, back turned · keeping score (“If she's not answering, I won't leave a message”) · eye-rolling hostility · threatening to leave, hoping to be stopped · acting busy · making them jealous.
Ryan and Shauna. On his first business trip, Ryan's call went to voicemail after two rings (she pressed “ignore”), then straight to voicemail. He couldn't concentrate. An hour later Shauna, who is secure, texted that her boss had been standing next to her. One text settled it. A little reassurance given early saves a great deal of firefighting later.

Why anxious people end up with avoidant partners

  • Familiar scripts: each confirms the other's beliefs (Pietromonaco & Carnelley; anxious women do date avoidant men more often).
  • The roller coaster: mixed signals activate you, a compliment brings elation, then doubt returns. Over time you learn to equate an activated attachment system with passion.
  • The law of large numbers: avoidants break up more, get over it quickly and re-enter the dating pool; secures pair off and leave it; avoidants rarely date each other (one study found no avoidant–avoidant couples at all). So a new date is far more likely to be avoidant than their 25% share suggests.
An activated attachment system is not passionate love… True love, in the evolutionary sense, means peace of mind.Chapter 5

So Chloe found secure, handsome, funny Trevor “missing the spark”, married Tony, who put her down constantly, and needed years and a divorce to learn to value calm. And common dating advice (play hard to get, act busy, never call first) does make you more attractive, but to avoidants, because it lets them set the distance.

Coaching for the anxious single

  1. Accept your needs as legitimate. Ask “Can this person give me what I need?”, not “How do I change to please them?”.
  2. Rule out avoidants early by watching for “smoking guns”: mixed messages; longing for an ideal partner who is clearly not you; always finding fault with “the one”; disregarding your feelings even when told; calling you “too needy”; ignoring what's inconvenient; answering you “as in a court of law”; your messages never getting through.
  3. Be authentic and communicate. When Brian came back after leaving, Janet again let him set the pace, and it slowly fizzled out without even a break-up talk. Saying plainly “I need to know I can talk to you every day, not just when it's convenient” would have settled it either way.
  4. The abundance philosophy. Date several people. Nicky, 31, told friends to look out for her and joined dating sites. She saw that many people liked her, moved on fast from avoidant behaviour, and within a year met George and had “the happiest, most secure relationship” of all her friends.
  5. Give secure people a chance. Calm may feel like boredom at first. Wait.

Chapter 6 · The avoidant style

Keeping love at arm's length

The lonesome traveller: Chris McCandless in Into the Wild turned down an adoption, a girl's love and a family's home, and told the man who gave him his last lift he could handle anything alone. Trapped by a swollen river, he died in the Alaskan bush. In his journal: “Happiness only real when shared.” Avoidance may have helped our species survive hostile times, but it doesn't make individuals happy; avoidant people report lower satisfaction in relationships.

The need is suppressed, not missing. In word-recognition studies, avoidants were quick to see “need” and “enmeshed” and slow to see “separation” and “loss”. Give them a distracting puzzle at the same time and they recognised the loss words as fast as anyone. Under heavy stress (divorce, a disabled child, combat) their defences break down and they look anxious.

Deactivating strategies

“I'm not ready to commit” (for years) · fixating on small flaws (how they talk, dress, eat) · the phantom ex · flirting · never saying “I love you” · pulling away after a great date · choosing impossible partners · mentally checking out · secrets and fog · avoiding physical closeness (separate bed, walking ahead).

Thought patterns

Self-reliance mistaken for independence: Joe's father's mantra was “You can only count on yourself!” His therapist: “That's nonsense… you do it all the time anyway.” Seeing the worm, not the apple: Carole left tender, patient Bob for his clothes and lack of sophistication, then mourned him as the best she'd ever had. Not reading signals, and seeing that as not their job.
“Stop, please don't leave.” John Gray (Men Are from Mars) came home to find his wife in pain all day with a newborn, her painkillers run out. He got defensive and was about to storm out when she said: “This is when I need you most.” He stayed and held her: “That day, for the first time, I didn't leave her.” Her well-being became his responsibility, a step toward a secure working model.

The phantom ex and “the one”. At a safe distance, admiration for an ex returns and the reasons for leaving fade, so every new partner looks small by comparison. With someone new, a sudden chill after the early high (“the way she eats”) feels like proof they're not the one; it is a deactivating strategy.

Chapter 7 · The secure style

The supermates of evolution

Secure people are not necessarily the most charming. They come in every personality: introverted Aaron, social-hub Brenda, divorced Gregory. What they share is invisible: they expect love and responsiveness, aren't worried about losing it, and so stay calm. In word-recognition studies, threatening words simply didn't register with them, even under distraction; when asked to think about loss they got stressed, and the stress vanished as soon as they stopped.

BufferCouples with even one secure partner functioned as well as couples where both were secure, and better than insecure pairs.
4 monthsSecure students kept their satisfaction, commitment and trust; insecure ones declined on all three (Keelan, Dion & Dion).
70–75%of adults keep the same style over time; the other 25–30% change, in either direction.
Stan's gift. Overwhelmed by work, Janet woke up and attacked her husband's business prospects. Stan answered without animosity: “I understand that you're frightened… but if you're trying to encourage me to be more efficient at work… this isn't the best way to do it.” Then he drove her to work. A counter-attack would have started World War III.

Be a secure base (Feeney & Thrush)

Be available (respond to distress, check in) · don't interfere (support behind the scenes, don't micromanage) · encourage (accept their growth, boost their self-esteem).

Choosing the secure way

Tanya, 28: the two men who waited two days to call were screened out immediately, and she didn't blame herself. Treat smoking guns as deal breakers, state needs from day one, believe there are many good partners, never take the blame for a date's bad behaviour, and expect respect.

But secure people aren't immune. Nathan endured eight years of Shelly's tantrums, a slap and affairs, believing it was a “rough patch”, because secure people forgive and feel responsible. Warning sign: if you, though secure, start feeling jealous, holding back or playing games, you're probably with the wrong person. “Just because you can get along with anyone doesn't mean you have to.”

Chapter 8 · The anxious–avoidant trap

Are we really fighting about this?

The washing machine. Mark has wanted one for two years; Janet refuses. She does the laundry at her sister's, around the block, and stays all day. The fight is about closeness.
The B&B in Vermont. Paul wants the twin beds for the view; Susan the queen bed. He never cuddles before sleep, and she knows he'd retreat to his own bed after sex.
Facebook exes. Kevin keeps exes as friends and screens Naomi's calls when out with his pals, then tells her she has “abandonment issues”.
the roller-coaster effectan emotional counterbalance (the avoidant feels strong only while the partner feels needy)stable instabilityfights about triviathe inner circle as the enemyfeeling it's wrong but too attached to leave

Why it rarely resolves: the differences spread to every new stage of life (marriage, children, money, illness). Resolving a conflict would create closeness, so the avoidant grows more hostile as the argument goes on. And the anxious partner loses ground in every clash: after a fight they're flooded with good memories and reach out, while the avoidant remembers only the worst. The anxious partner ends up pleading to get back to the same unsatisfying status quo.

Chapter 9 · Escaping the trap

Stable but plastic

Styles can shift. Being with a secure partner helps most, but even security priming works: recalling secure people and moments. Build an integrated secure role model: a manager who turned a disagreement into dialogue, a friend who backed his wife's switch from law to social work despite the pay cut, even the way you treat your pet (no grudges, a warm welcome home).

The relationship inventory

List every partner, then work down each column, one column at a time: what you remember → situations that activated or deactivated you → what you did, thought and felt → the attachment principle at play (protest behaviour? deactivation?) → how it hurt you → what your secure role model would do. Each time you recall a memory you rewrite it a little, so the exercise can reshape your working model. An “attachment-designated person” (a friend or therapist) can check you when you're activated.
The text that saved the day. Georgia's calls frustrated Henry, a busy doctor, and his silence frightened her. Solution: whenever he thought of her, he'd send a pre-written “thinking of you” text. A second of his time, and her worry was gone.
The toothpaste incident. Sam exploded when Grace squeezed the toothpaste from the middle; his flat felt invaded. She sublet a friend's studio for six months as a buffer, barely used it, and they adjusted.

If security isn't reached, knowledge still helps: the fights are a built-in clash, not your failing, and the avoidant partner would withdraw from anyone. Some people make peace by letting go of the dream: Doug greets his late-coming wife warmly instead of with anger, Natalie makes weekend plans of her own, Janis no longer expects Larry to help with the children. The authors are frank that this compromise is one-sided. It makes sense in a committed relationship you want to keep; early on, with many intimacy collisions, think hard before signing up.

Chapter 10 · Breaking up

When abnormal becomes the norm

Marsha and Craig. Craig ignored her at his track practice (“they don't need to know we're a couple”) and was surprised she'd stopped seeing her ex (“this might not lead to anything!”). He compared her to his perfect ex, belittled her intelligence and body (“You're what there is right now”), and introduced her as his “‘genius’ girlfriend”. On their Paris honeymoon he swore at her over a wrong metro line. She cried daily for years, threatened to leave, and was talked back each time, until he finally kept his promise and said “Okay!”. The $10,000 lost on their apartment was “the best money I ever spent.” Marsha came from a loving family and went on to an adoring partner. She was simply anxious, and he avoidant.

Sex can be another tool of distance: avoidants have less of it, especially with anxious partners, and may set rules such as “no kissing”. Leaving is so hard because breakups activate the same brain regions as physical pain, and because of the rebound effect. Myron Hofer's separated rat pups could be helped symptom by symptom with warmth, food and stroking, but only the mother's return fixed everything at once. One person can remove all the pain in a second.

Surviving a breakup

  1. Ask: am I treated like royalty or like the enemy?
  2. Build a support network beforehand, and stop hiding how things really are.
  3. Stay somewhere comforting for the first nights.
  4. Meet attachment needs in other ways: friends, massage, exercise, good food.
  5. If you slip back to “the scene of the crime”, be compassionate with yourself; self-loathing reactivates the system.
  6. The pain is real; treat yourself as you would a broken leg.
  7. When flooded with good memories, ask a friend for a reality check.
  8. Deactivate on purpose: write down every reason you wanted to leave, and reread it.
  9. Know that it will pass.
Part four

The secure way

Effective communication, and five principles for fighting well.

Chapter 11 · Effective communication

Say what you need, and watch the response

After three romantic but untouched dates, Lauren asked Ethan: “I'm looking for more than something platonic. What is it that you have in mind?” He dodged, and she knew there was no future (he turned out to be unsure of his sexual orientation). Tina simply asked shy Serge on their third date, “Can I have a kiss?”; three years later they're still together. Jena, at 40, told first dates she wanted children soon; most ran, Nate found it refreshing, and they now have two. Either way, the response tells you more in five minutes than months of guessing.

Judging the response

Does s/he try to get to the bottom of your concern, or dodge it? Take it seriously or belittle it? Try to make you feel better, or just defend? Answer only the facts “as in a court of law”, or also your feelings?

When to use it

Anxious: the moment you're about to protest (silence, threats), but only once you've calmed down, which may take a day or two. Avoidant: when you feel the urge to bolt; ask for space, say it's your need in any relationship, and offer options that also look after your partner.

The five principles

  1. Wear your heart on your sleeve. Be emotionally brave and honest.
  2. Focus on your needs, with words like need, feel, want: “I need to feel confident in the relationship. When you chat up the waitress, I feel like I'm on thin ice.”
  3. Be specific: “When you don't stay the night…”
  4. Don't blame. Never make your partner feel selfish or inadequate, and talk when you're calm.
  5. Be assertive and non-apologetic. Your needs are valid, full stop.

Chapter 12 · Working things out

Five secure principles for conflict

All couples fight. What matters is how they fight and what about: everyday “bread-and-butter” disputes (which channel, Chinese or Indian) can build closeness, while intimacy-centred ones are the trap. Secure people's knack can be learned:

  1. Show basic concern for the other's well-being. Frank loves the family cottage in the Berkshires, Sandy hates the packing and the traffic. Each gives way when the other is struggling, because it isn't a zero-sum game.
  2. Keep to the problem at hand. Kelly assumed George hid another woman when he wouldn't invite her up; the next night he flung the door open: “Welcome, welcome, welcome!” It really was just a mess.
  3. Don't generalise. Terry and Alex have fought over the wrong pasta brand for 30 years, but never escalate to “I've had it with you”. (Better: she calls him at the store, or orders online.)
  4. Be willing to engage, physically and emotionally present, instead of digging in or walking away.
  5. Communicate feelings and needs. Not “You're selfish” but “I know you hate going to my sister's, but it would mean the world to me this once. I've hardly seen you all week.”

Biology helps: oxytocin, the “cuddle hormone”, boosts trust and cooperation, so the Sunday-morning cuddle you skip for work may be your best protection against conflict. In the middle of a fight, remember: a single fight doesn't end a relationship; voice your fear instead of acting it out; your partner's mood probably isn't about you; they can't read your mind; and when in doubt, ask. Expecting the worst becomes a self-fulfilling prophecy. Sometimes all it takes is a sincere apology, like Shannon's when she forgot to greet the sister-in-law who had babysat: “Did I really not even say hello? I'm so sorry.”

Epilogue

Don't leave love to chance

Even the authors fell for a film's free-spirited heroine who strings the hero along and then marries “the one”. Three myths were at work: that everyone has the same capacity for intimacy; that marriage transforms people; and that only you are responsible for your emotional needs. Seen through attachment, it's simply avoidant meets anxious, and the hero probably became her phantom ex.

A relationship, from an attachment perspective, should make you feel more self-confident and give you peace of mind. If it doesn't, this is a wake-up call!Epilogue

Pocket version

What to keep

  1. Needing your partner is biology, not weakness: the dependency paradox makes you more independent.
  2. Know the three styles (secure, anxious, avoidant), the two dimensions behind them, and your own place.
  3. The master question about anyone: do they seek intimacy and closeness?
  4. An activated attachment system is not love. Peace of mind is.
  5. Anxious? Wait before reacting, avoid protest behaviour, date several people, and give secure ones time.
  6. Avoidant? Spot your deactivating strategies, drop the phantom ex and “the one”, and practise gratitude.
  7. Watch for smoking guns, and for becoming “the enemy” in someone's inner circle.
  8. Use effective communication: honest, specific, need-focused, blame-free, unapologetic. Judge the response.
  9. Fight securely: care for their well-being, stay on topic, don't generalise, stay engaged, say what you need.
  10. Secure partners are the supermates; choosing one is not settling.
