The big idea

Reason before you react

The book opens with two men who probably saved the world. In 1983 Soviet officer Stanislav Petrov saw his early-warning system report five incoming American missiles. A real first strike would have been an all-out barrage, not five missiles, and ground radar showed nothing. He judged it a malfunction and didn't pass it up the chain. He was right. In 1962 Vasili Arkhipov, on a Soviet submarine overheating under US depth charges, refused to approve a nuclear torpedo. Both were under extreme stress, and both stopped to think.

The opposite story is Mao's campaign against sparrows. Millions of people killed the birds to protect grain. China's leading ornithologist warned that sparrows mostly ate insects, and he was sent to hard labour. Without them locusts took the crops, and the famine of 1959–61 killed somewhere between 15 and 45 million people. Grimes calls this the politician's syllogism: something must be done; this is something; therefore this must be done.

His case is that our minds are powerful but full of trapdoors, and the information age has made them easier to fall through. Rationality isn't the same as intelligence. Clever people suffer from "dysrationalia" as often as anyone. But it can be trained. The book is a field guide, built from stories rather than theory, to six kinds of failure, and the method that guards against them.

The motto Grimes keeps returning to

There's no shame in being wrong. The only shame is refusing to correct yourself. The aim isn't perfect thinking, just being a little less wrong, a little more often.

Part 1 · Chapters 1–3

Without reason

Formal fallacies: arguments whose structure is broken, so the conclusion doesn't follow even if every premise is true."He, who will not reason, is a bigot; he, who cannot, is a fool; and he, who dares not, is a slave." William Drummond

01An Indecent Proposition

An argument can be valid and still be nonsense

For a conclusion to be trustworthy, the logic has to be valid and the premises have to be true. Most bad arguments fail one test while passing the other.

valid vs. soundaffirming the consequentheuristics
  • In January 897 Pope Stephen VI put his dead predecessor Formosus on trial, the corpse propped up in papal robes. Formosus didn't defend himself, so he was found guilty. The "logic" rested on a premise nobody should accept: that an innocent man would always speak up.
  • Heuristics, our fast rules of thumb, are fine for spotting a snake in the grass. They're poor tools for geopolitics or health care. Those need slow, deliberate reasoning.
  • Affirming the consequent (the converse error) turns "X implies Y" into "Y implies X". All men are mortal, Socrates was mortal, so Socrates was a man. The conclusion is true, but the argument would equally prove that Socrates was a dog.
  • Adverts run on a hidden converse error: successful, attractive people own this car, so owning the car makes you one of them.
  • Conspiracy theories use it as armour. "If there's a cover-up, official reports will deny it. The reports deny it. So there's a cover-up." Every debunking becomes evidence. About 15% of Americans still believe 9/11 was an inside job.

The story

As a 17-year-old student protesting the Iraq war, Grimes was half-persuaded by a classmate's 9/11 theories. What saved him was context. His father, a structural engineer, explained that steel doesn't need to melt to fail, because heat weakens it long before it melts. And having grown up in Saudi Arabia, he had seen the religious extremism that produced the hijackers. Later he published a model showing that large conspiracies leak quickly. Within hours, people emailed to say the paper proved he was part of the conspiracy.

02Stripped to the Absurd

Contradictions are alarms, and we're good at ignoring them

If a claim leads to an absurd result, the claim is wrong. That's reductio ad absurdum, and it's one of the sharpest tools in reasoning.

reductio ad absurdumundistributed middleaffirming the disjunctpositive conclusion from negative premises
  • The Pythagoreans worshipped whole-number ratios. One of their own, Hippasus, assumed √2 could be written as a fraction and showed that this leads to a contradiction. So √2 is irrational. Legend says he was drowned for it.
  • Mobile phones, Wi-Fi and 5G use low-energy, non-ionising radio waves. Only high-energy (ionising) radiation, such as UV or X-rays, can break chemical bonds and damage DNA. The WHO and FDA find no evidence that phones cause cancer.
  • The scare rests on the undistributed middle: all radio waves are electromagnetic radiation; some electromagnetic radiation causes cancer; therefore radio waves cause cancer. The middle term only covers "some", so nothing follows. By the same logic, Jimi Hendrix was an ancient Greek philosopher, because both are dead.
  • The BioInitiative Report that fed these fears was never peer reviewed. When a 2017 paper in a respectable journal cited it, Grimes and a colleague told the journal, and Grimes notes that one of a scientist's most useful jobs is helping reporters kill bad stories.
  • Affirming the disjunct: "Either you're wrong or I'm wrong. You're wrong. So I'm right." Both could be wrong.
  • Positive conclusion from negative premises: "I don't do that; bad people do that; so I'm good." It's the logic of the online mob, which feels more virtuous the harder it attacks.

The story

In 2012 Lindsey Stone, a well-liked care worker for adults with learning disabilities, kept up a running joke with friends of doing the opposite of what signs said. At Arlington National Cemetery she posed pretending to shout beneath a sign asking for silence and respect. Stripped of context, the photo went viral. She lost her job, received death and rape threats, and didn't leave home for a long time. Her tormentors felt more righteous the louder they shouted, then moved on to the next target.

03It Does Not Follow

The plural of anecdote is not data

Stories are memorable precisely because they're unusual. That makes them the worst possible guide to what usually happens.

anecdotal fallacybase-rate neglectsurvivorship biascherry-pickingfallacy fallacy
  • Testimonials sell. Pfizer's 2006 Lipitor adverts starred Robert Jarvik, billed as inventor of the artificial heart. He hadn't taken the drug before becoming its spokesman. In 2017, a London restaurant called The Shed at Dulwich became TripAdvisor's top-rated in the city. It didn't exist.
  • Original "snake oil" became a byword when Clark Stanley's best-selling version was tested in 1916 and found to contain no snake at all.
  • Every formal fallacy is a non sequitur: the conclusion doesn't follow. Rising cancer rates don't mean the world is more toxic. People live long enough to get cancer, which is mainly a disease of age.
  • Cherry-picking is the engine of the anecdotal fallacy. In a 1997 test, self-described psychics did no better than students at describing crimes from objects, and neither beat chance.
  • Psychics survive on remembered hits and forgotten misses, plus the "rainbow ruse": "You're usually upbeat, but there have been times you were very sad." That fits everyone.
  • A key warning: an argument containing a fallacy doesn't make its conclusion false. Assuming it does is itself a fallacy. Good conclusions can come wrapped in bad reasoning.

Where the bullet holes aren't

In the Second World War, analysts mapped damage on returning bombers and planned to armour the most-hit areas. Statistician Abraham Wald pointed out that planes hit in the engines or cockpit never returned to be counted. The clean areas were the ones to protect. Diagram illustrative, not data.

04The Devil in the Details

Experts, single causes and either-or choices

We crave simple stories with one cause and two sides. Reality rarely offers either, and demagogues know it.

argument from authorityreductive causefalse dilemma
  • Linus Pauling won two Nobel Prizes, then spent decades promoting vitamin C megadoses (he took up to 18,000 mg a day) against colds and cancer. The evidence never backed him, yet the myth lives on. Expertise in one field doesn't carry over to another.
  • The argument from authority cuts both ways. Semmelweis's hand-washing findings in 1840s Vienna were resisted by senior doctors. Grimes adds nuance: Semmelweis wasn't the first to suggest washing in lime, and he wrongly claimed that all such disease came from "cadaveric particles". His story shows the next trap too.
  • The reductive cause fallacy blames one thing for a complex outcome. After 1918, German generals refused to accept the many reasons for defeat. Ludendorff seized on a British general's phrase: they had been "stabbed in the back". The myth fed the Nazis' scapegoating of Jews, with catastrophic results.
  • The false dilemma squeezes a spectrum into two extremes: "you're either with us or against us". It turns up across the political spectrum, from Lenin in 1920 to Arthur Miller's witch-hunting judge in The Crucible.

A false dilemma hides the middle

The appeal is comfort. A simple binary feels like understanding, and it forces the listener to pick a side. That's why it suits demagogues and poisons compromise.

05Smoke without Fire

"After it" doesn't mean "because of it"

Our pattern-hungry minds link anything that happens in sequence. Sometimes the link is real. Often it's coincidence, and the cost of mistaking one for the other can be measured in lives.

post hoc ergo propter hocavailability heuristicSystem 1 vs. System 2
  • B.F. Skinner fed pigeons at random intervals. They developed rituals (spinning, head-bobbing) as if their dance caused the food. Superstition is the post hoc fallacy at work.
  • For two thousand years doctors blamed malaria on swampy "bad air", hence the name. The real cause, mosquito-borne parasites, was only found in the 1880s.
  • In 1998 Andrew Wakefield published a study of 12 children and then called a press conference suggesting the MMR vaccine was linked to autism. Journalist Brian Deer later showed the data was falsified. The Lancet retracted the paper and Wakefield was struck off.
  • The damage outlasted him. Each measles case infects 12 to 18 others. The US went from nearly measles-free to 677 cases in 2014. In 2019 the WHO named vaccine hesitancy a top-ten global health threat. In Samoa, coverage fell to 31% after a medical error; a measles outbreak followed.
  • Why did parents believe it? The availability heuristic: vaccines had made measles deaths invisible, while autism diagnoses were all around them.
  • Kahneman's fast System 1 jumps to answers; slow System 2 checks them. A bat and ball cost $110, and the bat costs $100 more than the ball. Most people say $10; the answer is $5. More than half of students at Harvard, MIT and Princeton got it wrong.

Why the timing fooled people

Schematic timeline, not to scale. The fallacy needs only the sequence. The evidence needs a comparison group.

06The Nature of the Beast

Essences that don't exist

Essentialism, the idea that groups and things have a fixed inner nature, works for triangles. Applied to people, it underpins racism and lets us dismiss arguments by attacking whoever makes them.

essentialismNo True Scotsmanappeal to naturead hominemtu quoquefundamental attribution error
  • "Race" doesn't carve humans at real joints. In the 1800s the Irish were considered a separate race. Light skin spread across Europe only within the last several thousand years. Genetically, all living humans are close relatives.
  • IQ gaps track nutrition, health and schooling, not ancestry. Kenya's average IQ rose 26 points between 1984 and 1998. Jewish soldiers scored poorly on First World War tests and above average a generation later.
  • No True Scotsman: "No Scotsman would do such a thing." "This one did." "Then he's no true Scotsman." It protects an idealised group from evidence, and turns up as "un-American", "un-Christian" and so on.
  • Appeal to nature: natural doesn't mean good (arsenic is natural) and unnatural doesn't mean bad.
  • Ad hominem attacks the speaker instead of the argument. Galileo's Dialogue made the geocentrist "Simplicio", read as "simpleton", and the Pope took it personally. Galileo spent his last years under house arrest. Tu quoque ("you too") is a variant: a smoker who warns you off cigarettes is a hypocrite, but still right.
  • The root is the fundamental attribution error: we blame people's character for what is often their circumstances.
Hamish reads:  "Brighton sex maniac strikes again."
Hamish says:   "No Scotsman would do such a thing."
Next day:      The culprit is from Aberdeen.
Hamish says:   "No true Scotsman would do such a thing."

07Bait and Switch

Beat the dummy, claim the win

A straw man swaps the real argument for a flimsy stand-in that's easy to knock down. Circular reasoning hides the conclusion inside the premises.

straw mancircular reasoningbegging the question
  • Richard Owen, a brilliant but vindictive anatomist, attacked Darwin by implying he claimed humans descended from modern apes. Darwin had argued for a common ancestor. At the 1860 Oxford debate, Bishop Wilberforce asked Huxley whether he was descended from an ape on his grandmother's or grandfather's side. Huxley answered that he'd rather have an ape for a grandfather than a man who used his gifts to obscure the truth.
  • The straw men never stopped. In 2007 two evangelists said evolution must be false because nobody had found a "crocoduck". A 2001 Louisiana resolution condemned "Darwinist ideology" as racist.
  • Straw men also run the other way, overselling a cause. Grimes was caught up in Ireland's cannabis bill, pitched with emotive stories as medicine for conditions where there was no evidence, including cancer. When a parliamentary committee rejected it, its backers cried sabotage. The Irish Cancer Society and Grimes were accused of being pharma shills.
  • Cannabis has real but limited medical uses, such as some forms of epilepsy and nausea. Cancer patients have died after dropping treatment for cannabis oil.
  • Circular reasoning: "The book is true because the book says so." In begging the question the conclusion is assumed in the premises. (Grimes's pet peeve: it doesn't mean "raises the question".)
What Darwin argued
  • Random variation, some of it heritable
  • Better-suited traits spread over generations
  • Humans and apes share a distant common ancestor
What critics knocked down
  • "Humans came from monkeys"
  • "Where's the croco-duck?"
  • "Evolution says some races are superior"

08Schrödinger's Bin Laden

We reason toward what we already believe

Motivated reasoning isn't a lack of information. It's identity at work: we protect the beliefs that bind us to our group, even when that takes bad maths.

motivated reasoningconfirmation biascognitive dissonanceidentity-protective cognition
  • Trofim Lysenko promised Stalin miracle harvests. His ideas suited communist ideology, so they became law. Genetics was banned as "bourgeois" in 1948. His mentor Vavilov died in prison. Lysenkoism wrecked Soviet biology and fed famines, including in China.
  • In 1954 psychologist Leon Festinger joined a cult awaiting rescue by flying saucer before a world-ending flood. When midnight passed with nothing, the group didn't give up. They announced their faith had saved the Earth and began recruiting. Festinger called the discomfort behind this cognitive dissonance.
  • Climate denial tracks ideology, especially strong free-market views, more than scientific knowledge. Accepting the science seems to demand regulation. The left has its own blind spots, which Grimes covers in ch. 21.
  • Conspiracy believers can hold contradictory beliefs at once. The more people believed Princess Diana faked her death, the more they also believed she was murdered. Likewise, the more people believed Osama bin Laden was already dead before the raid on his compound, the more they believed he was still alive. That's the chapter title: a Schrödinger's Bin Laden, both dead and alive.

Used the cream: rash improved (223 of 298)75%

In Dan Kahan's 2013 Yale study, most people saw "223 improved" and said the cream worked. Comparing ratios shows it didn't. 59% got it wrong. Then the same table was relabelled as gun control and crime. Numerate liberals and conservatives suddenly got it right only when the answer suited their politics. Maths ability stopped predicting who got it right.

When a prophecy fails, belief can grow

  1. Deep conviction that shapes what you do
  2. Commitment: costly actions that are hard to undo (quitting jobs, giving away possessions)
  3. A specific, testable belief about the real world
  4. Clear disconfirmation that the believer sees
  5. Social support from fellow believers

Festinger's conditions for doubling down. With all five in place, people often proselytise harder after the failure. "Tell him you disagree and he turns away. Show him facts and he questions your sources."

09The Memory Remains

Memory is a reconstruction, not a recording

Every time we recall something we rebuild it, and suggestion, stress and other people's stories can slip in. A confident memory can be completely false.

confabulationfalse memoriesconformity of memorysuggestive questioning
  • Grimes once remembered a dramatic event from 2007 in vivid detail, then found in his own diary that it happened to a friend. Oliver Sacks reported the same experience.
  • Elizabeth Loftus showed that false memories can be planted. A doctored photo of a childhood hot-air balloon ride led many subjects to "remember" the ride in detail. Hearing another witness's account changes what we recall ourselves. Trained soldiers under mock interrogation stress misidentified their interrogator.
  • The 1980 book Michelle Remembers, based on hundreds of hours of hypnosis, described satanic ritual abuse that the evidence contradicted. It helped trigger the "satanic panic".
  • In the McMartin preschool case, interviewers told children their friends had already disclosed abuse and rejected their denials. The trial took seven years, cost over $15 million and ended with no convictions. Some of the children later recanted.
  • False memories aren't lies. The person believes them. That's what makes them dangerous in courts and in therapy.

10Daggers of the Mind

Seeing, hearing and feeling what isn't there

Perception is active guesswork. A brain built to find patterns will find them in static, clouds and rocks on Mars, and our expectations can move our muscles without our knowing.

apopheniapareidoliafeeling of presencesleep paralysisideomotor effect
  • Apophenia is seeing meaning in randomness: ghost voices in radio static, satanic messages in records played backwards. In 1990 Judas Priest were taken to court over the suicides of two young fans. The supposed hidden message was found only after hunting through and amplifying dozens of snippets.
  • Pareidolia is its visual cousin: the 1976 "face on Mars" was an ordinary mesa in better photos, and holy faces appear in toast.
  • Ghostly "presences" come from the brain failing to merge conflicting signals. Shackleton's party felt a "third man" walking with them; exhausted pilots report the same. Sleep paralysis, waking while the body is still paralysed for dreaming, likely explains the incubus legends.
  • The ideomotor effect: tiny unconscious movements. Faraday showed in the 1850s that sitters, not spirits, moved séance tables. The same effect moves Ouija planchettes and dowsing rods. In 2013 a British businessman was convicted for selling useless dowsing-style "bomb detectors" to Iraq.
  • The tragic version is facilitated communication, where a helper guides a disabled person's hand to a keyboard. When the helper and the person were shown different pictures, the "answers" matched what the facilitator saw.
11Great Expectations

What we expect, we tend to experience

Belief changes both perception and the body. Vague statements feel personal, sugar pills feel effective, harmless signals feel harmful, and the least informed feel the most certain.

Forer (Barnum) effectplacebonoceboDunning–Kruger
  • In 1948 Bertram Forer gave every student the same "personal" profile, stitched together from horoscopes. They rated it highly accurate. "You have a great deal of unused capacity which you have not turned to your advantage." The trick powers astrology and some personality tests.
  • In a 2001 stock-picking contest, a financial astrologer, an investor and a five-year-old each got £5,000. The child, picking at random, beat both. The astrologer lost most.
  • The placebo effect (Latin for "I shall please") explains some of alternative medicine's appeal. Much of the rest is regression to the mean: people seek treatment at their worst and then get better anyway.
  • The nocebo effect works in reverse. People with "electromagnetic hypersensitivity" suffer real symptoms, but in blinded tests they react to sham sources and not to real ones they don't know about.

Lies, damned lies, and statistics

Numbers look objective, but they need context. Statistician Frederick Mosteller: it's easy to lie with statistics, but it's easier to lie without them."Politicians use statistics in the same way that a drunk uses lampposts: for support rather than illumination." Andrew Lang

12Chance Encounters

Probability is where intuition fails hardest

How likely something is depends on how common it was to begin with. Ignore the base rate and an accurate test can mislead, and an expert can put an innocent mother in jail.

Monty Hallgambler's fallacyBayes's theoremprosecutor's fallacyfalse independence
  • In 1990 Marilyn vos Savant said that in the Monty Hall problem you should switch doors. Thousands of readers, many with PhDs, told her she was wrong. She was right.
  • The numbers 1-2-3-4-5-6 are as likely in a lottery as any other set. After 20 heads in a row, the next flip is still 50/50 (though you might question the coin).
  • A 99.99% accurate HIV test, given to a low-risk person, means only a 50% chance they actually have HIV. The same test in a high-risk group is nearly certain. The difference is the base rate.
  • DNA works the same way. A profile shared by 1 in a million people is strong evidence against a suspect found by other means. Search a database of 10 million and you expect about ten innocent matches.

Why switching wins

13Sifting the Signal

Correlation is a clue, not a verdict

Hidden variables can make data say the opposite of the truth. Finding real causes takes detective work, and screening everyone for everything produces a flood of false alarms.

Simpson's paradoxconfounding variablescorrelation ≠ causationsensitivity & specificity
  • In 1973 Berkeley was sued because it admitted 44% of male applicants and only 35% of female ones. Department by department, there was a small bias in favour of women. Women had applied in larger numbers to the most competitive departments. This is Simpson's paradox, driven by a lurking variable.
  • Spurious correlations are everywhere. A parody chart links the decline of pirates to global warming. Correlation is still useful as a starting clue that something needs explaining.
  • London's cholera fell when cesspools were closed, which seemed to prove the "bad air" theory. In 1854 John Snow mapped deaths around the Broad Street pump. Local monks and brewery workers, who drank beer, were spared. The pump handle was removed. The well turned out to sit about 1 m from a cesspit. Epidemiology was born.
  • Theranos was valued at $9 billion on the promise of dozens of tests from one drop of blood. The machine didn't work. Even if it had, testing healthy people for everything with no reason to suspect disease guarantees false positives. A short talk with a statistician would have shown that.
Sensitivity
  • Of people who have the condition, how many does the test catch?
  • Low sensitivity → missed cases (false negatives)
Specificity
  • Of people who don't have it, how many does the test correctly clear?
  • Low specificity → false alarms (false positives), multiplied across a whole population
14Size Matters

Ask "how much?", not just "is there a link?"

A risk that sounds frightening as a percentage can be tiny in real terms. And "statistically significant" doesn't mean big, important or even real.

relative vs. absolute riskeffect sizep-valuesregression to the meanpublication bias
  • In October 2015 the WHO's cancer agency put processed meat in the same category as tobacco. Headlines screamed that bacon was as bad as smoking. The category measures how sure the evidence is, not how big the risk is.
  • Drug firms prefer relative numbers. Heart attacks dropping from 5 to 4 per 1,000 is a "20% reduction", or an absolute 0.1%.
  • A house that loses 50% one year and gains 50% the next hasn't broken even. It's worth 75% of what it was.
  • A p-value below 0.05 was Ronald Fisher's rough flag for "worth a closer look". Merged with rival frameworks, it hardened into a pass mark. Run enough comparisons and some will pass by chance.
  • "Publish or perish" pressure and journals' taste for positive results push the literature toward false findings. Meta-analyses, which weigh all studies by quality, are the safeguard.
  • Natural frequencies help: "10 in 100 people get this side effect" is understood far better than "a 10% chance".

Bacon in absolute terms

56 cases among lightest eaters +10 among heaviest eaters

Relative risk: 10 ÷ 56 ≈ 18% higher, the figure in the press release. Absolute risk: 10 more cases per 1,000 people, 1 percentage point. Both are true. Only one tells you what it means for you.

Source: UK lifetime bowel cancer figures as reported in ch. 14

Warning signs a finding may be false

  1. Small studies. Small samples aren't representative and throw up more false positives.
  2. Small effects. A tiny effect is easily produced by bias or noise.
  3. Many relationships tested without a prior reason. Some will hit by chance.
  4. Flexible designs and definitions. Room to adjust lets a negative result become positive.
  5. Money or prejudice riding on the answer.
  6. A hot field with many teams racing for headline results.

John Ioannidis's six corollaries from his paper on why most published research findings are false. Grimes has worked with Ioannidis on modelling "publish or perish" pressure.

Part 5 · Chapters 15–18

News of the world

How the media, and the media we build for ourselves, amplify bad thinking."Newspapers are unable, seemingly, to discriminate between a bicycle accident and the collapse of civilization." George Bernard Shaw

15Skewing the Balance

Two sides aren't always equal

Balance should match the evidence. Giving a fringe view equal airtime with an overwhelming consensus makes a settled question look open, and that doubt can be manufactured on purpose.

false balancemanufactured controversyconsensus gap
  • In 2016 news outlets treated Trump and Clinton as comparable candidates. Constant falsehoods were weighed against an email-server story as if they were equivalent.
  • Grimes was asked by an Irish radio station to debate Andrew Wakefield. He refused and urged them not to give him a platform. Putting science and pseudoscience side by side signals that the matter is disputed.
  • Lung cancer was under 1% of cancers in 1878, 10% by 1918 and 14% by 1927. German doctor Fritz Lickint linked it to smoking by 1929. When the evidence became overwhelming in the 1950s, the tobacco firms hired PR firm Hill+Knowlton. An internal memo summed up the strategy: "Doubt is our product."
  • Creationists used the same playbook with "teach the controversy", playing on two meanings of "theory". Climate denial is the biggest example. From 1988 to 2002, most articles in four major US papers gave deniers roughly equal space, leaving a wide gap between scientific consensus and public belief.

Anatomy of the tobacco industry's 1954 "Frank Statement"

What it said
  • Research shows many possible causes of lung cancer
  • Authorities disagree about the cause
  • There's no proof smoking is one of them
  • The statistics could apply to any part of modern life
The trick behind each line
  • Straw man: frame it as "only mice studies"
  • Many causes don't rule out a major one
  • Shift the burden of proof to an impossible standard
  • Ignore that confounders had already been ruled out

Published in hundreds of US newspapers on 4 January 1954. It sounded reasonable, and it bought the industry decades.

16Tales from the Echo Chamber

A huge sample can still be the wrong sample

When we only hear from people like us, the world looks the way we already think it is. Algorithms speed this up, but the urge is ours.

sampling biasfilter bubbleecho chambercyberbalkanisation
  • In 1936 the Literary Digest polled 10 million Americans and got 2.4 million replies. It predicted a Landon win, 57% to 43%. Roosevelt won every state except Vermont and Maine. The sample came from its own readers, phone books and car registrations: the well-off of the Depression. George Gallup, with a far smaller but representative sample, got it right and even predicted the Digest's error.
  • Feed algorithms flatter us with what we already like. But we also curate our own bubbles. Around 40% of users have unfriended or blocked someone over politics. MIT researchers predicted this "cyberbalkanisation" in 1996.
  • 66% of millennials get their news mainly from social media, where unchecked rumour sits beside reporting and the loudest voices dominate.
  • AIDS denialism shows the cost. Biologist Peter Duesberg claimed HIV didn't cause AIDS. The London denialist magazine Continuum rejected HIV drugs. By 2001 its entire editorial staff had died of AIDS-related illnesses. South African president Thabo Mbeki appointed denialists to a panel and delayed antiretroviral treatment, with a huge death toll.

17The Outrage Machine

Check where it came from, and who gains

Some misinformation is honest error. Some is made for clicks, and some is made by intelligence services. The defence is the same: check the source before you share.

conspiracy echo chambers"just asking questions"hyper-partisan mediadezinformatsiya
  • After 20 children and six staff were murdered at Sandy Hook in 2012, "truthers" claimed it was staged to take their guns. Normal reporting errors became "proof". Bereaved parents were stalked and threatened. Lenny Pozner, whose son Noah was killed, set up HONR to fight back and spent years getting harassing material removed.
  • Wild accusations dressed up as questions are a known bad-faith tactic, nicknamed "JAQing off".
  • Online "Targeted Individuals" groups convince people with paranoid delusions that they're being gang-stalked, keeping them from treatment, sometimes with tragic results.
  • Hyper-partisan pages on both sides, some run by the same owners, earn money from outrage. In 2016 Russian troll operations posed as Americans across the political spectrum.
  • The Soviet KGB's "active measures" planted stories to erode trust in the West: that water fluoridation was a plot, and, in Operation INFEKTION, that the US created AIDS. The story first appeared in 1983 in a pro-Soviet Indian newspaper and was relaunched in 1985 with a pseudoscientific report. Versions still circulate, and similar claims surfaced about COVID-19.
Nullius in verba: take nobody's word for it.
Motto of the Royal Society, which Grimes offers as the rule for anything you read before you pass it on
18Bad Influencers

Fame is not expertise

Celebrities and wellness gurus shape health decisions more than doctors do. The same reach can save lives when it's pointed at the truth.

appeal to celebritywellness pseudosciencepseudo-profound bullshitmere exposure
  • Australian wellness star Belle Gibson said she cured her brain cancer with diet and alternative medicine. Her app, The Whole Pantry, shipped with the Apple Watch. In 2015 it emerged that none of the charities she'd raised money in the name of had been paid, and that she'd never had cancer at all. The media that lifted her up had never asked for evidence.
  • Sites like Natural News and Joseph Mercola's pull millions of visitors, selling supplements alongside anti-vaccine and "Big Pharma" conspiracies.
  • Oprah gave platforms to anti-vaccine campaigner Jenny McCarthy and to The Secret's "law of attraction". Kim Tinkham, who went on the show to praise her positive-thinking approach to breast cancer instead of the treatment doctors urged, died of the disease. Dr. Oz has promoted products with no evidence. Goop sold a $66 jade egg for women to insert, a product that gynaecologist Jen Gunter publicly criticised.
  • David "Avocado" Wolfe tells his 12 million Facebook followers things like "chocolate is an octave of sun energy". Grimes uses the academic term for this kind of grand-sounding word salad: pseudo-profound bullshit.
  • Merely seeing a claim repeatedly makes it feel truer, so media can't hide behind "just reporting what people say".

The candle in the dark

What science is and what it isn't. Not a body of facts or a priesthood, but a method for catching our own mistakes."The first principle is that you must not fool yourself, and you are the easiest person to fool." Richard Feynman

19The Edge of Science

Trust the method, not the person

A scientific claim has to stick its neck out: make a prediction that could prove it wrong. Authority counts for nothing when the evidence disagrees, even if you're Lord Kelvin.

pathological scienceOccam's razorfalsifiabilityburden of proof
  • In 1988 Nature published Jacques Benveniste's claim that water "remembers" antibodies diluted out of it, which would support homeopathy. Editor John Maddox sent a team, including magician James Randi, to watch a repeat. Once the samples were blinded, the effect disappeared. A 30C homeopathic dilution would need a planet of water far bigger than the sun to contain one molecule of the original substance.
  • That's pathological science: wishful thinking in a lab coat. Occam's razor (prefer the explanation with fewest extra assumptions) would have pointed to error.
  • Kelvin calculated Earth was some tens of millions of years old, too young for evolution and geology. His former assistant John Perry found the flaw in 1895: Kelvin had assumed a solid Earth, but convection inside the planet carries heat far more efficiently. Perry put the age at 2 to 3 billion years or more. Radioactivity later added another heat source Kelvin couldn't have known about. The age is now known to be billions of years. Kelvin's prestige didn't make him right.
  • Karl Popper saw that Marxism and some psychoanalysis could explain any outcome, while Einstein's theory made risky predictions that the 1919 eclipse could have refuted. Falsifiability divides science from pseudoscience. Astrology is too vague ever to be wrong.

Seven checks for spotting pseudoscience

  1. Quality of evidence. Data and clear methods, or anecdotes and testimonials?
  2. Authority. Accepted because of the evidence, or because of who says it?
  3. Logic. Does every link in the chain hold? Beware single causes and single cures.
  4. Testable claims. Could it be shown wrong? Has anyone independent reproduced it?
  5. Totality of evidence. Does it fit everything we know, or only the cherry-picked parts?
  6. Occam's razor. Does it need a pile of extra assumptions to survive?
  7. Burden of proof. The person making the claim must support it. "You can't prove it's false" doesn't count.
20Rise of the Cargo Cult

The costume of science without the substance

Jargon, charts and lab coats borrow science's authority. What they leave out is the bending-over-backwards honesty about how you might be wrong.

cargo-cult sciencequantum quackerypseudo-forensics
  • After the Second World War, some Pacific islanders built bamboo control towers and runways, copying the rituals that had once brought planes full of cargo. On Tanna, believers still await "John Frum". Feynman used the image for research that copies science's forms but skips its integrity.
  • Science-flavoured scares about aspartame and water fluoridation travel well online. When Irish politicians brought anti-fluoride motions in 2013 quoting scientific-sounding reports, Grimes wrote and debated against them and became a target of abuse.
  • Quantum mysticism borrows the vocabulary of physics that only applies at subatomic scales to sell healing and self-help.
  • Kirk Odom was convicted of a 1981 rape largely on an FBI agent's testimony that a crime-scene hair was a "very rare" exact match. He spent 22 years in prison and nine on parole before DNA retesting cleared him in July 2012, on his 50th birthday. A 2009 National Academy of Sciences report found microscopic hair comparison lacked scientific basis. A joint review then found flawed hair testimony throughout FBI cases.
Science
  • Hunts for its own errors
  • Reports what didn't work
  • States its uncertainty
  • Invites replication
Cargo cult

21A Healthy Skepticism

Scepticism isn't doubting everything

It's asking "why do we think that?" and following the evidence, including where your own side doesn't want to go.

cancer-cure conspiraciesselective framingscientific skepticism
  • In 2012 seven-year-old Neon Roberts's mother took him and disappeared to avoid the radiotherapy he urgently needed after brain surgery. A court ordered treatment, and he recovered.
  • About 37% of Americans believe the FDA hides natural cancer cures. The claim fails on all three of its premises. "Cancer" is hundreds of different diseases, not one with a single cure. A cure would be enormously profitable, and natural compounds are patented all the time. And cancer has been with us since ancient Egypt.
  • The "dihydrogen monoxide" spoof lists only true facts about water, framed to sound terrifying. Selective framing does the work.
  • The left has blind spots too. The Chernobyl Forum counted 28 deaths from acute radiation sickness and 15 from thyroid cancer, and found no rise in overall cancer rates or birth defects. Far higher figures circulate in campaigning. Meanwhile fossil fuel pollution kills about 5.5 million people a year. After Fukushima, Germany shut nuclear plants and burned more coal.

Epilogue · "Love truth, but pardon error." Voltaire

Facts aren't enough. People change their own minds.

In 1870 naturalist Alfred Russel Wallace accepted a 500-pound bet from flat-Earther John Hampden and proved the Earth's curvature along a six-mile stretch of canal. Hampden refused to accept it, harassed Wallace for years and never paid. Wallace learned that you can't argue some people out of a position they didn't reason their way into.

The bigger story is personal. In 2015 an anti-vaccine campaign and a fear-mongering TV documentary scared Irish parents away from the HPV vaccine, which prevents several cancers. Uptake fell from 86.9% to about 50%. What turned it round wasn't more statistics. It was Laura Brennan, a young woman dying of cervical cancer, telling her story publicly alongside health officials. Uptake rose again. Laura died in 2019, aged 26. Grimes counted her as a close friend.

We feel first and think second, so the facts need a human voice. Most people aren't zealots; the loudest commenters are a tiny fraction. Debate rewards the best performer, not the best evidence. And mocking people never changed a mind.

Practical takeaways

The toolkit

Grimes insists the book is a collection of stories, not a textbook. These are the habits the stories teach.

Check the argument

Test validity and truth separatelyDoes the conclusion follow? Are the premises true? You need both.
Watch for one-way arrows"All X are Y" never means "all Y are X". "Some" never tells you which.
Count the missesFor every impressive hit or survivor, ask about the ones you didn't hear about.
Find the hidden third optionWhen offered two choices, look for the spectrum in between.
Reply to the real argumentRestate the other side well enough that they'd agree before you answer it.

Check yourself

Notice when a conclusion feels goodThat's when motivated reasoning is most likely. Apply your toughest standards to what you want to believe.
Hold memory looselyVivid and confident isn't the same as accurate. Look for records.
Let System 2 check System 1For anything that matters, slow down and work it out, as with the bat and ball.
Be suspicious of your own certaintyFeeling expert is easiest when you know least.
Admit errors fastYour identity isn't your opinions. Being less wrong is the goal.

Check the information

Ask for the base rateHow common was it to begin with? Then think in counts: "out of 1,000 people…"
Ask for absolute risk"18% higher" may mean 1 extra case in 100.
Look for the confounderBefore accepting a correlation, ask what else could drive both.
Weigh balance by evidenceTwo speakers doesn't mean two equal sides.
Trace it before you share itRead past the headline. Who made it, who profits, and can it be verified?
