The cry of the heart for intimate belonging

The big idea

Everyone carries an impostor

Manning was, in his own words, ambushed by Jesus in a small chapel in Pennsylvania in 1956. Nearly forty years later he writes about the seasons since: fire and boredom, faithfulness and betrayal, and the way the first passion can quietly drain out of a life.

His diagnosis is a split inside every person. The impostor, or false self, is the polished version we present so that everyone admires us and nobody really knows us. The true self is Abba's child: loved as we are, not as we should be.

The impostor isn't evil so much as frightened. It was born to protect us from pain. Manning's own appeared when he was eight, after a trauma that shut down his feelings for decades. The cure isn't to beat the impostor up, but to bring him, again and again, into the presence of Jesus, where he slowly shrinks.

The book mixes psychology (codependency, the inner child) with the Gospels and a wide circle of voices: Merton, Nouwen, Buechner, Julian of Norwich, Flannery O'Connor and Woody Allen.

Two selves

Manning's own summary of the false self: a compulsion to present a perfect image so that everybody admires us and nobody knows us. The true self is defined by one thing only: being loved by God.

Part one · Chapters 1–2

Come out of hiding

Since Adam and Eve, we hide whenever we don't like what we see in ourselves. Manning's first call is simply to stop: to let God see us as we are, because he already does.

1Come Out of Hiding

A faith that rises and falls with performance

If God's love seems to depend on how well we did today, we will hide on the bad days. That's the opposite of what he wants.

The story · Ruller's turkey

In a Flannery O'Connor short story, a lonely boy named Ruller catches a wounded turkey. Suddenly he feels chosen and generous: he thanks God, prays for a beggar to give his only dime to, and feels like a future preacher. Then some country boys take the turkey from him, and his warm feeling about God collapses. Manning sees himself in it: our sense of God swings with what we can show for ourselves.

  • God is the father who runs toward the limping prodigal. He grieves when shame and self-hatred freeze us. Yet the moment we lose our nerve, we take cover.
  • Self-acceptance isn't vanity. Manning quotes Carl Jung: what if the least of the brothers whom I must love turns out to be me? Most of us are far harder on ourselves than on anyone else.
  • Self-hatred tends to turn destructive. Judas couldn't face his shadow; Peter could. Peter made friends with the impostor inside him; Judas raged against his.
  • Julian of Norwich: God doesn't want his servants to despair because they fall often. The goal is to be reconciled to "the poor shell of me", as the curate writes at the end of Bernanos' Diary of a Country Priest.
2The Impostor

The people-pleaser who wears a thousand masks

The false self lives in fear of disapproval. It's the most-discussed chapter of the book, and Manning says the impostor keeps returning in new disguises.

  • Zelig. In Woody Allen's film, Leonard Zelig changes personality to fit every room: rabbi with the rabbis, psychiatrist with the psychiatrists, Yankee at spring training. Famous for being nobody. Manning finds much of Zelig in himself.
  • Fear underneath. Manning was proud of always being punctual. In the cabin's silence he discovered it came from fear of being scolded, echoes of childhood authority.
  • Can't say no. Impostors overcommit to people, projects and causes, not from conviction but from fear of disappointing expectations.
  • Codependent by nature. To be accepted, the false self hides feelings, so emotional honesty becomes impossible. Life becomes a roller coaster of elation and depression depending on others' reactions.
  • Even in ministry. A sketch of a celebrity athlete-evangelist moving a crowd to tears, while his eyes show the words no longer live in him. Success can quietly replace intimacy with God.

How the impostor was born

Age 8A trauma. The inner voice says: don't be your real self, nobody likes you as you are. Invent someone admirable.
ChildhoodHe becomes the good boy: polite, top grades, a scholarship, and constant fear of abandonment.
AdulthoodPerfect performance earns approval; feelings stay switched off.
1992Twenty days in a Colorado cabin, therapy each morning, silence all day. He realises he hasn't felt anything since he was eight.
The letter

The chapter ends with a letter Manning writes to his impostor. Not an attack, but a friendly note: you protected me once, now come with me into Jesus' presence. There you'll need less applause, because you'll discover he is enough. He gives the impostor a pet name, a little nickname for someone who is shrinking.

Part two · Chapters 3–5

Define yourself as beloved

Manning's core sentence, paraphrased: let being loved by God be your whole identity. Every other identity is an illusion.

3The Beloved

Holiness looks ordinary

The true self is unselfconscious: absorbed in life, breathing God the way a fish swims in water.

  • A travel writer watches a student unpack an elaborate Bible-reading kit at breakfast: stand, clips, coloured pens, wet wipes. It's comic, but Manning reads it kindly: spirituality isn't a compartment of life but a way of living all of it.
  • Thomas Merton came to think the highest spiritual development is to become "ordinary", fully human without the distortions of greed or ambition.
  • John Eagan, a high-school teacher in Milwaukee for thirty years, never wrote a book or became famous. His journal, published after his death, shows a flawed man who learned that his sins couldn't keep him from God. The introduction's point: our false humility is often the main obstacle.
  • Joyless Christianity repels people. Buechner: the good news that we are loved is better than we ever dared hope.
4Abba's Child

The name that scandalised Israel

Jesus called God Abba, the intimate word a child uses for a father. And he doesn't keep that intimacy to himself.

  • Jesus grew up praying the Shema three times a day, surrounded by God's absolute otherness. Yet he addressed God with a word no prophet had dared. The scholar Joachim Jeremias called it the great novelty of the gospel.
  • Romans 8: we didn't receive a spirit of slavery that falls back into fear, but the Spirit of adoption that cries "Abba". And 1 John 3:1: see what love the Father has given us, that we should be called children of God.
  • After a parish renewal week in Florida, an 83-year-old woman wrote to him: last week she was terrified of dying; now she was homesick for her Abba's house.
  • The Abba experience makes us tender toward others. In New Orleans, Manning and a friend were kind to a young woman collecting for the Unification Church. She cried: in eight days, they were the first Christians who had been kind to her. One woman had hit her with a Bible.
5The Pharisee and the Child

The pharisee is the impostor in religious clothes

If the pharisee is the religious face of the false self, the inner child is the religious face of the true self.

Two religious faces

The pharisee
  • Bible as a manual of directions
  • Edits feelings, gives stock responses
  • Measures others, condemns them
  • "Nothing to repent of today"
  • Religion that narrows life
The child
  • Bible as a love story
  • Aware of feelings, says them
  • Open, playful, full of wonder
  • Knows they are loved anyway
  • Faith that widens life

Manning walks through the meaning of the Sabbath (a memorial of creation and covenant, a day of honest stock-taking) to show how Jesus' opponents turned a gift into a rulebook.

  • At a prayer meeting, a man in his sixties thanked God that he had nothing to repent of that day. His wife groaned. He meant he hadn't committed any big sin; his blind spots were invisible to him.
  • Don't deny the pharisee within. Befriend him, talk with him and ask why he looks outside the kingdom for peace.
  • A child secure in love achieves things as an overflow, not to earn acceptance. That's the difference between the two faces.
Part three · Chapters 6–9

Living from love

The last chapters turn the identity into a life: awareness that Christ is here, passion recovered, courage against what people think, and rest against his heart.

6Present Risenness

"He is"

The most radical demand of faith, for Manning, is saying yes to the fact that the risen Jesus is present right now.

  • A reporter asked G. K. Chesterton, a new Christian, what he would do if the risen Christ stood behind him at this moment. Chesterton answered: he is.
  • If Jesus didn't rise, the Sermon on the Mount is a fine ethic. If he did, it's a portrait of our future. The gospel's power comes from the resurrection.
  • After almost fifty years, Manning knows faith is lived more in the valley than on the mountain, and is never doubt-free.
  • His own "agnosticism" isn't denying God. It's unbelief growing like lichen from inattention. How we spend time and money shows how aware we are.
7The Recovery of Passion

Passion means being moved

Passion literally means "to be affected by". The capacity to be moved by something is the soul's energy.

The parable, retold

Manning retells Matthew 13:44 as a scene: a day labourer's plough hits a buried pot full of coins and jewels. He hides it, sells his hut and sheep, borrows from everyone, and buys the field while his family despairs and neighbours say he has had too much sun. He is unruffled, even joyful, because he knows what he found.

  • Recovering passion starts with re-valuing the treasure, continues by letting Jesus hold us against his heart, and ends in a change we may not even notice.
  • Jesus is the same yesterday, today and forever: the way he treated Peter, John and Mary Magdalene is how he treats us. For those who, like Peter, have denied him three times, it's a word of liberation.
  • Apart from Christ, the impostor's virtues are "brilliant vices".
8Fortitude and Fantasy

The tyranny of other people's opinions

Manning names his dominant adult sin as the refusal to live from his true self because he feared rejection.

  • Anthony de Mello describes how we fill our emptiness with people, who then control us by approval and disapproval. John 5:44: how can you believe when you look to each other for approval?
  • He still believes in Jesus and loves him, but peer pressure has set limits to his faith, and sometimes he loves his "glittering image" more.
  • The opinions we fear most often come from people we don't even respect. The result is mediocrity: so much good left undone.
  • Fortitude is the courage to live from the beloved self. Fantasy is the life we imagine instead. A teacher in Tokyo advised never to banish the thought of death; Manning adds: never banish the awareness that Christ is risen and present.
9The Rabbi's Heartbeat

Let him hold you against his heart

John at the Last Supper leaned against Jesus and heard his heartbeat. That is knowing someone, not just knowing about him.

  • God is love, Jesus is God: if he stopped loving, he'd stop being God. Manning quotes a chorus of writers who agree that God's love is unconditional, and that a God who swings between kindness and fury is a caricature.
  • Some hear "God loves you as you are, not as you should be" and call it dangerous. Manning answers that it's the only love that can change anyone.
  • He tells the folk tale of Mordecai, a boy who won't go to the synagogue until a great rabbi simply holds him against his heart. Then he learns.
  • The book ends in worship: from John leaning on Jesus' chest to John falling down before the Lamb in Revelation. Tenderness leads into awe.
Learning who he is, you find out who you are.
Manning's closing thought, paraphrased
Practice

Bringing the impostor home

Manning offers no system, but the book keeps returning to a few practices. Here they are, gathered.

Notice him

Spot the maskWhere do you change to fit the room? Where do you say yes from fear?
Name the fearBehind punctuality, perfection or busyness, ask what disapproval you're avoiding.
Write him a letterThank your false self for protecting you, and invite him along.

Receive love

Say "Abba"Pray with the child's word for father, plainly, as Jesus did.
Silence and solitudeLike the cabin: time without media, where buried feelings can surface.
Stop the flagellationBeating up the false self is just the impostor in another form.

Watch the pharisee

Read the Bible as a love storyNot a manual of directions.
Be honest about feelingsThe child feels and says it; the pharisee edits.
Kindness firstEvery encounter gives life or drains it; there's no neutral exchange.

Stay aware

"He is"Remind yourself during the day that the risen Christ is present.
Count the treasurePassion comes back when you re-value what you found.
Listen for the heartbeatLet him hold you silently, like John at the table.
