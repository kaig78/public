Abba'sChild

The cry of the heart for intimate belonging

A former Franciscan priest and recovering alcoholic on the false self we build so that everyone admires us and nobody knows us, and the slow way home to who we really are: a child the Father is very fond of.

9 chapters1 impostor1 pharisee20 days in a cabin
The big idea

Everyone carries an impostor

On 8 February 1956, in a small chapel in Loretto, Pennsylvania, Manning says he was "ambushed by Jesus". Almost forty years later he looks back on seasons when God felt more real than the chair he sat on, and seasons when, quoting Mae West, he "used to be Snow White, but drifted": when the Word was as stale as old ice cream and he mistook dried-up enthusiasm for grey-haired wisdom.

His diagnosis is a split inside every person. The impostor, or false self, is the polished version we show so that everyone admires us and nobody really knows us. The true self is Abba's child: loved as we are, not as we should be. Its religious twin is the pharisee, who tries to earn God's love by keeping rules.

The impostor isn't evil so much as frightened. Manning's own was born when he was eight, after a trauma that shut down his memory for nine years and his feelings for five decades. The cure isn't to beat the impostor up, Manning insists, but to befriend him and bring him, again and again, into the presence of Jesus, where he shrinks.

The book weaves psychology (codependency, the inner child) with the Gospels and a wide circle of voices, from Merton, Nouwen and Buechner to Julian of Norwich, Flannery O'Connor, Woody Allen and Alcoholics Anonymous.

Two selves

Manning's image: the impostor walks into a room announcing himself, "Here I am"; the true self, hidden with Christ, says to the other person, "Oh, there you are!"

Part one · Preface and chapters 1–2

Come out of hiding

Since Adam and Eve, we hide whenever we don't like what we see in ourselves. Manning's first call is to stop: to let God see us as we are, because he already does.

PPreface

Don't whip the false self

Years after the first edition, the chapter on the impostor still drew more letters than all the others combined, and Manning admits the impostor keeps returning in new disguises.

  • His hardest lesson: bringing the impostor to Jesus instead of beating him up. As a 23-year-old Franciscan novice, on Lent Fridays, he whipped himself with coiled telephone wire while Psalm 51 was recited in Latin, until he raised blood blisters. The novice master reprimanded him for "intemperate zeal".

  • Next door, Brother Dismas seemed to scourge himself even harder. Peeking in, Manning saw him smiling with a cigarette in one hand, whacking the wall. Manning felt superior, which was the impostor again.
  • "The grace to grasp grace is grace": even receiving love is a gift.
  • His old spiritual director's blessing: may all your expectations be frustrated and your plans thwarted, so you can know the poverty of a child and dance in God's love.
1Come Out of Hiding

A God who gives turkeys and snatches them away

We project our feelings about ourselves onto God. Pascal: God made man in his image, and man returned the compliment.

The story · Ruller's turkey

In Flannery O'Connor's story, lonely Ruller catches a wounded turkey. Suddenly he feels favoured: he thanks God, imagines becoming a preacher, prays for a beggar and gives away his only dime. Then country boys take the turkey from him. He runs home in the dark, certain that "Something Awful" is chasing him. Manning: many Christians live like this. When God gives, he likes us; when things are taken away, he has rejected us.

  • It takes a deep conversion to accept that God is tender toward us as we are: not in spite of our sins, but with them.
  • A friend asked his disabled son what he saw in Jesus' eyes. "Tears." Why? "Because I'm afraid." God's sorrow is our fear of him.
  • After an alcoholic relapse, Manning says he had two options: sink into guilt and depression, or run into his Father's arms.
  • It's easy to feel loved when life works. What about after a divorce, a crash, a failure? Julian of Norwich: our falling doesn't stop him loving us.
  • Carl Jung and the depressed man. Jung told him to work eight hours instead of fourteen and spend evenings alone. The man read Hesse and played Chopin. Jung: I meant completely alone. "I can't think of worse company." Jung: yet that's the self you inflict on others fourteen hours a day.
  • Manning calls self-hatred the dominant illness of Christians. Nouwen: the greatest trap isn't success or power, but self-rejection.

The cabin, summer 1992

Age 8A trauma. The inner voice: don't be your real self, nobody likes you as you are. Invent someone everyone admires and nobody knows.
Growing upThe good boy: polite, top grades, a scholarship, and constant terror of abandonment.
18 years of ministryPreaching God's love with total conviction, without ever feeling loved. A therapist: a steel trapdoor over his emotions.
Day 10 in the cabinTears, then sobbing. He hears music and dancing, like the prodigal coming home. "Breakdowns lead to breakthroughs."

That afternoon he wrote what feeling safe meant: no longer hiding behind books, TV or ice cream, no need to impress anyone, present to the moment. He immediately warns that even this experience can become a glossier impostor.

  • Merton to a fellow monk: if I make anything of being Thomas Merton, I'm dead; and if you make anything of running the pig barn, you're dead too. Stop keeping score.
  • Thornton Wilder's play about the healing pool of Bethesda: an angel stops a melancholy physician from being healed. "Without your wounds, where would your power be? In Love's service, only wounded soldiers can serve." Hiding our wounds deprives others of our healing gift; AA is a community of wounded healers.
  • Only when we feel safe with God do we feel safe with ourselves.
2The Impostor

A people-pleaser in a thousand masks

The false self lives in fear of disapproval and wants to be admired without being known.

  • Zelig. In Woody Allen's film, Leonard Zelig turns into whoever he's with: a rabbi among rabbis, a psychiatrist among psychiatrists, a Yankee at spring training. Famous for being nobody. Manning finds a lot of Zelig in himself.
  • Fear underneath. He had been proud of always being punctual. In the cabin he saw it came from fear of being scolded.
  • Can't say no. Impostors overcommit, not from conviction but from fear of disappointing people. They're the classic codependents: feelings hidden, emotional honesty impossible, life a roller coaster of others' reactions.
  • He is what he does. Manning hid in ministry: sermons, books, stories. The more successful he was, the more real the impostor became.
  • Small vanities. Despair on the scale after a pint of ice cream; checking his reflection in shop windows; the flush when another author quoted him.
  • The glittering image. In a Susan Howatch novel, a priest about to be exposed asks his spiritual director to tell him about his navy days, a trick that always won older men's approval. The director stays silent.
The dream · Carlton and Moe

In the cabin, Manning heard: you give full attention to the impressive and famous, and less to the plain and lowly. Then he dreamed of Carlton Hayes, an eight-time Olympic champion, giving a tearful speech to 5,000 people at a Ritz-Carlton banquet, with eyes that showed the words no longer lived in him. His paunchy, one-eyed attendant Moe wasn't invited. Moe ate a TV dinner alone at a cheap motel, whispered "Thank you, Jesus" and read Psalm 23. And Manning in the dream? His impostor rented a tuxedo and went to the Ritz.

  • Why settle for this smaller life? Painful childhood memories, and, Manning admits, plain cowardice: silence in the face of injustice, called "being an instrument of peace".
  • The impostor also explains our resistance to prayer, the "sacramental slump" on the way to morning worship. Silence threatens him, because there he'd discover he is nothing.
  • Hating the impostor is self-hatred, and it turns into irritation with other people's faults. Judas raged against his shadow; Peter befriended his. Jung: what if the least of the brothers I must love is me?

The letter on the last day

"Good morning, impostor." Instead of a rod, an olive branch. You protected me when I was little; at four you taught me to build a "cottage" under the blankets where no one could find me. Then you started lying: stuff your feelings, fit in everywhere. Now I'm taking you where you've always longed to be, into Jesus' presence. You're already shrinking, and you look better that way. He gives the impostor the nickname "Pee-Wee". The more you get used to his face, the less applause you'll need, because you'll find that he is enough.

Part two · Chapters 3–5

Define yourself as beloved

The book's central sentence came from a retreat director, striking the table with his fist: make God's love for you the basis of your worth. Every other identity is an illusion.

3The Beloved

The ordinary self is the extraordinary self

The true self is unselfconscious, absorbed in life, breathing God as naturally as a fish swims in water.

  • A travel writer watched a student unpack a Bible stand, clips, coloured pens, syrup and wet wipes at breakfast, half expecting the Ark of the Covenant next. Manning reads it kindly: spirituality isn't a compartment, but a way of living all of life.
  • John Eagan, a Milwaukee high-school teacher for thirty years, never wrote a book or became famous. His journal, published after his death in 1987, records day six of a retreat when his director said: define yourself radically as one beloved by God. The introduction's point: our false humility is often the main obstacle.
  • Holiness looks like raking leaves, sitting in traffic, flying kites and hearing rain on the roof. Revelation 2:17: a white stone with a new name.
  • The church can feed the impostor too, with honours and pecking orders.
  • Mike Yaconelli, a youth-ministry founder, went burned out to the L'Arche community in Toronto. In the silence he heard: Michael, I love you, but you've been so busy proving you're loved that you couldn't hear me. He learned that ministry is sharing, not dominating; caring, not fixing. "That seems to be enough."
  • The impostor's language is puffed up. Manning quotes a pompous book review he once wrote, and a talk where a woman said she only remembered his little story about a strawberry.
The image · the basin

A harried visitor complained to a desert hermit about his failing prayer and relationships. The hermit poured water into a basin. It swirled and slowly settled until the man could see his own face. That's what living constantly among others does, he said: you can't see yourself, and the sense of being beloved fades. Silence makes solitude real. Theologian Edward Schillebeeckx: failing to value simply being with God, without doing anything, guts Christianity.

4Abba's Child

"My Abba is very fond of me"

Jesus called God Abba, the intimate word a child uses for a father, and invites us into the same relationship.

  • Jesus grew up praying the Shema three times a day, yet addressed God with a word no prophet had dared. Joachim Jeremias called it the great novelty of the gospel. Romans 8: not a spirit of slavery that falls back into fear, but the Spirit who cries "Abba".
  • After a parish week in Florida, an 83-year-old woman wrote to him: last week she had been terrified of dying; tonight she was homesick for her Abba's house.
  • On a retreat in January 1977, everything was dust for five days. Then he sat thirteen hours in the chapel until one phrase came: "Live in the wisdom of accepted tenderness." Tenderness grows when you know you're liked; sarcasm, name-dropping and the need to impress fall away.
  • A priest walked with his Irish uncle on his eightieth birthday to watch the sunrise over Lake Killarney. The old man skipped down the road beaming: "My Abba is very fond of me." Manning's question: do you believe God likes you, not just loves you because he has to?

Being like God means being compassionate

If God is mainly…
  • all-knowing → chase knowledge
  • all-powerful → chase influence
  • unchangeable → be granite-hard
Jesus shows
  • compassion
  • forgiveness of enemies as the badge of Abba's children
  • sun and rain on the good and the bad

Forgiveness is humanly impossible for the deepest wounds; Manning says there's only one place to go: Calvary, and stay there a long time. Healing is usually slow, not a sudden release.

  • Compassion matures when we find out where our enemy cries. Stephen Covey on a quiet subway, annoyed by a father ignoring his wild children, until the man says their mother died an hour ago.
  • A 1944 Life photo essay showed 600 people in Ohio encircling and clubbing foxes, teaching their children how. Manning asks who our foxes are; in the early 1990s he meant people with AIDS. He served with an AIDS ministry in New Orleans. One man told them his best friend of twelve years couldn't bear to be near him, while they, strangers, still wanted to be around him.
  • Manning confesses the racism and homophobia he absorbed growing up in Brooklyn, and that denying them is what gives them power. A friend's line: wholeness is brokenness owned and so healed.
  • Compassion isn't "chronic niceness". It doesn't ask a widow to befriend her husband's killer, or wink at injustice. It sees each person's worth.
The story · Bourbon Street

In New Orleans, a young woman collecting for the Unification Church pinned flowers on Manning and his wife. Instead of arguing, he praised her integrity, and his wife Roslyn hugged her. She cried: in eight days, they were the first Christians who had been kind to her. One woman had hit her with a Bible.

5The Pharisee and the Child

The pharisee is the impostor in religious clothes

The real battle isn't sinner against saint inside us, but pharisee against child.

  • The Sabbath. Originally a joyful memorial of creation and covenant: candles lit by the mother, the father blessing each child, guests and the poor at the table, the home as a little sanctuary. Later it became a list of prohibitions. Manning calls this the genius of legalism: making secondary things primary. An old Connecticut code even fined spouses for kissing on Sunday.
  • Jesus wasn't killed by thugs, but by society's most respected religious people. "I want mercy, not sacrifice."
  • The pharisee's gift is blaming. Speeding to a funeral, Manning got a ticket from a Black officer and immediately accused him in his head of racism.
  • A girl who has never been loved tries to earn her parents' love by brushing her teeth, never crying and never needing anything. That's the pharisee before God, who imagines him as the cruel nurse in One Flew Over the Cuckoo's Nest.
  • At a prayer meeting a man in his sixties thanked God he had nothing to repent of that day. His wife groaned.

Two religious faces

The pharisee
  • Earns love by performance
  • Bible as a manual of directions
  • Edits feelings, wears a "religious face"
  • Blames, compares, plays power games
  • "Spiritualizes" feelings away
The child
  • Already loved, does good as overflow
  • Bible as a love story
  • Feels and says it
  • Open, playful, trusting
  • Not crushed by falling

Thérèse of Lisieux: children fall often, but they're too little to do themselves much harm. And no mother holds up her baby and says, "She's going to be a lawyer."

  • When Jacqueline Kennedy visited, Pope John XXIII was told to say "Madame". He threw his arms around her and cried "Jacqueline!" Jesus too showed his feelings: tears, anger, irritation, tenderness.
  • Spiritualizing. Manning wanted to throttle a bigot, but told himself it must be the man's childhood trauma. When a friend said he never listened, Manning called it God testing him. Pious cover-ups smother the child.
  • At nine, his wife Roslyn lost her playmate Bertha Bee, the daughter of the Black housekeeper. Her father said: it is no longer appropriate. Children don't see labels; pharisees live by them.
  • The inner child isn't the goal. There's also a "child in shadow", selfish and fearful. The child is a doorway: my inner child is Abba's child, held in light and shadow.
Part three · Chapters 6–9

Living from love

The last chapters turn identity into a life: awareness that Christ is here, passion recovered, courage against what people think, and rest against his heart.

6Present Risenness

"He is"

A reporter asked G. K. Chesterton what he would do if the risen Christ suddenly stood behind him. Chesterton: "He is."

  • If Jesus didn't rise, the Sermon on the Mount is a fine ethic; if he did, it's a portrait of our destiny. The resurrection kept only in the past or only in the future makes Jesus irrelevant to today.
  • After nearly fifty years, Manning knows faith is lived more in the valley than on the mountain and is never free of doubt. Saying yes to the present Christ is personal and scary, and has to be repeated.
The story · room 422

Frances Brennan, his "second mother" for forty years (he took her surname), lay dying at 91, weighing 62 pounds, in room 422 of a nursing home. They kissed three times; she smiled and died hours later. At a Chicago motel with 161 rooms, he was given room 422. He sank into a chair and heard: why do you seek the living among the dead? "You're alive, Ma!" His inner sceptic said the cheese was sliding off his cracker. He chose to hear a "rumour of angels".

  • A hard day: writer's block, a closed office, a thunderstorm that nearly blew him into the Mississippi while jogging, a fight on the phone with his wife. "I am not my feelings" didn't help. In the evening he prayed the Jesus Prayer, and slowly the sadness grew light. A hard day, yes. Unable to cope, no.
  • The calls that change everything (an accident, an arrest, a toddler by the pool) are our Gethsemane. Etty Hillesum, who died in Auschwitz, spoke of safeguarding the little piece of God in ourselves.
  • Without the risen Christ, life is a series of disconnected news items. With him nothing is wasted, and we stop being one person at home and another at church or in traffic.
  • Hawthorne's "Great Stone Face": a boy gazes at the face on the mountain all his life, until people realise he looks like it. Contemplation is looking at Jesus while loving him.
  • Telling the story doesn't need a pulpit: what my life was like, what happened when I met Jesus, what it's like now. Herman Wouk recalls the day after his bar mitzvah, an almost empty synagogue kept alive by a handful of stubborn old men.
  • The enemy is the "agnosticism of inattention": media overload, shallow reading, perfunctory prayer. Thorns choke the unused path.
7The Recovery of Passion

Passion means being moved

Passion literally means "to be affected by". The ability to be moved by something is the soul's energy.

  • Manning retells the treasure in the field: the plough hits a pot, the peasant screams so loud the ox blinks, sells everything, and his family thinks he's had too much sun.
  • In 1993 a Wisconsin teacher won $111 million. Imagine he missed the 180-day deadline because he was absorbed in baseball. Foolish? Manning did the same, with bourbon instead of baseball, hiding bottles in the geranium pot while knowing where the treasure was.
The parable · Mordecai

A long-awaited boy refused to go to the synagogue, preferring the woods and the lake. Behaviourists and psychoanalysts failed. The Great Rabbi simply held him silently against his heart. The next day Mordecai went to learn, and the Word became one with the woods and the lake. As an old man he said: I first learned the Word of God when the Great Rabbi held me silently against his heart.

  • On a five-day retreat in John's Gospel, Manning's first and last journal entry was John 13: the beloved disciple leaning on Jesus' chest. John's identity wasn't "apostle" but "the one Jesus loves".
  • A dying man who couldn't get past the first pages of a famous book on prayer was told by a friend to put an empty chair by his bed and talk to Jesus in it. When he died, his daughter found him with his head resting on the chair.
  • John's verbs: Mary got up quickly; Magdalene clung; Peter and John ran; Peter, who had denied him, jumped into the water and swam a hundred yards. He denied Jesus, but wasn't afraid of him. What would you do after your worst night?
  • Leadership belongs not to fundraisers or brilliant preachers, but to people "laid waste by passion" for Christ. Before giving Peter authority, Jesus asked three times: do you love me? Few people leave a church for being too forgiving.

8Fortitude and Fantasy

The tyranny of other people's opinions

Manning names the dominant sin of his adult life: refusing, out of fear of rejection, to live from his true self.

  • Anthony de Mello: we fill our emptiness with people, and they then control us with approval and disapproval. John 5:44: how can you believe when you seek each other's approval? The opinions we fear most often come from people we don't even respect.
  • Passion isn't high emotion; it's steely determination, fired by love. Kierkegaard mocked Christians who invent commentaries because the New Testament is uncomfortably clear.
  • Jesus' own family thought him mad. The theologian Johann Metz calls it "the poverty of uniqueness": standing alone when the alternative is a deal that costs your integrity. A picture of Merton on Manning's desk reads: from now on, everybody stands on his own two feet.
  • Doing matters. A dying woman withdrew her hand from her husband, a man with a "Honk if you love Jesus" sticker: forty years of cruelty showed his faith was an illusion. Visiting the used-car dealer who cheated you, with homemade cookies, while still disliking him, isn't hypocrisy. It's doing winning over feeling.
  • Jesus washing feet in the dress and duty of a slave: God wants to be known through servanthood. Downward mobility in an upward culture.
  • Peter walking on the water: Manning finds comfort in the rock of the church sinking like a stone.
  • Fantasy here is the illusion of invincibility, the denial of death. Manning notices he fears death most when the impostor feels successful. A character of Iris Murdoch's, trapped in a cave by the rising tide, realises: only love, reconciliation and forgiveness matter. Benedict: keep your death daily before your eyes.
9The Rabbi's Heartbeat

"Sin will be no shame, but honour"

God is love; Jesus is God; if Jesus stopped loving, he'd stop being God.

Five ways to hear "God loves you as you are"

"That's dangerous."It will make people lazy and lax.
"He loves me too much to leave me there."The response Manning commends.
"Very interesting."The dabbler. Eugene Peterson: Scripture isn't entertainment.
"Words, words."The cynic, a hurt sentimentalist turned inside out.
"Beautiful thoughts."The sincere but unmoved: "uncashed symbols".
  • Sebastian Moore said it took him thirty years to see that admitting sin and being forgiven is the heart of the New Testament. We confess sin in general terms, or assume Mother Teresa's confessions of brokenness were false modesty. Sin's essence is self-centredness. If we only pretend to be sinners, we can only pretend to be forgiven.
  • AA: humility is "stark raving honesty". Recovery starts when holding on hurts more than letting go.
  • On the cross, Jesus went to the far edge of loneliness, forgave our ignorance, and made his pierced heart a safe place for cynics, sinners and self-loathing derelicts.
  • Julian of Norwich: sin will be no shame, but honour, as with David, Peter, Paul and Charles Colson. When Manning confessed his heavy drinking in the Marines, a priest smiled: rejoice, God will use your brokenness to bless many people.
  • We only confess to someone we know accepts us. Moore: only to your lover do you show your worst. Evil impulses are transformed: possessiveness becomes hunger for the treasure; the inner killer kills prejudice; chronic niceness becomes compassion.
  • Why "Rabbi"? Jesus was the teacher who led Manning out of the taverns. And the title keeps Jesus' Jewishness in view: Manning calls antisemitism spit on the face of a Jewish Saviour, much of it Christian spit.
The parable · the iron tank

A man shut himself in an iron tank to escape life. Twice a day bullets ripped through it, wounding him. He cursed the shooter and plugged the holes, until he began looking through them at kites, lovers and the moon, and forgot himself. When the tank finally rusted apart, the gunman stood outside: "I am not your enemy." His hands and feet had scars shining like the sun. The unwounded life bears no resemblance to the Rabbi.

  • Listening to his heartbeat brings four things. You hear Abba's footsteps ("Abba, I belong to you"). You realise you're not alone on the road, so every encounter gives life or drains it. You hear a God who asks, "Do you love me?", something no god before him had asked. And you fall into astonishment: from John leaning on Jesus' chest to John falling down before the Lamb in Revelation.
Learning who he is, you find out who you are.
Manning's closing thought, paraphrased
Practice · Guide for groups

Read it slowly

Manning distinguishes reading for information from reading for transformation: slowly, pausing, sometimes twice. His group guide begins each session with five minutes of silence, a short Bible passage and a few honest questions. Here are its practices, gathered.

Notice him

Name two disguisesWhich masks has your impostor worn lately? Give him a nickname.
Name the fearBehind punctuality, perfection or busyness, ask whose disapproval you're avoiding.
Write him a letterThank your false self for protecting you, and invite him along.

Receive love

Say "Abba"Pray with the child's word for father, plainly.
Not 90%, 50%, 20%Abba doesn't love Jesus fully and you a little (John 17:23).
Let the water settleSilence and solitude, without doing anything.

Watch the pharisee

Feel, then say itDon't spiritualize anger or hurt away.
Hold no grudgeTwo seven-year-olds fight, then share bubble gum.
Find where your enemy criesCompassion starts with understanding.

Stay aware

The empty chairTalk to Jesus as to a friend. Pray as you can; the only way to fail is not to show up.
Stand on your own feetSpeak the truth even when it costs approval.
Listen for the heartbeatLet him hold you silently, like Mordecai.
