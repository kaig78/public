What the book is

A diagnosis that opened a closed man

Barry Michels co-wrote The Tools and Coming Alive with his mentor, the psychiatrist Phil Stutz. Their method is practical: short, repeatable exercises that turn fear, pain and self-doubt into energy for action. Its villain is Part X, the inner saboteur that wants to "x you out". Its source of strength is the Life Force, the creative current that people have to choose to use.

In 2023, at sixty-nine, Michels was diagnosed with Lewy body dementia. He had watched it erase his father and expected five to seven years. About two years later a second diagnosis, Alzheimer's, was added. The book is part memoir, part field report on using his own Tools against a disease that cannot be beaten.

What he finds surprises him. A lifelong depressive raised by militant atheists, who hid his feelings from almost everyone, starts telling everyone the truth, writing poetry in the middle of the night, and feeling, without irony, that God is reaching for him. He takes his title from a Rilke sonnet about letting go before you are forced to.

The title

Rilke's sonnet asks the reader to be ahead of all parting. Michels reads it as practice: meet each small loss consciously now, so that the last one is not your first lesson in letting go.

I · Chapters 1–4

Before

In 2019 Michels is at the top of his field and quietly losing a fight with his own darkness. A tremor appears. The pandemic traps him, his wife and her mother together, and he retreats into work.

01Tremors

The expert who froze on stage

The Tools worked for thousands of other people, but they never quieted the voice telling Michels he was a fraud.

  • Costa Rica, 2019. At an Omega Institute retreat his right hand shakes before a workshop. He blames nerves: he has always dreaded speaking without a script. Once he starts working with a volunteer, the fear goes.
  • Cosmic Rage, demonstrated. A man named Norman is obsessed with a brother-in-law who undermines him in the family laundromat business. Michels has him picture a dark force around the man and rage at the force, not the person. Norman ends up calm.
  • How he got here. A lawyer turned therapist, he met Stutz when Stutz bluntly stopped his domineering mother mid-flow at a family session.
  • Defy, don't silence. A screenwriter paralysed by perfectionism was told to write the worst sentence he could. Bad sentences led to good ones, and the finished script won an Academy Award.

Part X's moves, and the Tool for each

The Tools don't aim to get rid of Part X. In the book it is always there, and each use of a Tool wins back a little access to the Life Force.

02Lockdown

A velvet cage, then a real one

He resisted a home renovation because he wanted insulation, not peace. Then the pandemic closed the door behind him.

  • March 13, 2020. He and Judy, also a psychologist, move into her mother Joan's apartment for what is supposed to be a three-month renovation. Six days later California orders everyone to stay home, and work on the house stalls.
  • Close quarters. They see patients on Zoom from a bedroom and a den and have to time bathroom visits so no patient hears a flush. Joan is 86 and at high risk, so every walk becomes a negotiation.
  • An old darkness returns. Michels has had depressive episodes since adolescence. His paternal grandfather died by suicide, and his mother often threatened it. Now he fixates on the apartment's unscreened twelfth-floor windows. His handwriting turns into a scrawl.
  • Shadow workshops. Hearing from isolated patients and strangers, he starts online workshops on Jung's Shadow, the parts of ourselves we disown. The first, in May 2020, has a few dozen people; a month later, nearly 150.
03The Shadow

The boy who told him off

The Shadow isn't a concept. It's an exiled part of you that is still waiting, often angrily, for you to come back.

  • A daily visit. In a bleak stretch in his mid-forties, Michels began picturing himself at fourteen and asking the boy what he needed. Eventually the boy swore at him and accused him of coming only to ease his own misery. Michels admitted it, and the dialogue opened.
  • 1968. The boy took him back to that year: the Tet Offensive on the nightly news, the killings of Martin Luther King Jr. and Robert Kennedy, and police beating demonstrators in Chicago.
  • The Light. The Shadow's account was that before all that, a child's world was lit by something ancient and warm, and the boy let it go out. Michels accepts the verdict: there were reasons, but no excuses.
04The Genius of Part X

Avoidance dressed up as important work

Part X didn't make him lazy. It made him busy, superior and unreachable.

  • Back home, further apart. They return to the renovated house in July 2020. He hides in his studio over the garage, sees 40 to 50 patients a week, and cries to post-punk hardcore. Part X tells him Judy's worry is poison.
  • Reversal of Desire, misfired. The Tool means moving toward what you fear. He pictures Judy, says "bring it on", then storms downstairs and tells her he is miserable and feels trapped by her.
  • She names it. Judy brings up the shaking hand, the cramps, the memory lapses and the outbursts. He deflects with a trip to Hawaii.
II · Chapters 5–10

Descent

A trip goes wrong, tests confirm the worst, and Michels is surprised by what follows: a flood of calls, beauty, prayer and poetry, with his father's death in the background.

05Lost

The man who never got lost, lost

The diagnosis felt like a guillotine, so he did the least characteristic thing possible and told everyone.

  • Hawaii, 2023. On the Kona coast he feels sealed in a bubble, can't find his hotel room, and even the ocean, his lifelong refuge, feels muted. Returning the rental car, his GPS takes him to a strip mall and he panics.
  • The tests. Judy makes the call he can't. The tests include a skin biopsy that checks the nerves for an abnormal protein, phosphorylated alpha-synuclein. A doctor who keeps her eyes on her screen gives the result: Lewy body dementia.
  • Be real with us. He tries to soften the news for his children. His son Jesse tells him they need honesty, not reassurance.
  • Phil's orders. Stutz, who had lived with Parkinson's for nearly two decades, tells him to treat this as karma and study his own psyche, write every night, exercise, and stop pretending that coping alone is noble.
  • The calls. That night he works through his contacts, starting with his college friend Steve Motenko. He feels energised, not drained, and decides to live this in the open.

What Lewy body dementia takes, as Michels watched it in his father

The order follows Michels's description of his father's decline, not a clinical staging. He adds that LBD can hit memory, perception and impulse control at once (ch. 12). The later Alzheimer's diagnosis added a slow fog on top of what he calls the Lewy "storm" (ch. 16).

06Searching for Meaning

A journal that reports back

He decided the suffering had a direction, and that ending his life would abandon the process that was shaping him.

  • Patients stay. He tells every patient. The malpractice insurer requires waivers, and not one patient refuses to sign.
  • Rage, then record. When despair hits, he uses Cosmic Rage to push it back and clear what Stutz calls the "I Am" space, where you are free to be yourself. Then he writes the win down, because otherwise Part X erases the memory of it.
  • Beauty adds up. Twice-daily walks on the hillside stairs train his attention: a puddle, frogs, lichen. He recalls Viktor Frankl in the camps, sustained by a light in a distant farmhouse. Max Richter's Three Worlds lets him grieve.
07Death Dance with the Divine

Orpheus, read differently

Within a week of the diagnosis, the man of boundaries wrote about dancing with God.

  • The myth. Orpheus looks back and loses Eurydice. Most readers see a failure of faith. Michels also sees a love so strong he would rather have stayed in the underworld. For him Eurydice stands for the creative muse he now has to find quickly.
  • The prose poem. Writing at night to the Richter piece, he describes an image that came to him unbidden: a single dance by candlelight, given to God with nothing held back. Its message is that God cares less about what you do than about how fully you do it.
08Prayer

The sleepover debate

At nine he won an argument against God for his parents, and afterwards started praying in secret.

  • 1963. At a friend's house he refuses to say the Lord's Prayer and lectures the boy's mother that life has no purpose. His atheist parents are thrilled. He feels like a hypocrite and senses God asking why he drove one of his flock away.
  • Underground prayer. He kneels again, watching the door, asking only to be seen and not abandoned. His mother would have treated it as betrayal.
  • The mother he feared. Brilliant and volatile, she punished him by withdrawing for days. He became the clairvoyant good boy who sensed her needs before she spoke them.
09For My Father

Taking the helm

His silent father spoke through sailing, and the same disease Michels now has finally let him speak in words.

  • A wound passed down. When his father was seventeen, his own father warned he would kill himself, then did it a week later. His son found him. The Jewish community refused a Jewish burial and shunned the family. He moved his mother and sister to Los Angeles and became a staunch atheist.
  • Catalina. At thirteen his father handed him the wheel of their forty-foot sloop at night and went below to sleep for four hours. Under a full moon, Barry felt he had received a kind of bar mitzvah.
  • The confession. His father was diagnosed with Lewy body dementia in 1997. Watching Barry with five-year-old Jesse, the disinhibited father said he had loved his son so much it frightened him, because he feared leaving him as his own father had. He said he was grateful for the disease.
10Beauty, Pain, and Creation

Insight by day, demons by night

The same disease that wrecked his moods seemed to thin the veil, in sessions and on the page.

  • Words through him. In sessions, precise intuitions arrive, such as a prompt to ask a client about her son, and they work. Nights bring exhaustion, 3 a.m. waking and violent mood swings.
  • Poems to the Shadow. He realises his catastrophic projections are terrifying his Shadow. One poem resolves not to fix the Shadow's pain but to sit beside it so it isn't felt alone.
  • Phil's verdict. After months he shows Stutz the poems. Stutz says this work is on a higher plane than anything they have done, that no one is better than him, and that he should treat even the depression as positive, demand more of himself.
"We're not looking for this to feel good, we're looking for meaning."
Phil Stutz to Michels, on the poems
III · Chapters 11–15

Roots

The book turns to the event that sealed his atheism, the career that nearly buried him, and the practices that, years later, helped him open again.

11The Incident

The night the walls went up

One act of abuse at fifteen convinced him that no one, God included, was on his side. Imagination, shared with a friend, kept a door open.

  • Worlds with Steve. From seventh grade he and Steve Kivelson invented Middle-earth characters and later spy and post-nuclear stories, staying in role for days. They kept it up into their thirties.
  • A mother without boundaries. She confided her sexual frustrations to her adolescent son at night and demanded displays of affection.
  • The incident. When he was about fifteen she came to his room and staged a "role-play" in which she tried to seduce him so he could practise refusing. He dissociated and felt marked for life. Afterwards he stopped praying, decided God was untrustworthy, and shut his heart.
  • Poems later. LBD brought poems for both: one sees his mother alone at the piano at midnight, too damaged to help; one thanks Steve for being a soul brother in those games.
12Higher Forces

When the guardrails broke upward

Dementia can release reckless impulses. In Michels, so far, what came loose was the urge to love and to say so.

  • Losses. Tremors, cramps, poor balance, forgotten conversations, and a phone screen he can no longer work. He can't drive, which in Los Angeles means giving up independence.
  • The curated self. Shame about his family made him secretive. He brushed off a kind teacher who asked if he was all right, kept girlfriends at a distance, and let only Steve Motenko, his partner in a college doo-wop group, see his depression.
  • Escaping the law. After graduating in 1975 and interning for Congressman Les Aspin, he drifted into Berkeley Law and a corporate firm. His top achievement was merging three pickle companies, for which he was given a jar of pickles. One morning, seeing his reflection among the grey suits outside, he felt a new force push him upstairs to quit.
  • Phil's frame. After a lonely spell in Tunisia he trained at USC. Stutz offered homework, forward motion, and Higher Forces you experience rather than believe in.
13Rilke

God doesn't care about my mind

When his memory failed, Michels concluded that what was being asked of him was not intellect but an open heart.

  • A forgotten fall. Judy asks why the manuscript leaves out her knee giving way in Hawaii. He can't remember it. Then he loses his notes and collapses.
  • Rilke's life. Born in Prague in 1875, once Rodin's secretary, he died of leukaemia at 51, refusing pain relief because he held suffering sacred.
  • The poems he leans on. One Rilke poem imagines losing sight, hearing, limbs and even the heart, and still reaching for the beloved with whatever is left. Michels feels he is living that poem.
  • Ahead of parting. The book's title comes from Rilke's sonnet. Michels reads it this way: death runs through life, every lost expectation is a small death for the ego, and practising those consciously prepares you for the last one.
14Outflow

The sun in the chest

The Tool he uses most: give without holding back, because the energy comes through you, not from you.

  • The exercise. Picture a brilliant sun in your chest and send its light outward in widening circles: to people you love, to people you barely know, then to everyone and everything, including people you despise.
  • The driveway test. Years ago, drained after work, he did it in the parked car, then walked in and played with his children as if rested.
  • Off script. For decades he read workshops word for word. LBD made long reading hard, and a voice said that if he was dying he wanted to speak freely. Prepared with Outflow, he improvised, and people said he seemed deeper.
  • New friends, old secrets. In 2024 he met Jordan Roberts, an Oscar-winning screenwriter moving into coaching. At 71 Michels spent an hour and a half with him in a batting cage. At Omega in Rhinebeck he told the story of the incident in public for the first time, and a woman thanked him for making it possible for her to speak about incest in her own family.

15Communion

Watch what your judgments do, not what they say

About twenty years before the illness, a sulky morning at synagogue gave him a glimpse of what participating could feel like.

  • The reluctant husband. Judy grew up observant; her father was an ordained rabbi. Michels kept her kosher kitchen perfectly but felt nothing.
  • A Saturday service. After a fight in the car he sat with his prayer book shut, mocking jewellery, clothes and an old man's off-key singing, and cursing God for his grandfather and his mother. An inner voice asked what his judgments were doing to him.
  • Participate. He opened the book and sang, and every time he wanted to withdraw he told himself to participate. He wept. The opening didn't last; the lesson did.
A judgment's content
  • "Too much jewellery." "Terrible voice." "Hypocrites."
  • Feels important and often seems correct
  • Keeps attention on other people
A judgment's function
  • Separates you from the community
  • Separates you from God, since what you indict is part of creation
  • Gives you an excuse not to take part in something that might expand you
Exposure · 1Name the possessionWhen your partner is taken over by Part X, tell yourself it's a destructive part of them, not the whole person.
Exposure · 2Drop responsibilityRemind yourself you didn't cause it and can't fix it.
Exposure · 3Drop expectationsExpect no empathy, validation or truth while it lasts. Then their Part X can't set off yours.

Exposure is Stutz's Tool for marriages. Michels says his Part X (a craving for freedom) and Judy's (self-doubt that seeks reassurance) lock together.

IV · Chapters 16–23

Outward

The illness worsens and Los Angeles burns, but he says yes to television, readings, his children's hard questions and two rabbis. The book ends by speaking straight to the reader.

16Walking the Path

Overheard: "He also has Alzheimer's"

Transformation is not a switch. It's showing up, day after day, for work that is often boring.

  • The phone left on. About two years in, waiting for Judy at a hospital, he hears her tell a nurse he has Alzheimer's too. His doctor had advised keeping it from him during a depression. He makes them promise never to withhold again.
  • Raised stakes. Oddly, the news ends his depressive spiral. He insists that his capacity for love won't go anywhere until God says otherwise.
  • Do the work. A patient with back pain wonders whether God is pushing him to lose forty pounds and asks why he can't feel God's presence yet. Michels tells him, bluntly, to stop asking for rewards. AA's "one day at a time" is his model.
17Drew

When the patient steadies the therapist

The roles of helper and helped turned out to be thinner than he thought.

  • Public by her choice. He writes about Drew Barrymore only because she has spoken publicly about their work. They began in 2011. After her 2016 divorce she was drinking again, and he ended therapy until she got sober. She thanked him, got sober, and came back two years later.
  • Vulnerability on air. After interviewing Jennifer Garner, Barrymore told her audience she had been spiralling in comparison. Michels calls this Shadow work in its purest form.
  • Early 2024. A few months after his diagnosis she invited him onto her show. He worked with a volunteer, spoke openly about LBD, and she told him he had saved her life.
18Rudolf Steiner

A God you grow toward

Steiner gave a dogma-allergic man a God who is alive, evolving, and changed by human growth.

  • Steiner's God. Human consciousness is a small whirlpool in a divine ocean, and each shapes the other. Steiner read the Exodus and God's "I am" as the moment humans learned to meet the divine freely and as separate selves.
  • Why "he". Asked by Judy, Michels says that near death God feels like Father Time, the one who sets the limits.
  • The blank. Some days there is "no one home". His answer is to reach up with the heart, not the mind.
19Fatherhood

Saying the true thing to your children

He had feared that openness would drive his children away, as his mother's had driven him. They wanted more of it.

  • Two children, two misses. Hana, oppositional and very funny, went from stand-up comedy to life coaching and joined the Tools community. Jesse, like him, needed limits his permissive father found hard to set.
  • An old poem. He finds a raw piece he wrote years ago about a night of rage at twelve-year-old Hana, and about his mother's poison coming out of him. He reads it to her by phone.
  • What she says. Hana describes growing up with two therapist parents, feeling like the family's "odd one", and quietly acting as peacekeeper. She isn't blaming them. He couldn't have said this to his own parents.
20Fire

Readings, interrupted by a firestorm

Disruption is cruel to a failing brain. Poetry still found him a place to stand.

  • First reading. In December 2024 Jordan Roberts got him onto a bill of six poets at the Ruskin Group Theatre. He read four poems and warned the audience he might cry.
  • January 2025. Days before that reading, Jesse bangs on the door: they are under evacuation orders, with flames on the ridge. Their house survives, smoke-damaged. In a rental he puts an electric kettle on the stove.
  • Small rooms. In March, ten people gathered in a rental living room and he read twenty poems. A month later about forty came to the Miles Playhouse, including his doctor. Hana asked why it had taken him so long. He answered that it took the diagnosis, and apologised that it had.
21Two Sermons

Advice for an anxious congregation

The man who hated services ended up shaping two High Holiday sermons.

  • Procrastination as denial of death. Rabbi Naomi Levy built her Yom Kippur sermon around an idea he gave her years earlier: we put things off because we act as if time won't run out.
  • Letter to Ed. Rabbi Ed Feinstein asks what to tell a community anxious about the state of the world. Michels's reply: facing evil is an honour, complaining about evil does its work for it, and he himself, losing his mind and crying every day, is still giving everything he has.
22Four Poems

Tears as daily weather

A man who barely cried as an adult now cries every day, and these four poems come from that.

  • Can I Leave? On a walk, looking tenderly at the world, he feels it giving him permission to go. He loves it too much to want to, but his bones are tired.
  • He Is in Exile. A boy lives in a world full of the sacred, loses it overnight, and spends the rest of his life missing it.
  • Only Then Am I a Man. A chain of conditions, from silence to words that reach someone.
  • Giving Birth to God. Part X will always knock happiness down, and you build it again knowing it will fall. Tears become seeds.
23To You, My Reader

Love is something to allow

He published while he still could, offering what he learned as testimony, not doctrine.

  • Surrender. The disease took his body, then his intellect, then his sense of self. He calls that surrender the start of redemption.
  • Yearning is sacred. Approached intellectually, God was absent. Approached from the broken, unguarded part of him, God was immediately there. Admitting you're incomplete is the key to intimacy with God and with people.
  • Trauma isn't destiny. As a therapist he rejects the idea that early wounds define us forever. They can heal, he argues, through contact with something larger than the wound. For him that is God's love.
  • His closing counsel. Let go of needing to know. Don't be ashamed of longing. Give without keeping score. Dance and cry when the moment calls for it. Each surrender is a way in.
"Surrender is the beginning of redemption."
Barry Michels, ch. 23
Takeaways

The toolkit

Michels writes for readers who aren't dying too: you don't have to wait for death to start. These are the practices the book keeps coming back to.

Facing the dark

Rage at the force, not the personCosmic Rage: picture the hostility as an impersonal dark force and push it away with full force. Then write the victory down.
Move toward what you avoidReversal of Desire: picture the dreaded thing, say "bring it on", and go through it. Avoidance spreads quietly, like gas from a leak.
Turn toward your ShadowTalk to the disowned part of you, for example yourself at fourteen, and expect it to be angry at first.

Opening the heart

Run Outflow before you walk inPicture a sun in your chest, send its light out in widening circles, then enter the room. "Outflow is not depletion; it's replenishment."
Ask what your judgments doIgnore what they say. If they keep you apart, put them down and take part.
Use Exposure in conflictName your partner's Part X, accept you can't fix it, and stop expecting anything from it while it lasts.
Tell the true thingStart small, choose people who aren't volatile, and let them help you. Coping alone isn't noble.

Living with an ending

Practise small partingsTreat each lost expectation as a rehearsal for letting go, as Rilke's sonnet suggests.
Write every nightLook for meaning in frightening events before fear tells the story for you.
Let beauty build upWalk daily and notice small things. Let sad music help you grieve.
Take the next step onlyDon't look too far ahead. Sense of self, Michels argues, comes from the will to keep moving.
