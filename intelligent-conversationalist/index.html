Introduction

Never knowingly short of an opinion, and always ready to leave

Imogen Lloyd Webber studied history at Cambridge and from 2009 made hundreds of appearances on American cable news, often as the only woman on a panel of men. Before writing she asked more than a hundred people, aged eighteen to eighty, what they didn’t know. Even the most polished admitted gaps, usually quietly and usually after a drink.

Her answer borrows the format of the student’s crib sheet. Eight school subjects (English, money, religion, history, politics, geography, “biology” as sex and gender, and culture) are split into 31 sheets. Most have the same sections: a background briefing, why it matters now, talking points and red flags.

The book is meant to be dipped into before an occasion, not read straight through. A dinner with a banker calls for the money sheets. A job interview with a Brit calls for the slang list. The tone is deliberately cheeky, and she says plainly where her own views take over, above all in the sections on sex and gender.

The useful part is the method. She learned it on live TV, where Sean Hannity once switched topics on her as he introduced her and her research was suddenly useless. Every sheet ends with a social survival strategy in three steps. She calls the last one the most important: when a conversation goes wrong, change it.

Her on-air stall

Every TV regular has a filler phrase for thinking time. Hers is to agree “on some levels”, which buys a few seconds to steer towards ground she knows. The two fillers she says never to use are “like” and “you know”.

The social survival strategy: from heated topic to safe ground

The argument should be close to impossible to disagree with. The crisp fact (her Cambridge professor’s term) shows you know something. The pivot is a question about travel, TV, food or a drink, so everyone can answer it. A stall comes first if you need one: repeat the question, call it a good point, or hand it to someone else.

Part I · Subjects 1–2 · Sheets 1–8

Words & Money

Lloyd Webber’s view is that a misspelt word loses you the argument before you have made it. Money comes next because it underpins everything else, and because most people skipped it at school.

01Sheets 1–4

Spell it right or lose the room

On social media every typo is broadcast. If you misspell a word, readers stop listening to your point.

  • The prime minister’s wife. The book was prompted when the then British prime minister’s wife misspelt “definitely” on Twitter. The spelling sheet pairs words people confuse (affect and effect, lose and loose, stationary and stationery) with a memorable, often rude, sentence for each. She says the only real excuse is dyslexia, which affects 10 to 15 percent of Americans.
  • Grammar without the lecture. Apostrophes either shorten words or show ownership, never both by accident. “Which” is never used for people. For who versus whom, answer the question yourself: if the answer is “him”, use whom. Split infinitives are the one point still open to debate, and nobody objected to them until the 1800s.
  • Separated by a common language. She blames Noah Webster for some of the gap. The real hazard isn’t spelling but words that mean something else in Britain. “Pants” are underwear and “bill” can mean the police. Most dangerous is “quite”, which to a Brit means “not very”. She says people have been fired for misreading it. “Skint” and “gutted” are fine for Americans to borrow.
  • Debate kit. An A-to-Z of impressive words (borborygmus is a rumbling stomach; qoph and xenon win at Scrabble), then lines for every stage of an argument: for buying time, for when you are sinking and for leaving with your reputation intact.
ArgumentIf you misspell “argument”, you have lost it.
Crisp factA spelling nugget, such as chord versus cord, takes the sting out of a row.
PivotAsk for everyone’s funniest autocorrect story. Blaming technology is a relief.
02Sheets 5–8

The emperor’s new money

Since 1971 a dollar has been worth whatever we collectively agree it is worth. Only fear of inflation keeps that agreement honest.

  • From barter to belief. Barter gave way to gold coins and then to paper notes backed by gold. China’s Song Dynasty used paper money early. By the early twentieth century nearly every country was on the gold standard. Roosevelt loosened the dollar’s ties to gold during the Depression. At Bretton Woods in 1944, 44 Allied nations pegged their currencies to the dollar. In 1971 Nixon ended dollar-to-gold conversion, because foreigners with dollars were draining US gold reserves.
  • Why deflation is scarier. If a car will cost a third as much next year, nobody buys one this year. Spending dries up and the economy falls into a spiral. Bitcoin, started in 2009 under the name Satoshi Nakamoto, offers a capped supply, but no government backs it. Only 8 percent of the world’s currency exists as physical cash.
  • Economists versus astrologers. A quick tour runs from Aristotle and Aquinas’s “just price” through Adam Smith (1776) and Marx to Keynes and the Chicago school. “Capitalism” was a word made popular by its critics, mainly Marx. Her default line on any forecast comes from John Kenneth Galbraith: “The only function of economic forecasting is to make astrology look respectable.”
  • 2008 had two causes. The first was the US housing bubble, which peaked in 2006 and was full of mispriced subprime loans. The second was government. Glass-Steagall had separated gamblers’ banks from savers’ banks in 1933 and was repealed in 1999. The shadow banking system grew without capital rules. TARP, a $700 billion bailout, became law on October 3, 2008. She argues the euro was a hasty marriage: monetary union without political union, so eurozone members could not print money to rescue themselves.
  • Three American dreamers. Warren Buffett invests only in what he understands and still lives in the house he bought in 1958 for $31,500. Bernie Madoff’s Ponzi scheme left almost $65 billion missing from client accounts and got him 150 years, after at least six failed official investigations. Oprah, whose birth certificate says Orpah, turned hardship into a media empire and put “aha moment” in the dictionary.

What a unit of money could be swapped for

The link between a note and anything physical got longer over time, and in 1971 it was cut. In her words, everyone agrees to act as if we are fully clothed. Even under Bretton Woods only foreign governments, not ordinary holders, could convert dollars into gold.

Part II · Subjects 3–4 · Sheets 9–17

Faith & History

Religion is where conversations most easily go wrong. History is where she spent her degree. In both, her advice is to know where people’s grievances come from before you start talking.

03Sheets 9–11

More alike than they admit

The big religions share ancestors, calendars and contradictions. Most of the damage comes from not knowing the others.

  • Two families. Judaism, Christianity and Islam all trace back to Abraham. Hinduism, Buddhism and Sikhism are Dharmic and focus on refining consciousness. The world has roughly 4,200 religions and about 1.1 billion people who follow none. She admits that until 2013 she didn’t know Jesus is a prophet in Islam. He is mentioned five times as often as Muhammad in the Qur’an.
  • The grid. For each faith, a table covers followers, beliefs, scriptures, diet, rules on sex and marriage, the afterlife and a celebrity follower. It is drawn as a chart below.
  • Moving holidays. All the main faiths use lunar calendars to some degree, so their dates shift. Easter is the first Sunday after the first full moon following March 21. Islamic law has only two festivals, the two Eids. The Jewish day starts at sunset, so festivals begin the evening before. Find out which day is someone’s sabbath before sending them a pushy email.
  • Christmas is half pagan. Mistletoe, the date and much of the rest came from older festivals. Pope Julius I fixed December 25 in the fourth century. The Puritans cancelled Christmas in 1644. Since those Puritans helped found America, she says the “war on Christmas” is the oldest American tradition there is.
  • No excuse. She borrows The West Wing’s answer to people who quote Leviticus against gay people: scripture also endorses selling daughters and executing sabbath-breakers. Every faith has produced violence, including Buddhist monks in Sri Lanka and the Hindu who killed Gandhi. Most scholars, she reports, say jihad means struggle, usually inner struggle, and that its laws forbid killing non-combatants.

04Sheets 12–17

Blame the British, and remember the spies

History is written by the winners. Commentary on current affairs also misses most of what the intelligence services know.

  • The secret world. Her Cambridge teacher, Christopher Andrew, changed how she sees international affairs. Perspectives on World War II shifted in the 1970s once the Bletchley Park code-breaking was revealed. In 1983 a NATO exercise, Able Archer, was misread in Moscow as a possible first strike. Intelligence from the British double agent Oleg Gordievsky helped persuade Reagan to tone down his rhetoric. Her stock line: all we can hope is that leaders get good intelligence and listen to it.
  • American beginnings, for immigrants. She writes this sheet for people who were never taught it, as one of about a million legal immigrants who arrive each year. Roanoke’s 116 settlers vanished between 1587 and 1590. Jamestown came in 1607. The Mayflower sailed in 1620 with 102 people, under a third of them Pilgrims. Boston’s Tea Party in 1773 dumped 342 chests of tea. The slave population grew from almost 700,000 in 1790 to almost 4 million by 1860. She sees America’s history as a series of civil-rights struggles. Henry Hudson sailed up his river on September 11, 1609.
  • Grids of rulers. There have been 44 US administrations but 43 presidents, because Grover Cleveland served twice. England has had 40 monarchs since 1066, fewer than America’s presidents. A children’s rhyme lists them all. She also notes that Britain tried a republic in the seventeenth century and ended up offering its leader the crown.
  • Empire by another name. She traces America’s outward push from Hawaii and the 1898 war with Spain to Vietnam, where more than 3 million people died, over 55,000 of them American. She argues that after the Cold War, foreign policy tried to change what happens inside other countries, which is far harder. She is openly critical of Obama-era armed drones, which she argues set a precedent other states will copy.
  • Two world wars and a cold one. In World War I, armies planned for victory using national birth rates, and Britain lost 58,000 men on the first day of the Somme. World War II killed an estimated 45 to 60 million people, about 85 percent of them on the Allied side, including some 27 million Soviets. She argues nobody won the Cold War, and lists its nine tensest moments, from the Berlin blockade to Able Archer.
  • The Middle East. Her key point is that Saudi Arabia is Sunni and Iran is Shia, and the likely outcome is two rival spheres of influence. The borders drawn after World War I ignored religion and ethnicity, which she blames on Britain. She covers the 1953 CIA-backed coup in Iran, the 444-day hostage crisis and an Israel–Palestine timeline from the 1947 partition plan to 2015.
ArgumentThe post-1918 Middle East borders made no religious or ethnic sense. The British will agree.
Crisp factIn World War I the German army dropped leaflets telling Black American soldiers a German victory would mean less racism.
PivotBerlin was entirely surrounded by East Germany. Have you been?
Part III · Subjects 5–6 · Sheets 18–23

Power & Place

An outsider’s admiring guide to how American government works, and a short lesson for anyone who confuses Austria with Australia.

05Sheets 18–21

Built to make change hard

The Constitution forces compromise at every step. That causes gridlock, and it also keeps extremists out.

  • The oldest and shortest. Written in 1787 and in use since 1789, the US Constitution is the longest-serving written national constitution in the world. It has seven articles, the first three setting out Congress, the presidency and the courts. It has been amended 27 times, with 26 amendments in force, because the Twenty-first repealed Prohibition. The Twenty-seventh, on congressional pay, took 203 years to pass.
  • Guns as a British idea. She points out that the right to bear arms can be traced to the English Bill of Rights of 1689. Britain then tightened gun laws from 1824 onward. She contrasts Britain’s roughly 150 gun deaths a year with America’s more than 30,000. She reports an estimated 300 million guns in the US.
  • Law-making vocabulary. Bills get marked up, tabled, reported out and enrolled. The record filibuster is Strom Thurmond’s 24 hours and 18 minutes against the 1957 Civil Rights Act. The word comes from the Dutch for pirate. FDR vetoed 635 bills. She is sharply critical of the secret surveillance court FISC, which hears only the government’s side.
  • Scandals. She runs through anti-gay Republicans caught with men, Democrats caught with women, and corruption from Grant’s presidency to Watergate and Iran-Contra. The lesson is always that the cover-up is worse than the crime.
  • Elections. To win the White House you need 270 of 538 Electoral College votes. Florida in 2000 came down to 537 votes. In 2012 Ohio saw 219,414 political ads and 51 Romney visits. That year Democrats won 1.4 million more House votes but Republicans won the House 234 to 201. She treats America as a 51/49 country where about 40 percent of voters call themselves independents.

How a bill becomes law, and how a veto is beaten

Each step is a chance for the bill to die. Since 1980 neither party has held 60 Senate seats, so the threat of a filibuster shapes most bills. The 113th Congress (2013–15) passed 296 laws. Truman’s “Do Nothing” Congress passed 906. She adds that what matters is the quality of the laws, not the count.

06Sheets 22–23

Two maps are all you need

Americans have a reputation for weak geography. A short list of common mix-ups fixes most of it.

  • The myths. The claim that only 10 percent of Americans hold passports is out of date. Recent figures, she says, put it nearer a third. Only about 3.5 percent travel overseas in a given year, and Canada and Mexico don’t count. Britain is England, Scotland and Wales. The United Kingdom adds Northern Ireland. Turkey’s capital is Ankara.
  • Her top-ten mix-ups. Brunei is in Southeast Asia, not the Middle East, and has almost no public debt. Oman is a country and Amman is a city. Austria has no kangaroos. Oakland is not Auckland. Bern, not Geneva or Zurich, is Switzerland’s capital, and Ottawa, not Toronto, is Canada’s. Paraguay is landlocked and Uruguay is not. Slovenia and Slovakia get confused so often that their embassy staff meet monthly to swap misdirected mail.
  • Climate. She treats this as settled: in 2013, 900 scientists reviewed 9,000 studies and put the chance that climate change is man-made at 95 percent. She adds a cost argument. Hurricane Sandy alone cost $50 billion, so cutting emissions would be cheaper than paying for disasters.
  • The nuclear club. Every permanent member of the UN Security Council has the bomb, and the US is the only country to have used one in war. She argues that after Gaddafi gave up his programme in 2003 and fell eight years later, dictators concluded nuclear weapons are their only real guarantee of survival.

Who has the bomb, by the book’s three tiers

Fourteen countries in three tiers. Iran signed the Non-Proliferation Treaty, but disputes over its status led to the 2015 negotiations. The book reminds readers that enriching uranium is not in itself illegal.

Part IV · Subjects 7–8 · Sheets 24–31

Body & Culture

She warns readers that she gives up any pretence of impartiality here, describing herself as a European-born feminist who works on Broadway. Then she ends with the arts and awards season, which she knows best.

07Sheets 24–26

The personal is political

Views on feminism or gay marriage often change when a daughter is underpaid or a son comes out.

  • A detour for the uncomfortable. Readers who expect to disagree get a set of neutral nuggets and permission to skip ahead. Phones carry ten times more bacteria than most toilet seats. Distilled alcohol was first made by Muslim chemists. Until 2011 Russia classed beer as a soft drink.
  • Contraception and abortion. Some 99 percent of American women aged 15 to 44 who have had sex have used contraception. The pill was approved in the US on May 9, 1960. She stresses that the morning-after pill prevents pregnancy and does not end one. Roe v. Wade (1973) was decided 7 to 2. In a 1972 Gallup poll, Republicans were more likely than Democrats to say abortion was a matter for doctor and patient. She says polls consistently put three-fifths of Americans somewhere between pro-life and pro-choice.
  • Death. As of May 2013, 140 countries had abolished the death penalty in law or practice. In 2014 the US ranked fifth for executions, after China, Iran, Saudi Arabia and Iraq. By 2015 it was the only country in the western hemisphere still carrying them out.
  • Feminism’s waves. The Oxford English Dictionary’s 1895 definition is simply equality. The first wave won the vote, and the second, sparked by Betty Friedan, won a string of laws from 1963 to 1978. The Equal Rights Amendment fell three states short of ratification. Marital rape was not illegal in every US state until 1993.
  • LGBTQ rights. The word “homosexuality” was coined in 1869. The American Psychiatric Association stopped calling it an illness in 1973. Then came Stonewall in 1969, Gilbert Baker’s eight-colour flag in 1978, Don’t Ask Don’t Tell, DOMA and Proposition 8. In 1980, 11 percent of Americans backed same-sex marriage. By June 2015 most did, and the Supreme Court made it a constitutional right. As of 2015, 66 countries still made homosexuality a crime.

When women got the vote

Her favourite fact for this subject is Switzerland in 1971, eight years after Iran. She notes it offends no one because it is simply true. Saudi Arabia granted partial suffrage in 2011, effective from 2015. Brunei has no elections for anyone.

08Sheets 27–31

There are no facts, only interpretations

The arts are the safest place to disagree, and awards season gives everyone something to talk about.

  • Grids of the greats. Authors from Homer to the moderns, artists from Giotto on, composers from Machaut on. Each entry has what it is famous for, a quirk and a talking point. Cervantes spent five years as a slave. Leonardo never handed over the Mona Lisa. El Greco waited about 250 years to be appreciated. The British Airways tune is Delibes’s “Flower Duet”.
  • Theater’s long run. Greek drama used only three actors, and their masks helped carry their voices. England’s first theater, the Red Lion, opened in 1567. The Puritans closed the theaters in 1642, and women were allowed on stage after 1660. Shakespeare, she reports, used about 17,000 words and coined about 1,700 of them. One of them was her first name, Imogen.
  • Broadway and the West End. Broadway has about 40 theaters seating more than 500 and draws around 13 million people a year. The West End sells about as many tickets. The Golden Age ran from Oklahoma! (1943) to Hair (1968). The Mousetrap has run in London since 1952. Sondheim and her father, Andrew Lloyd Webber, share a birthday, March 22. A show is ON Broadway but IN the West End.
  • Awards season. The People’s Choice Awards, the Globes, the guild awards, the BAFTAs and the Oscars run in order, followed by the Tonys, Grammys and Emmys. The Oscar statuette is 13.5 inches tall. Since 1950, winners can’t sell one without first offering it back to the Academy for a dollar. After a Grammy win, weekly sales jumped 714 percent for the Dixie Chicks in 2007.

Conclusion & toolkit

Generalists are specialists at changing the subject

She ends by admitting the sheets are only an overview, then argues that the overview is what you need to see how stories fit together. Her parting quotation is from Desmond Tutu: “Don’t raise your voice, improve your argument.”

Hold the floor

Have a stalling move readyRepeat the question, call it interesting, or hand it to someone else. Agreeing “on some levels” buys a few seconds.
Drop the fillers“Like” and “you know” tell people you are lazy or out of your depth.
Proofread before you postOne misspelling and nobody hears your point. Check before you press send.
Keep an outsider’s maximA safe line nobody can argue with, like Galbraith on forecasters or Wilson on a week in politics, steadies the room.
Know the slang trap“Quite good” is faint praise to a Brit. Check what a word means to the person you’re talking to.

Land a fact

One crisp fact beats a lectureChoose something surprising, true and short enough to remember after a drink.
Remember the secret worldPundits rarely know what the intelligence services know. Saying so is smarter than claiming certainty about foreign policy.
Separate the mythsBretton Woods isn’t the Marshall Plan, Austria isn’t Australia, and the morning-after pill isn’t an abortion pill.
Respect the calendarKnow which day is someone’s sabbath and whether it is Ramadan before you offer lunch or chase a reply.
Admit what you don’t knowOn terrorism or the Middle East, if you aren’t an expert, ask one questions rather than lecture.

Pivot out

Ask what anyone can answerFavourite city, best bridge, guilty-pleasure TV, Oscar speech. Nobody can be wrong about these.
Travel is the universal exitSix of the 31 pivots go to places. Everyone has somewhere they loved or want to see.
Offer a drink or foodThe oldest distraction still works: the bar, the canapés, or a spot by the kitchen.
Let yourself be the jokeBlame autocorrect, mock your own sense of direction, admit an addiction to old sitcoms. It takes the heat out.
Close with a line, then switchOn politics, saying “wake me up after election day” lets everyone relax and move on.
