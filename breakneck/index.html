Breakneck

China's quest to engineer the future

Dan Wang spent six years in China watching a state that builds anything and controls everyone. He holds it up as a mirror to an America that has forgotten how to build.

Introduction & the big idea

One country builds, the other blocks

Wang is a Canadian who left Yunnan with his parents at seven and returned as technology analyst for the research firm Gavekal Dragonomics, living in Hong Kong, Beijing and Shanghai from 2017 to 2023. His starting point is that Americans and Chinese are more alike than either admits: materialist, pragmatic, in love with grand projects, and sure their country should throw its weight around.

Labels like capitalist and socialist no longer explain either country, he argues. He proposes a new pair. China is an engineering state: run by people trained as engineers who reach for concrete, steel and quotas to solve any problem, physical or social. The United States is a lawyerly society: run largely by people trained in law, very good at procedure and at stopping things, good and bad.

Neither label is a compliment. The engineering state gave Chinese citizens subways, bridges and a manufacturing base, and also the one-child policy and zero-Covid. The lawyerly society protects individuals, but it mostly serves the well-off and has lost the ability to deliver housing, transit and factories. Each, Wang argues, has something the other badly needs.

The worst year to be born

Wang's thought experiment: a girl born in 1949 lives through famine, a shut university system, the one-child policy and, at seventy, Covid lockdowns. A boy born ten years later gets into a reopened university, rides the export boom and gets a city flat almost free. In the engineering state, your birth decade can decide everything.

Build

The first three chapters set up the framework and show its strongest side: infrastructure even in poor provinces, and a manufacturing workforce that keeps getting more capable.

01Engineers vs. Lawyers

Two governing classes, two failure modes

Engineers can't stop building and lawyers can't stop objecting. Each habit solved an old problem and created a new one.

  • Engineers in charge. Deng Xiaoping promoted engineers to clean up after Mao. For his third term from 2022, Xi filled the Politburo with executives from the aerospace and weapons industries. Since 1980 China has built a highway network twice the length of America's and almost as much wind and solar capacity as the rest of the world combined.
  • A right-wing state in red. Wang argues that the Communist Party only looks leftist. It taxes lightly, offers a thin safety net, restricts migration and enforces traditional gender roles.
  • How America changed. The US was once an engineering state too, from canals to Eisenhower's interstates. After the pollution, urban highways and segregation of the 1960s, elite law students turned to suing the government, and the right attacked government from the other side. That fixed real problems. Wang argues it has since become a cause of new ones.
  • Two pathologies. Process takes the place of outcomes: agencies study every option so no lawsuit can stop them, and then little gets built. And the system favours the rich, who can afford the lawyers to block a project or protect their wealth.
  • The engineers' blind spot. They see people as aggregates, as if society were a set of pipes and valves. Few voices take part in decisions, so policy can switch direction overnight and pull the whole population into a crisis.
  • Why it matters now. The US has more allies, a younger population and better software. China can build, and a war would need drones and munitions in huge numbers. Wang thinks both countries are wearing down their own ability to govern.

02Building Big

Bridges to nowhere that become somewhere

China's version of socialism doesn't redistribute much money. It builds public works instead, and the debts and waste are part of the deal.

  • Cycling Guizhou. In 2021 Wang and two friends rode about 400 miles through Guizhou, China's fourth-poorest province, to Chongqing. Income there is about $8,000 a head, similar to Botswana, yet it has 45 of the world's 100 highest bridges, eleven airports and around 1,000 miles of high-speed track. Locals were proudest of the bridges.
  • The scale of it. China built an interstate's worth of expressways in 18 years and a second one in nine. Cars went from half a million in 1990 to 435 million in 2024. Its high-speed network is longer than the rest of the world's combined and makes about two billion trips a year. Its urban population has grown by about 16 million a year since 1978.
  • Cheaper, not just faster. Standard designs and good project management bring high-speed rail in at about $33 million a mile, against $192 million in California. A 2019 World Bank study found the network economically viable. China has 31 nuclear reactors under construction to America's one.
  • A stingy welfare state. Social spending is about 10% of GDP, half the US level. Nearly three-quarters of people pay no income tax, and only about a tenth of the unemployed qualify for benefits. In 2021 Xi warned that welfare could make people lazy.
  • The bill. Five of Guizhou's airports get fewer than a dozen flights a week. Tianjin built a hollow "Manhattan" in Binhai, then admitted overstated growth and cut its GDP by nearly 20%. China can make about 60 million cars a year in a world market of about 90 million. Building has also meant pollution, forced resettlement and badly built schools that collapsed in the 2008 Sichuan earthquake.
  • The US mirror. Cape Wind off Massachusetts was abandoned after sixteen years of lawsuits by wealthy residents. In 2023 the US added 6 gigawatts of wind and China added 76. Wang's conclusion is that China should build less and better, and America should build more.

The local official's building loop

Officials are selected, not elected, and get moved around. Without a property tax, local governments rely on land sales and borrowing. Projects that look good in a growth record get approved, and the cost arrives after the official has moved on. Wang's case study is Li Zaiyong in Liupanshui: a ski resort with little snow, imitation European squares now grazed by goats, and about $21 billion of new debt. He was later sentenced to death with a two-year reprieve.

03Tech Power

Technology lives in people, not blueprints

China's tech strength comes from factory floors where millions of workers keep learning by doing, not mainly from planning or theft.

  • Shenzhen. It grew from 300,000 people in 1980 to 18 million in 2020. Toys and clothing gave way to electronics, and then to the iPhone. At peak season Foxconn's Shenzhen campus holds about 300,000 workers. Apple's planners once estimated it would take nine months to hire some 9,000 industrial engineers in the US. In China it took two weeks.
  • A city that works as a supply chain. Nearly any part can be found within a short drive. When a new design needs an odd screw, someone knows a supplier. Companies like DJI and BYD grew out of cheap smartphone components and a workforce that already knew how to assemble them.
  • Three kinds of technology. There are tools, written instructions, and process knowledge: the skill that comes only from practice. The US lost the recipe for a nuclear-weapons material called Fogbank because the people who knew it had retired, and spent $69 million working it out again.
  • What offshoring cost. Each closed US factory takes its know-how with it. Wang links the decline of Boeing and Intel to this erosion, and contrasts the Chinese approach: let Tesla fully own its Shanghai plant in 2018 so local carmakers had to raise their game. BYD lost sales at first and later became the world's largest EV maker.
  • Completionism. Xi doesn't want any industry to leave China, low-end ones included. Manufacturing is 28% of GDP and the five-year plan wants it to stay there. Wang also describes the online "Industrial Party", nationalist writers who believe technology decides which nation wins, and who have made Liu Cixin's Three-Body trilogy their favourite text.
  • Export controls backfired. Wang argues that US sanctions made American suppliers look unreliable, which pushed Chinese firms toward domestic vendors they had previously ignored.

Control

The same habits, applied to people. Each policy had a number in its name, was pursued like a military campaign, and caused great harm before it was dropped.

04One Child

A missile scientist plans the population

The one-child policy came from engineering logic applied to families. It projected a straight line, set a target and enforced it at any cost.

  • Mao versus the planners. Mao believed a big population meant power. After his death, leaders were shocked by an estimate of nearly one billion people at the end of 1978, and feared it would swamp Deng's modernisation plans.
  • Song Jian's model. A missile-guidance scientist who had studied cybernetics in Moscow, Song heard warnings of ecological collapse at a 1978 conference in Helsinki. His team projected that at 3.0 children per woman China would reach 3 billion by 2050 and over 4 billion by 2080, and that the "optimal" population was 700 million at most. His printed, computer-drawn graphs beat rivals working with abacuses. China adopted the policy in 1980.
  • Enforcement as a campaign. General Qian Xinzhong ran it with "shock brigades". In 1983 the state sterilised 16 million women and carried out 14 million abortions. Officials were rewarded or punished on their quotas, and late-term abortions were common. In 1991 one Shandong county ordered no births at all for a hundred days.
  • The toll. Over 35 years there were 321 million abortions and 108 million women and 26 million men sterilised. The birth sex ratio reached 120 boys to 100 girls in 1999, and demographers estimate about 40 million "missing" women. More than 150,000 children, almost all girls, were adopted abroad.
  • Probably unnecessary. Fertility had already fallen from about 6.0 in 1970 to 2.7 by 1980, under milder policies. Demographers credit economic growth, urbanisation and education for the rest, as in Japan, South Korea and Taiwan.
  • Now the reverse. Births fell from 15 million in 2019 to 9 million in 2023, and fertility is about 1.0. Xi tells women to build families, and neighbourhood officials call to ask when they will have children. Wang doubts pressure will raise birth rates; pronatalist policies elsewhere have rarely worked for long.

05Zero-Covid

Three years pursuing one number

Zero-Covid showed what the engineering state does best and worst: it can mobilise a whole country, and it won't stop when the costs mount.

  • Act one: fury. Wuhan officials hid the outbreak to keep a political meeting on track. Doctor Li Wenliang was reprimanded for warning people, then died of the virus. Wang points out that this followed the same pattern as Henan's AIDS outbreak and SARS.
  • Act two: pride. A hospital went up in eleven days, borders shut, and from spring 2020 life in China was mostly normal while other countries struggled. Health-code apps decided who could go where. State media presented this as proof of the system's superiority.
  • Act three: Shanghai. Officials denied a lockdown was coming, then announced an eight-day "pause" in late March 2022. It lasted about eight weeks, until 1 June, for 25 million people. Food supply broke down, with trucking at 15% of normal. Neighbours organised group buying on WeChat. Infected people were taken to mass quarantine centres, and patients with other illnesses struggled to get care.
  • Why few protested. People had been taught to fear the virus, controls were added gradually, and nobody expected eight weeks. Police relied on signed statements that hung over people, not open force. Wang spent months in Dali in Yunnan, where the state's reach is weak, and that is where the engineer/lawyer idea first came to him.
  • The end. After an Urumqi fire in which ten people died, protests followed in November 2022, including the blank-paper protests. In early December the policy was dropped, with no extra vaccination push, few fever medicines and no warning to hospitals. Official deaths were about 125,000. Scholarly estimates of excess deaths approach two million.
  • The lesson. The party "followed the science" here as it had with birth control. Wang argues that how to apply science is a political judgement, and that lawyers, economists and ethicists in the room give a society a better chance of making it well.

06Fortress China

The people leave, the factories stay

The engineering state is driving out its creative and wealthy citizens while getting better and better at manufacturing. Both are true.

  • Rùn. Pandemic slang meaning "to flee". Wang meets young Chinese in Chiang Mai who hide from their parents that they are in Thailand. An emigration firm estimated nearly 14,000 millionaires left in 2023 and over 15,000 in 2024. US border arrests of Chinese nationals rose from 450 in 2021 to 38,000 in 2024.
  • The unicorn hunter. From late 2020 regulators went after the tech sector: Ant Financial's listing was stopped, Didi was investigated, children's gaming was capped at three hours a week, and online tutoring firms were barred from making a profit. A trillion dollars of market value disappeared. In 2024 Xi asked why China was producing fewer and fewer unicorns.
  • "Sixty percent correct." Wang's verdict on Xi: usually right about the problem, but the brute-force fix makes it worse. Big tech was too powerful, developers were too indebted and corruption was real. The results were frightened entrepreneurs, a property slump and a paralysed bureaucracy.
  • What engineers can't do. They can't make culture the world wants. Comedy is censored, films are pulled, and the renminbi is only 3% of global payments because capital controls stay. The Belt and Road gave many developing countries useful infrastructure, but by 2023 its forum drew about three dozen leaders instead of 120.
  • What they do well. Resilience. China stockpiles, grades officials on food self-sufficiency, and in 2023 added twenty times more coal capacity than the rest of the world, alongside its renewables. It will graduate more than twice as many STEM PhDs as the US in 2025. Wang expects something like a more successful East Germany: surveillance combined with strong science and industry.
  • Hardware versus software. In 2022 China had nearly 1,800 ships under construction and the US had 5. Ukraine fired in two days as many shells as the US makes in a month. Wang warns that AI alone won't win a war fought with drones and munitions.

07Learning to Love Engineers

What America should take back

The US doesn't need China's methods. It needs its own building tradition back, alongside the pluralism that made it worth emigrating to.

  • A family bet. Wang's grandparents were variously a soldier, a landlord's daughter, a Nationalist official's daughter and a fallen merchant heir, and all suffered under Mao. His parents, both born in 1959, left Kunming for Canada in 2000 and had years of poverty before settling near Philadelphia. His mother sometimes wishes they had stayed. His father says most of their classmates would trade places with them.
  • Rehabilitating Robert Moses. Robert Caro's 1974 The Power Broker taught Americans to fear master builders. Wang visits Sunset Park, the Brooklyn neighbourhood Caro said Moses had gutted, and finds it thriving with immigrants and a busy Moses-built pool. Moses had serious flaws, Wang says, but New York still runs on what he built.
  • A message for each side. The left should stop fighting the battles of the 1960s. The right should remember Admiral Rickover's nuclear navy, built inside government, and target procedure rather than civil servants.
  • The costs of slowness. A kilometre of subway costs five times as much in New York as in Paris. $42 billion for rural broadband in 2021 had connected no homes four years later. $7.5 billion for EV chargers had produced seven stations after two years.
  • Two tasks. Remember America's engineering heritage, and widen its elite so that engineers and economists sit alongside lawyers. Countries like Spain, Germany and Japan show a middle path between consultation and construction.
  • Who wins? Wang thinks China won't outcompete the US: its leaders distrust their own people and drive out its most creative citizens. America still has to prove it can build again.

Takeaways

The toolkit

The book is argument and reportage rather than a manual, but it offers ways of thinking that help in reading the news about either country.

Read the system

Ask who is in the roomEngineers, lawyers or economists? The training of the people at the top shapes what a government reaches for.
Watch for a number in the nameOne child, zero Covid. A single target makes enforcement easy and lets the costs pile up unseen.
Trace the official's incentivesA short posting plus growth metrics plus land-sale financing means debt and vanity projects. Most local failures follow from that.
Hold two truths at oncePeople are leaving and factories are rising. China is getting better and worse at the same time.

Build better

Count outcomes, not processMeasure track laid and homes finished, not studies, meetings or jobs announced.
Keep the know-howTechnology is people practising. Closing a plant loses skills that a later subsidy can't easily buy back.
Invent and then scaleA lab breakthrough that someone else manufactures is a lost industry. Solar cells were invented at Bell Labs and are now made in China.
Notice who a veto servesLengthy review often protects those who can afford lawyers, not the people a project would help.

Keep the people

Science on tap, not on topSong Jian's graphs looked rigorous. Policy needs lawyers, economists and ethicists to test what "following the science" means.
Leave room for weapons of the weakFoot-dragging, loopholes and arguing back are how ordinary people live with heavy-handed rules. Wang sees hope in that everyday resistance.
Right diagnosis, wrong doseWang's verdict on Xi is a warning for any reformer: a sledgehammer can make a real problem worse.
Call yourself developingA country or a person that considers itself finished stops building.
