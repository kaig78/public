Life of theBeloved

Spiritual living in a secular world

A Catholic priest writes a long letter to a secular Jewish friend in New York, trying to say one thing without church language: you are loved before you prove anything, and a whole life can grow from hearing it.

The big idea

One word, written for one friend

The book starts with a bad interview. While Nouwen was teaching at Yale Divinity School, a bored young journalist named Fred Bratman came to profile him for the Connecticut section of the Sunday New York Times. Fred admitted he disliked the job and really wanted to write a novel. On the spot, Nouwen told him to quit, come to Yale for a year and write it, and promised to find the money. Fred came. The novel never got written, but the two became close friends.

Years later, walking down Columbus Avenue, Fred asked for something Nouwen had never managed: “Why don't you write something about the spiritual life for me and my friends?” He meant young, ambitious, secular New Yorkers who found Nouwen's earlier books good but not meant for them.

The answer is this book, written as a personal letter to Fred. Its core is one word, Beloved, which Nouwen takes from the voice heard at Jesus' baptism. He argues that it names the deepest truth about every person, religious or not. The rest of the book shows how that truth becomes real in ordinary life through four movements, borrowed from what he does with bread at the communion table every day: take, bless, break, give.

The four movements of the Beloved

Nouwen says the four words sum up his life on three levels: as a priest, who takes, blesses, breaks and gives bread every day; as a Christian, called to become bread for the world; and as a human being, because all four are happening in every moment. Drawn as a loop for this page. Each movement ends by turning outward, which feeds the next.

Being the Beloved

The greatest trap is not success but self-rejection

The quiet voice that calls you beloved is always speaking. The trouble is that other voices are louder, and they all say the same thing: you are worth nothing unless you prove otherwise.

Nouwen's one message to Fred is the sentence from the baptism story: “You are my Beloved, on you my favor rests.” The best gift a friend can give, he says, is to hand that truth on, and you can only hand on what you have claimed for yourself.

The obstacle is self-rejection. Success, popularity and power are real temptations, but he thinks their pull comes from something underneath: once we believe we are unlovable, they look like solutions. When he is criticised or left out, his reflex is to conclude that he is a nobody, blaming not what he did but who he is.

Arrogance, he argues, is the same wound from the other side: a pedestal built so no one sees how little we think of ourselves. Inflated or deflated, we lose touch with the truth.

Plenty of people loved him well, he admits, and it never quite convinced him: would they still, if they saw everything? So he kept waiting for some job, trip or relationship to deliver the feeling, a hunt he says ends in burnout.

The alternative is to listen. He compares it to finding damp ground in a desert: once you touch it, you want to dig, or rather to clear away the dry sand that already covers a well.

Two kinds of voice

Schematic of the argument in “Being the Beloved”. The loud voices are paraphrased. Nouwen's point is that both arrogance and low self-esteem grow from the same root, and that worldly success is attractive mainly as a remedy for it.

Becoming the Beloved · Four movements

From being to becoming

Knowing you are beloved changes nothing while it stays a lovely idea. The work is to let it show up in how you eat, talk, work and love, among rent, phone calls and errands.

Our age can describe the psyche in detail; Nouwen wants the same precision for the movements of the spirit. Citing Augustine's restless heart, he argues we can only search for what we have already tasted. Becoming the Beloved means closing the gap between that memory and Tuesday afternoon.

ITaken

Chosen, without anyone left out

God's choosing is compassionate, not competitive. Being chosen includes others instead of ranking you above them.

  • “Taken” sounds cold, so he swaps in a warmer word: chosen. Seen as unique and precious long before anyone praised or hurt you.
  • He knows the word is loaded for Fred, whose parents came to America in the shadow of pogroms back in the old country, and who knows that being called the chosen people can bring persecution as well as admiration. Nouwen felt something similar as a priest set apart, and joked that the “J. M.” in his name was his way of saying he was “just me”.
  • Every memory of being chosen in this world is tied to someone else not being chosen: the soccer team, the Boy Scout patrol, the awards. That is why the word makes us think of elites. He asks Fred not to give it up anyway, because losing touch with your chosenness opens the door to self-rejection.
  • The dark voices say you are one more mouth to feed. Many children grow up unsure they were wanted. The world, he notes, finds insecure people easier to use.

Three disciplines for claiming chosenness

1 · Unmask the worldWhen you feel hurt or rejected, tell yourself the feeling is strong but not true. The world's verdicts are manipulative.
2 · Find remindersSeek people and places that keep telling you who you really are: synagogues, churches, support groups, family, friends, teachers.
3 · Celebrate itSay thank you. Every good moment can also be read with suspicion, so gratitude is a decision, and it gets easier each time.

On gratitude he points to the core members at Daybreak, people with mental disabilities who have many reasons for bitterness (loneliness, rejection by family, needing help with everything) and mostly choose gratitude anyway.

The story · Helen

When Helen arrived at Daybreak she made only noises and no contact, and Nouwen kept his distance, half afraid of her. As the community trusted that she too had a gift, she began to smile and became a joy to everyone. He realised he could only see her goodness once he had claimed his own. Friendship with Fred went the same way: early rivalry faded as each grew sure of his own worth.

IIBlessed

A blessing creates what it speaks

To bless is to say good things of someone, and to say yes to their belovedness beyond any tally of their virtues or faults.

  • Fred took him to a synagogue where a thirteen-year-old was having his bar mitzvah. What moved Nouwen most was the father telling his son, in front of the congregation, that whatever happened, success or failure, health or sickness, his parents would always love him.
  • The Latin benedicere means to speak well of. A blessing is more than praise. It goes beneath the split between admiration and condemnation and touches someone's original goodness.
  • The patriarchs and matriarchs, and Jesus at his baptism, heard such a blessing and held on to it. Our mood swings, he suggests, show we have stopped hearing ours.
  • Many people feel cursed instead. The curses are loud, but they are lies.
  • You live either under blessing or under curse; there is no middle ground. You choose where to live, moment by moment. And those who know they are blessed start blessing others without effort.

Two ways to claim the blessing

Prayer
  • Less talking to God, more listening to the voice that speaks well of you
  • Half an hour a day, even if it feels like nothing happens
  • Repeat a memorised text slowly, as Eknath Easwaran taught; Nouwen used the prayer of St Francis
  • Return to the text when the mind wanders
Presence
  • Notice the blessings already arriving, every day
  • Stop brushing compliments aside with “it's nothing”
  • Be willing to “waste” time with people
  • Receive from the poor, from trees and music, from words of love

Prayer, he warns, sounds self-indulgent but is hard: the fear of hearing you are not good enough makes you fill the silence. Perhaps few people give real blessings, he adds, because so few are present enough to receive them.

Adam

A Daybreak member who cannot speak, walk alone, eat without help or dress himself. Nouwen says he has never met anyone who spent real time with Adam and did not come away feeling blessed. The blessing comes from simple presence, which busy people find hardest of all.

The story · Janet's real blessing

Before a prayer service, Janet, a member of the community, asked for a blessing. Nouwen traced a cross on her forehead; she protested that she wanted a real one. Later, with about thirty people on the floor, she came up and hugged him. He wrapped her in the sleeves of his robe and told her she was God's beloved daughter, naming her smile and kindness. Others followed, and finally John, a twenty-four-year-old assistant, who was moved to tears.

IIIBroken

Befriend the wound, then put it under the blessing

Your brokenness is as personal as your chosenness. What hurts most is not the pain itself but reading it as proof that you are worthless.

  • Nouwen and Fred talked about brokenness from the first meeting: his loneliness and fears, Fred's frustration with his job. Later came Fred's divorce and Nouwen's long depression.
  • Each person suffers in a way no one else does. He is more grateful to someone who admits he is alone in his pain than to someone who says others have it worse.
  • In his world the dominant suffering is the broken heart: broken relationships and feeling rejected or useless. At Daybreak the heaviest burden is not the disability but feeling of no value to anyone.
  • He sees the hunger for communion most painfully in sexuality, amid a fragmented, commercial culture. Writing at the time of AIDS, he calls it a telling symptom: young people risking their lives for intimacy, and friends forming generous communities around the dying.
  • He once depended completely on one person's affection, near to a self-destructive depression. It changed when he could read the dependency as a call to trust a love he could rely on without fear. Twelve-step groups, he says, do something similar.

The same blow, lived two ways

Schematic of Nouwen's two responses. He calls putting pain under the blessing the precondition for befriending it: you can only face a wound if it doesn't confirm a curse. Neither response makes the pain smaller, he stresses. It often makes the wound feel deeper.

His comparison is a runner, who can feel pain and joy at once while nearing the finish. Joy and sorrow become two sides of one desire.

The night Fred's marriage ended

Nouwen happened to be in town. Reassurance would have been useless, so he simply stayed, and urged Fred not to run from the pain but to stand in it. Years later Fred could say he had, and grown stronger.

The story · Bernstein's broken chalice

Two years before writing, Nouwen and Fred heard Leonard Bernstein conduct Tchaikovsky at Lincoln Center; it turned out to be their last chance. Bernstein had been his great music teacher since his teens. Near the end of Bernstein's Mass, written in memory of John F. Kennedy, a richly robed priest is lifted high by the crowd, falls, and his glass chalice shatters. Stripped to jeans and a T-shirt, he stares at the shards while children sing, and finally says it.

“I never realized that broken glass could shine so brightly.”
The priest in Bernstein's Mass, as quoted by Nouwen
IVGiven

Broken so as to be given

The first three movements only make full sense in the fourth. We are chosen, blessed and broken for others, in life and in death.

  • Beneath the wish to be rewarded, Nouwen sees a plain desire to give, like a mother delighted by her baby's smile, or Nouwen himself spending hours in Dutch shops hunting for a birthday present for his father or mother.
  • He saw it at Fred's second wedding, to Robin, a lawyer for the poor and homeless, on a sunny May weekend with Jewish rituals led by Rabbi Helene Ferris. Fred had come through a long loneliness and knew the gift of himself would have to be renewed every day.
  • Brokenness and giving are linked. The years when he and Fred felt they had nothing to offer turned out to make them able to give more. That is no excuse to hurt anyone: only a fool, he says, breaks glass to make it shine.
  • A shared meal is the clearest picture: breaking and giving bread are one act, and at the table we are unarmed. Under the wish to eat together, he thinks, lies the wish to be food for each other.

Giving yourself in death

If we are broken to be given, our final brokenness can be a final gift. Society hides death and barely leaves time to mourn. Nouwen insists we choose, every day, between clinging to life so that death is pure failure and letting go so that we can still be a source of hope.

After death the wounds that limited someone's love fall away. Francis of Assisi died in 1226 and is, he says, still bearing fruit nearly eight centuries on.

Two friends, two good deaths

Murray McDonnellDied suddenly of heart failure. His last five years had been a preparation: more open with his wife Peggy and their nine children, at peace with old struggles. A year on, his family gathered with stories of the hope he had left.
Pauline VanierDied at ninety-three. Widow of a former governor-general of Canada, she joined her son Jean's community of the weak and became “Mammie” to many, including Nouwen during the year he lived in her house.
The story · Elisha's loaves

A servant protests that Elisha's twenty barley loaves can't feed a hundred men. Elisha says to serve them anyway; everyone eats and there are leftovers. For Nouwen this is the spiritual life in miniature: small lives, once given, multiply far beyond what we can see, like a mustard seed or a stone dropped in a still pond.

Living as the Beloved

Stay in the city. Just don't serve it.

Nouwen doesn't tell Fred to leave New York for a monastery or for L'Arche. Ambition, good food and good clothes, a concert at Lincoln Center: none of it is the enemy.

The struggle is to live in the world without belonging to it. All the good things on offer are Fred's to enjoy, as long as he receives them as signs of his belovedness rather than as proof of his worth. The same truth will let him drop whatever starts to distract or enslave him.

Nouwen asks Fred to think of himself as sent: loved before the world began and sent into it for a short time. Then life stops being a long test you are bound to fail and becomes a string of chances to say yes. God not only tells us we are beloved, he writes, but asks whether we love in return, and every event, from praise and rejection to illness and dying, carries that question.

Seen this way, opposites lose their edge. He notes that “diabolic” literally means dividing; wherever division gives way to reconciliation, inside a person or between religions, he sees the Spirit at work.

Eternal life, then, is not a surprise at the end but the full showing of what we have lived all along. He fears death when he listens to the voices that say his little life is all he has, and not when he listens to the other one.

What stops being opposite

joy~pain
winning a prize~falling short

Each pair, he argues, offers the same chance to say yes to being beloved, so each is more alike than different. He backs this with a letter from novelist Julien Green to the philosopher Jacques Maritain, which suggests that at a high enough level joy and suffering are one phenomenon, as extreme cold burns.

“The world is evil only when you become its slave.”

Epilogue · A Friendship Deepens

The book failed its first reader

The most unusual part of the book is that Nouwen prints his friend's verdict on it, and the verdict is no.

Fred came to Toronto to talk the manuscript over. A decade on, both men were steadier: Nouwen had found a home at L'Arche, Fred was happily married, expecting his first child, and writing. He had published two books for teenagers and was working on a novel in the early mornings.

But Fred was clear. He had shown the text to two friends and it hadn't touched them. It was, he said, still writing for the converted. Before talk of being beloved could mean anything, secular readers needed answers to more basic questions: who God is, who I am, why I'm here, how anyone comes to faith. Nouwen compares himself to a man so excited about sailing that he forgets his listeners have never seen water.

The deeper issue, he concedes, is whether anything still feels sacred at all. He won't try to prove God; he can only say what God is for him. Perhaps the gap must be bridged inside himself, since he too is more secular than he admits.

Then the irony arrived. Gordon Cosby and Diana Chambers of the Servant Leadership School in Washington, D.C., found it more helpful than his earlier books and built a course on it. Bart Gavigan of the South Park Community in England was just as enthusiastic. Fred hadn't got the book he needed, they told Nouwen, but he had made it possible for them to get theirs. Nouwen published it largely as it was.

Who the book reached

Written for
  • Fred, a secular Jew in New York
  • His friends: young, ambitious, unchurched
  • Result: “good stuff, but not for me”
Helped most
  • Searching Christians in Washington and England
  • A new course on the life of the Beloved
  • Result: urged him to change little

Nouwen reads the irony as God using a secular friend to teach believers. The open question he leaves, where the sacred can be found again, is the one he admits the book doesn't answer.

Reflective practices

Practices for each movement

The book is a letter, not a manual, but each chapter names concrete disciplines. These are the ones Nouwen stresses, grouped by movement.

Taken

Talk back to the hurtWhen rejected, tell yourself the feeling is real but not the truth about you.
Find your remindersKeep company with people and communities who speak your worth back to you.
Choose gratitudeEvery good thing can be read cynically. Decide not to, and notice it gets easier.
Look for others' chosennessBeing chosen should open your eyes to theirs, not lift you above them.

Blessed

Listen for half an hourDaily, in silence, for the voice that speaks well of you, even when nothing seems to happen.
Use a text you know by heartRepeat a psalm or prayer slowly, and come back to it when your mind drifts.
Receive complimentsStop waving off good words. Be present enough to take them in.
Bless out loudSay good things to and about people, with your arms as well as words.

Broken

Step toward the painHealing starts by moving closer to it, not away, ideally with someone beside you.
Refuse the curse readingWhen something goes wrong, catch the thought that it proves you are worthless.
Don't compare sufferingAcknowledge that each person's pain is their own instead of ranking it.
Name your dependenciesAdmitting them openly, as twelve-step groups do, can turn them into hope.

Given

Ask who, not whatAsk who you can be for someone, not only what you can do for them.
Break breadShare meals. They are the plainest form of giving yourself.
Mourn properlyTake time when someone dies instead of carrying on as if nothing happened.
Prepare your death as a giftPractise letting go daily, so that what you leave is hope, not bitterness.
