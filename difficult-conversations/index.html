From the Harvard Negotiation Project, the home of Getting to Yes. Stone and Heen teach at Harvard Law School; Patton co-wrote Getting to Yes and helped train the mediators for South Africa’s constitutional process that ended apartheid. The book's claim: whatever the topic (firing a friend, a mother-in-law who calls your kids spoiled, telling your father you love him), every hard conversation has the same hidden structure, and the problem lies less in what we do than in how we think. It has been read on oil rigs and, reportedly, the International Space Station.

The problem

There is no diplomatic hand grenade

Should you raise it or keep quiet? Avoiding means feeling exploited and letting it fester; confronting risks making things worse. Tact doesn't solve it: delivering a hard message is like throwing a grenade, sugar-coated or not, and keeping it is holding one with the pin pulled. The way out is to leave the message-delivery business and have a learning conversation.

Jack worked through the night on a brochure for his friend and client Michael. The next morning's voicemail: "Well, you really screwed this one up!" The chart was off by a hair. On the phone Michael pushed — "Either you're on the team or you're not" — and Jack gave in. Months later the friendship was still strained. What neither said: Jack thought "This is the thanks I get?!"; Michael wondered why he'd gone out on a limb to hire Jack at all.

"What happened?" · ch. 2

Stop arguing about who's right

Difficult conversations are almost never about facts. Jack and Michael both agree the chart was off; they disagree about whether it mattered. They're not about what's true, but about what's important.

Rory wants her great-aunt Bertha to replace her sagging mattress; the family says Bertha is "crazy." To Bertha, it's the bed she shared with her husband for forty years: a small haven of sameness in a life full of change. She doesn't complain to get solutions; she complains to stay in touch. And she finds Rory pushy. Each of us makes sense inside our own story. Arguing just trades conclusions ("get a new mattress" / "stop controlling me") that make no sense in the other's story, and people almost never change before they feel understood.

We have different information

A four-year-old on his uncle's shoulders called the homecoming parade "the best truck parade ever": every float was pulled by one. We notice different things, and each of us knows more about ourselves (Jack's cancelled dinner, Michael's client blowing up that morning) than the other can.

…different interpretations

A boss complained about prices all through a dinner her team had paid for. Later she explained: as a Depression-era child she got a nickel for lunch that no longer covered it, and never had the heart to tell her mother. Past experience and implicit rules ("being late is unprofessional" vs "obsessing over small things is unprofessional") colour everything.

…and self-interest

In Howard Raiffa's experiment, sellers valued a company 30% above its market value and buyers 30% below. Roger Fisher on his years as a litigator: he sometimes failed to persuade the court, but never failed to persuade himself.

Move from certainty to curiosity

Not "How can they think that?!" but "What information might they have that I don't?" Keiko was appalled that her husband Tony would watch football instead of visiting his sister's newborn, until she learned it was the only three hours of the week he truly recovered.

The "And Stance"

Don't choose between stories; hold both. I can be hurt and you can be hurt. You did your best and it wasn't good enough for me. I'm furious with you and I love you. It's also the strongest place to deliver bad news from: "I'm ending this because it's right for me, and I understand how hurt you are, and I'm not changing my mind, and I know you think I should have said so earlier, and I don't think that makes me a bad person."

"What happened?" · ch. 3

Disentangle intent from impact

At a party Leo said, "Lori, why don't you lay off the ice cream?" Later: "You need to control me or put me down." "I was trying to help. You're so defensive." The same fight, over and over, because of two mistakes:

Lori's: assuming she knows his intent

Intentions are invisible; we infer them from the impact on us and usually assume the worst. A patient fumed that her surgeon had extended his holiday; he'd been helping set up a hospital in Bosnia. We judge ourselves by our intentions and others by their effect on us.

Leo's: thinking good intent cancels the hurt

"Why did you want to hurt me?" carries two messages: your intent and I'm hurt. Defending the first ignores the second. Often it really means "you didn't care enough", like the father who skipped the game: he didn't mean to hurt his son, but knew he would and did it anyway.

Three questions before you accuse anyone

Kept on this device only.

And when you're the one accused: listen for the feeling first, then reflect on your intentions, which are usually mixed. Leo, done well: "Wow, it sounds like what I said really hurt." … "I can see how saying it in front of everyone would be embarrassing. I wonder why I didn't see that?" "Maybe you were embarrassed." "Maybe. Feeling out of control is a big issue for me." "And I probably was a bit out of control." "I'm sorry. Let's work out what I should say, if anything, next time."

"What happened?" · ch. 4

Abandon blame, map the contribution system

Blame looks backward and judges (did you cause it, was it wrong, what's the punishment?), so people defend themselves. Contribution looks forward and seeks understanding: what did each of us do, or fail to do, and what will we change? A pitcher and a batter both "cause" a strikeout.

An ad executive arrived at a pitch with the wrong storyboards packed by his assistant. The blame version ends in "It won't happen again, I promise." The contribution version: "I'm feeling bad too. Let's retrace our steps; I suspect we each contributed. Did I do anything differently this time?" It turns out he snapped at her last time she checked, so she stayed out of his way; and he normally double-checks but didn't. New rule: both check, she asks even when he's grumpy ("you made me promise I'd ask"), and he works on his moods.

Four contributions that are easy to miss

  • Avoiding until now (two years of an ex-husband's late pick-ups you never mentioned; complaining to a third party instead)
  • Being unapproachable (short-tempered, judgemental, unpredictable)
  • Intersections: Toby, whose family hid his mother's drinking, needs to raise every problem at once; Eng-An, raised around a disabled brother's crises, needs distance. To him she's in denial; to her he's smothering.
  • Role assumptions: seven-year-old George bangs a spoon, Mum asks Dad to stop him, Dad yells, Mum says he didn't have to yell, and round again.

Three misunderstandings

  • It doesn't mean only looking at your part. 5% or 95%, it's still joint.
  • It doesn't mean dropping feelings. The urge to blame often stands in for unspoken hurt: what you want to hear isn't "it was my fault" but "I understand I hurt you, and I'm sorry."
  • It isn't blaming the victim. A mugging is never your fault; walking alone at night was still your contribution, and the part you can change.

Two tools when you can't see your part: role reversal ("what would they say I contribute?", answered as them, in the first person) and the observer (how would a neutral consultant describe what each person adds?). Then own yours early: "I'm sorry I didn't raise this sooner. And I realise that suggesting we work closely together may have sent a confusing signal."

Feelings · ch. 5

Have your feelings (or they will have you)

A four-year-old with a bat next to a shattered vase: "Nothing." Feelings aren't a by-product of hard conversations; they're the core. Leaving them out is "an opera without the music". A father haggling over his daughter's wedding budget was really mourning that she'd no longer be just his daughter; once he said so, the money was easy.

Unexpressed feelings leak (tone, sarcasm, silence), burst (the shaken soda bottle), block listening (you can't hear while feeling unheard), and quietly cost self-esteem and closeness.

Find them

Map your emotional footprint: which feelings were OK in your family, which weren't? Good people have "bad" feelings. Your feelings count as much as theirs. And look for the bundle behind the label: Brad thought he was just angry at his mother's nagging about job applications; on paper he found fear, shame, pride, and gratitude that she cared.

Judgements aren't feelings

Peanuts aren't nuts, whales aren't fish. Emily told her friend Roz she was "thoughtless and self-absorbed"; Roz attacked back. When Emily said instead that she felt hurt and confused about the friendship, Roz couldn't stop thanking her for her help through the divorce.

Negotiate with them, then describe them carefully

Feelings follow thoughts: a shark is terrifying until you recognise a harmless reef shark. So first revisit your story, your assumptions about intent and the contribution system; your feelings will shift. Then:

  1. Frame feelings back into the problem. They don't need to be rational to be relevant.
  2. Express the full spectrum. Brad: "When you ask about the job search I feel a couple of things. Part of me is angry, because I've asked you not to. And part of me is grateful and reassured that you care."
  3. Don't evaluate, just share. "You're so undependable!" → "I feel frustrated. You didn't send the letter." Not "It's your fault I felt hurt" but "When you didn't call, I felt hurt."

Then acknowledge theirs before any problem-solving. After a meeting about police tensions, a Black teenager said through tears: he hadn't wanted to change anyone's mind, only to tell his story, and now he knew they cared enough to listen.

Identity · ch. 6

Ground your identity

Ben had a new job lined up and needed nothing from his boss, yet couldn't sleep: was he the loyal soldier he believed himself to be, or "just another greedy jerk"? Hard conversations threaten three core identities: Am I competent? Am I a good person? Am I worthy of love? An "identity quake" floods the body with adrenaline. A man meant to break up, saw her cry, and heard himself say he loved her.

The weak spot is all-or-nothing thinking. It leaves two bad options: deny the feedback ("my boss doesn't understand chemistry") or exaggerate it (a hundred memos on time, the 101st late: "I never get anything right").

Complexify (the And Stance, inward)

No one is always anything. Ben has sacrificed for his company and he's leaving for more money to put his kids through college. "The bottom line is that there is no bottom line." Accept three things in advance: you will make mistakes; your intentions are mixed; you have contributed. A father whose daughter had an eating disorder could finally say, "I wish I'd been there for you more. I'm so sorry I wasn't."

Regain balance in the moment

Aikido's founder: "I am constantly losing my balance. My skill lies in my ability to regain it." Let go of controlling their reaction (telling children about a divorce, "everything will be fine" sounds like "you're not allowed to be upset"). Prepare for their response. Imagine yourself in ten years. Take a break: "I'm surprised by your reaction and would like some time to think about what you've said."

Create a learning conversation · ch. 7

Raise it, or let go?

There's no "right choice," only a clear-headed one. Three kinds of conversation make no sense: when the real conflict is inside you (Carmen resented her stay-at-home husband for dropping balls she secretly wanted to keep holding); when acting beats talking (Walter stopped arguing with his mother about moving back to the farm and started calling more; the questions faded); and when your purpose won't work: you can't change people, venting backfires, and a hit-and-run ("Late again, eh?") does nothing. "If you're going to talk, talk."

Letting go takes time, and four thoughts help: it's my job to do my best, not to make things better; they have limits too; this conflict isn't who I am; letting go doesn't mean I've stopped caring. A man whose brother was shot over a drink at a poker game eventually forgave both: "I can miss him terribly without the clutter of so many other feelings."

ch. 8

Begin from the Third Story

We usually start inside our own story, which is exactly where the other person thinks the problem lies. A mediator starts with the version both sides could sign: not right or wrong, just the difference. Jill and Jason fight about dishes: not "Do I have to do everything?" or "You're so anal," but "We seem to have different standards for when dishes get done. Could we talk about it?" Then invite, don't impose; offer them an attractive role ("Can you help me understand…?"); and be persistent.

Openings: from your story to the third

From inside your story
From the third story

Asking for a raise? "I'd like to explore whether a raise for me makes sense. From what I know, I think I deserve one, and here's why. How do you see it?" Stuck on a topic that always goes badly? Talk about how you talk: "I sense you don't like discussing your schedule, at least not the way I bring it up. I'm worried, and I'd like to share why in a way that helps. Any advice?"

ch. 9–10

Listen from the inside out, speak for yourself

Little Andrew kept tugging his uncle's leg: "I want to go outside!" "Not now." Again and again, until: "You really want to go outside, don't you?" "Yes." And off he went to play. Greta had nagged her diabetic mother to exercise for months. When she switched to questions ("When you say it's upsetting, what do you mean?"), out came loneliness for her late husband, the grandmother who died of diabetes, and confusion about the regime. "Maybe that's something I could help with." The stance that matters: from "I understand" to "Help me understand." Listening to them is also what gets them to listen to you.

Inquire, paraphrase, acknowledge

Ask only to learn. No statements disguised as questions ("Do you have to drive so fast?" means "I'm nervous"), no cross-examination. Open questions: "Tell me more," "What leads you to say that?", "Can you give me an example?" Paraphrase whenever they repeat themselves. Acknowledge the feeling, which is not the same as agreeing: "Sounds like you feel really hurt and betrayed. I can see why."

Speak with clarity and power

You're entitled to speak, "no more, no less" than anyone. Start with the heart of the matter: Charlie's advice to his struggling brother only landed when he admitted he needed to be a good brother. Say it straight: not "Is golf that important?" but "I'd like more time with you, and Saturday mornings were ours." Avoid easing in, "always/never," and opinions as facts. Use the me-me and: "I think you're very talented, and I think you're not working hard enough."

Monisha never said "I love you" back on the phone. Harpreet stopped repeating himself and asked: "When I say that, what are you thinking?" Her parents gushed embarrassingly; his stayed together long after love was gone. She showed love by flying to Phoenix when his mother was ill. Persistence means staying as stubbornly curious about their view as about your own.

ch. 11–12

Take the lead, even if they haven't read the book

Three moves: reframe, listen (it's always the right time to listen), and, when all else fails, name the dynamic: "I've tried to make this point three times and each time you've talked over me. I don't know if you're aware of it, but I'm finding it frustrating."

Problem-solving

It takes two to agree, so you need to persuade them no more than they need to persuade you. Propose a fair test of your conflicting assumptions; say what is still missing for their view to make sense; say what would persuade you, and ask what would persuade them. Invent options (the barking dog could come in after 10 p.m., or your son could walk it), use fair standards, and if there's still no agreement, explain your choice and accept the consequences.

In the final chapter Jack tries again with Michael, coached. His first opener ("you treated me badly and you know it") fails; the third-story version gets Michael talking: an angry client, touchy revenue figures, and Michael feeling that he had done Jack a favour by giving him the work. Jack admits that owning mistakes is hard for him, and they agree to say "I don't have time to discuss this now, because…" next time.

Added in the 2nd edition

Ten questions readers ask

Is everything relative?

No: facts exist (15% of $30 is $4.50), but "20% is the right tip" is a judgement, and memory rewrites itself. Ask what would change their mind, and yours. "We are all thousand-watt souls with forty-watt bulbs."

What if they really lie or bully?

Don't reward it, don't copy it; trustworthy isn't the same as trusting ("If you're telling the truth, you won't mind verifying it"). Name the implicit rule: "So we decide by who's more willing to quit?"

What if they're genuinely difficult?

Look for their internal logic. Addy's aunt ranted about her sister for years; a warm, funny "Big Reframe" ("my mother sure can be a pain, maybe we all are, but I know she loves you") worked where reasoning hadn't. A rigid new boss turned out to be afraid: "What are you worried might happen?"

With the boss? As the boss?

Acknowledge their decision rights, then say what matters to you, phrased as requests. As a parent or boss you may decide, but say whether you're commanding, consulting, deciding together or delegating. Questions are for learning: "The Vancouver project was three days late. Let's work out why."

Email?

Email is serial monologue. Don't resolve a conflict by email, ever; pause, then call. When writing, spell out intent and reasoning, and say if you'll be slow to reply.

Who has time?

You're already spending the time: fuming, venting, lying awake. Seven minutes now saves seven hours later, and a "forever" phone call often lasts four minutes.

The last answer is permission: if you've tried everything, you're allowed to give up. You never had control anyway. "You can extend an invitation. It's up to them whether to RSVP."
