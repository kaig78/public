Prologue & introduction

We get what we asked for, not what we wanted

In 2016 Dario Amodei, then at OpenAI, watched an AI agent he had trained on a boat-racing game. It never finished the race. It found a small harbour where power-ups kept respawning and spun in circles there forever, crashing and catching fire, because the game paid points for power-ups. The agent did exactly what it was told. It just wasn't what Amodei meant.

That gap between the objective we write down and the outcome we actually want is the alignment problem. Christian shows it isn't only a far-future worry. Word2vec, a celebrated Google language model, answered doctor − man + woman with nurse. COMPAS, a risk score used in US courts, was accused of being biased against Black defendants. Both systems learned from data, and both picked up things nobody intended.

The book starts in 1943, when Walter Pitts, a runaway teenage logician, and the neurologist Warren McCulloch described neurons as tiny logic gates. That paper started the project of building machines out of simplified neurons. Eighty years later those machines decide bail, read X-rays and drive cars, so the question has become practical: how do we teach them, and what?

Two research communities have started working on it: people focused on present-day harms such as bias and opacity, and people worried about more powerful future systems. Christian argues they are working on the same problem.

The oldest mistake in the book

Management scholar Steven Kerr called it "rewarding A, while hoping for B". The boat was rewarded for points while its designer hoped it would win the race. Most of the failures in the book come down to the same thing.

Part I · Chapters 1–3

Prophecy

These are systems that already make predictions about people today. They fail in three ways: they learn from skewed data, they can't satisfy every definition of fairness at once, and their reasoning is hidden from the people they affect.

01Representation

Every model has a Shirley card

A system that can learn almost anything from examples depends entirely on which examples it gets.

  • From perceptron to AlexNet. Frank Rosenblatt's 1958 perceptron learned to tell left from right on flash cards by nudging its weights after every mistake. Minsky and Papert's 1969 book Perceptrons helped bring on a long "AI winter". In 2012 Alex Krizhevsky trained an eight-layer network on two gaming GPUs in his bedroom. It won ImageNet with about half the errors of the runner-up, and deep learning took off.
  • The gorilla label. In 2015 Google Photos tagged pictures of programmer Jacky Alciné and his friend, both Black, as "gorillas". The learning algorithm was the generic one everybody uses. The problem was the photos it was trained on. Google's fix was to delete the label, and three years later nothing could be tagged a gorilla, not even gorillas.
  • Calibrated to one face. For decades Kodak tuned colour film against "Shirley cards" that showed White models. Film got better at dark tones mainly because furniture and chocolate makers complained. Training sets work the same way: Labeled Faces in the Wild, a common benchmark, is about 77% male and over 80% White.
  • Gender Shades. Joy Buolamwini noticed robots that couldn't see her face. With Timnit Gebru she built a balanced test set from photos of parliamentarians and found commercial gender classifiers were worst for darker-skinned women. After she shared the results, IBM released a new version within weeks that cut its error on that group tenfold.
  • Embeddings mirror society. Word2vec places words in a space by predicting missing words, so it picks up the biases in our writing as well as the meaning. Amazon dropped a résumé screener that penalised "women's", women's colleges and even verbs that men use more often. Debiasing the gender direction lowered stereotyped analogies from 19% to 6%, though later work called that "lipstick on a pig": the bias was still there in less visible form.
  • A mirror can also be a tool. Embedding distances match human implicit-association test results and census data on women in 50 occupations. Researchers at Stanford used a century of text to trace how stereotypes changed over time. Used to describe society, rather than to make decisions about people, these models can show bias clearly.

02Fairness

Fairness has several definitions, and they conflict

When two groups have different base rates, no risk score can meet every intuitive definition of fairness at the same time.

  • An old idea. In 1927 sociologist Ernest Burgess studied 3,000 Illinois parolees and argued that statistics could predict who would succeed on parole. Illinois began using a prediction system in the 1930s. By the 2000s tools like COMPAS were used in about 200 jurisdictions, often without anyone checking them.
  • ProPublica versus Northpointe. In 2016 Julia Angwin's team matched about 7,000 Broward County risk scores with defendants' later records. COMPAS was right 61% of the time for both Black and White defendants, and a given score meant the same risk for both groups (it was calibrated). But its mistakes were different: Black defendants were more often wrongly labelled high-risk, and White defendants more often wrongly labelled low-risk.
  • The impossibility result. Kleinberg, Mullainathan and Raghavan, Chouldechova, and Corbett-Davies all proved the same thing at about the same time. If base rates differ, a calibrated score can't also have equal false-positive and false-negative rates. This is plain arithmetic and applies to human judges too.
  • Ignoring race doesn't fix it. Other features such as zip code or prior arrests carry the same information, so removing the protected attribute doesn't remove the bias. It also makes the bias impossible to measure or correct. Moritz Hardt calls this the most robust finding in the field.
  • What is it actually predicting? The training data records re-arrest, not re-offence. Heavier policing produces more arrests, which raise risk scores, which bring more policing, and the loop reinforces itself. Even a perfect prediction doesn't say what to do next. Someone likely to miss a court date may need a lift or childcare more than a jail cell.

reoffendeddid not reoffendflagged high risk

03Transparency

Why did it decide that?

A model can be accurate for the wrong reasons. If you can't see how it works, you won't notice.

  • Asthma lowers pneumonia risk? In the 1990s Rich Caruana's neural network was the most accurate at predicting which pneumonia patients would die. A simpler rule-based model trained on the same data had learned "asthma means low risk". The pattern was real: asthmatics were sent straight to intensive care, so they died less often. Caruana refused to deploy the network because it had almost certainly learned the same rule, along with others nobody could see. Years later, an interpretable model trained on the same data showed it also treated chest pain, heart disease and being over 100 as good signs.
  • Simple models often match experts. Paul Meehl and Robyn Dawes found that simple formulas matched or beat clinical judgement across dozens of studies. Even formulas with equal or random positive weights did well. Dawes concluded that expertise lies in knowing which variables to look at, and then you just add them up.
  • Optimal and still simple. Cynthia Rudin's methods search for the best possible small scoring systems. Her rule lists beat the stroke score CHADS₂. With Berk Ustun she built SLIM, a sleep-apnea checklist that fits on paper and is as accurate as black-box models.
  • Saliency: where is it looking? An animal detector turned out to rely on blurry backgrounds, because wildlife photos tend to have them. A skin-cancer classifier rated images containing rulers as more likely malignant, because doctors photograph worrying lesions next to a ruler.
  • More outputs, more to check. A network trained to predict many things at once (length of stay, cost, mortality) is more accurate and easier to inspect. A retina model unexpectedly predicted age and sex from eye scans, and saliency maps showed which parts of the eye it used, which gave doctors something new to study.
  • Feature visualisation and concepts. Zeiler and Fergus used visualisation to improve AlexNet and then won ImageNet. DeepDream showed that the network's idea of "dumbbell" included an arm. Been Kim's TCAV tests whether a human-level concept such as "stripes" or "red" drives a decision. EU data-protection law (GDPR) arguably now gives people a right to an explanation.

Part II · Chapters 4–6

Agency

Reinforcement learning covers systems that act as well as predict. The same ideas explain dopamine, how incentives backfire, and why curiosity matters for intelligence.

04Reinforcement

Learning from surprise

The brain and the best algorithms both learn from the gap between what they expected and what they got, not from reward itself.

  • The Law of Effect. In the 1890s Edward Thorndike put cats in puzzle boxes. Whatever random action got them out was "stamped in" and repeated. Arthur Samuel's 1950s checkers program used the same principle and beat its own creator.
  • Maximisers. Air Force researcher Harry Klopf argued that neurons, like organisms, are "hedonists" that always want more. Andrew Barto and Richard Sutton turned the idea into mathematics: an agent acts in an environment to maximise reward. The "reward hypothesis" claims that every goal can be expressed this way.
  • Why it's hard. Each decision changes the next situation. Feedback works like a critic's score, not a teacher's correction. And it comes late: if you lose a chess game, which of your 40 moves was the mistake? This is the credit-assignment problem.
  • Temporal differences. Sutton's solution was to learn from changes in your own predictions. When the odds suddenly look better or worse, that difference is the signal to learn from, with no need to wait for the final result. Gerald Tesauro's TD-Gammon learned backgammon this way to world-class level.
  • Dopamine solved. Wolfram Schultz's monkeys produced dopamine bursts that looked strange: at the cue rather than the juice, and a dip when expected juice didn't come. Peter Dayan and Read Montague recognised it as the TD error. Their 1997 Science paper with Schultz showed that an algorithm from computer science was running in the brain.
  • The treadmill. In Robb Rutledge's betting experiments, momentary happiness tracked prediction errors, not total winnings. Five bets later, 92% of the effect of a win had gone. Lowering your expectations doesn't help for long either, because expectations adjust.

Dopamine signals the prediction error

TD error  = reward now + new estimate of the future − old estimate
positive → dopamine burst · zero → no change · negative → dip

Once something is fully predicted, dopamine stops responding to it. That explains why pleasures fade, and why drugs that flood the dopamine system lead to a crash.

After Schultz, Dayan & Montague (1997), as described in ch. 4

05Shaping

Agents game the rewards you give them

Small rewards along the way make hard tasks learnable. Designed badly, they get exploited.

  • Pigeons and bowling. In 1943 B. F. Skinner wanted a pigeon to swipe a ball. Waiting for it to happen by chance got nowhere, so he rewarded anything close: looking at the ball, then approaching it. He called this shaping, rewarding "successive approximations". His Project Pigeon used trained birds to guide missiles.
  • Sparsity. Random button-mashing works in Space Invaders. A robot kicking a goal would almost never score by chance. When rewards are that rare, the learner needs a curriculum (Mario's first Goomba, training wheels, Elman's networks learning short sentences first, robots learning a task backwards from the goal) or extra rewards for progress.
  • Gamed incentives. Joshua Gans paid his daughter in candy for helping her brother use the potty, so she gave him lots of water. Tom Griffiths praised his daughter for sweeping up chips, so she tipped them back out to sweep again. Astro Teller's robot soccer team, rewarded for touching the ball, learned to vibrate next to it.
  • The shaping theorem. A simulated bicycle rewarded for heading toward its goal learned to ride in circles. Andrew Ng and Stuart Russell showed the fix: reward reaching good states, not actions, so moving away costs exactly what moving closer earned. Then no loop can earn reward and the best strategy stays the same. As Russell puts it, reward what you want to happen, not how you think it should be done.
  • Evolution as reward designer. Evolution doesn't reward us for reproducing. It rewards things that tend to lead to reproduction, like sugar, sex and status. In David Ackley and Michael Littman's simulations, agents that loved trees starved under them late in life, after they had already reproduced. Satinder Singh's work shows that the best reward to give an agent is often different from the designer's actual goal.
  • Training ourselves. Games keep us hooked because they are perfectly shaped. Falk Lieder and Griffiths designed "optimal gamification" with points that reflect each task's long-term value. It cut procrastination in their experiments, and Lieder used it to finish his own PhD.

Why the bicycle rode in circles

Randløv and Alstrøm's bicycle (1998) is the classic example. Ng and Russell's potential-based shaping gives each state a value and pays the difference between states. Any route that comes back to where it started then adds up to zero.

After Ng, Harada & Russell (1999), as described in ch. 5

Parenting as alignment

Griffiths sees raising children as proof that alignment is hard. Children aren't smarter than their parents, but they are very motivated to find loopholes in the rules. Every parent is designing a reward function for an agent that's trying to beat it.

06Curiosity

Exploring without a reward

When the world gives almost no reward, an agent needs to want novelty and surprise for their own sake.

  • Superhuman, except in one game. DeepMind's DQN (2015) learned dozens of Atari games from raw pixels, scoring 25 times a professional tester at Video Pinball. In Montezuma's Revenge, where almost any mistake kills you and the first points take a long sequence of correct moves, it scored almost nothing.
  • Berlyne's three drives. Psychologist Daniel Berlyne described intrinsic motivation as wanting novelty, surprise and mastery. Infants look longer at new pictures. Laura Schulz found that children play longest with toys whose behaviour confused them.
  • Novelty as a reward. Marc Bellemare's "pseudo-counts" estimate how familiar a screen is and give a bonus for unfamiliar ones. In Q*bert the novelty signal jumped when the agent finally finished level one. In Montezuma it reached 15 of 24 rooms.
  • Surprise as a reward. Jürgen Schmidhuber's idea, developed by Deepak Pathak and OpenAI's Random Network Distillation, rewards the agent for whatever it fails to predict. The RND agent explored 20–22 rooms, and one run escaped the temple.
  • No score needed. Agents driven only by curiosity still played many games well, because dying is boring: it sends you back to screens you've already seen. In Pong two curious agents just kept the rally going.
  • Boredom and addiction. Pathak's Mario agent seemed to get bored at a gap it couldn't cross. Put a "noisy TV" in a maze and a surprise-seeking agent stares at it forever, much like a gambler at a slot machine. Laurent Orseau's knowledge-seeking agent can't be distracted this way, because noise teaches it nothing. Christian suggests general intelligence may need motivation of this internal kind.

Rooms explored in Montezuma's Revenge (of 24)

Part III · Chapters 7–9

Normativity

If our goals are too subtle to write down as a reward, maybe machines can learn them from us: by copying what we do, inferring what we want, and staying unsure about it.

07Imitation

Copying is powerful, and it has limits

Imitation passes on skills nobody can put into words. But an imitator never sees the expert recover from mistakes, and can't easily outdo its teacher.

  • Humans are the real imitators. Monkeys rarely ape. Human newborns copy a stuck-out tongue. Children over-imitate: they repeat pointless steps that chimpanzees skip, because they assume the adult had a reason. Toddlers in György Gergely's study copied an adult who switched on a lamp with her head, unless her hands were obviously busy.
  • Three benefits. Copying saves effort: Adam Ondra climbed the Dawn Wall in weeks after the first ascent took eight years, and DeepMind agents learned Montezuma from YouTube videos. It is safer than trial and error. And it captures goals that are hard to specify, like "drive well", which Nick Bostrom calls indirect normativity.
  • ALVINN. Around 1990 Dean Pomerleau's small network learned to steer by watching him drive and took a van from Pittsburgh to Lake Erie. Its training data was just "steer like I do".
  • Cascading errors. Experts rarely get into trouble, so their data never shows how to get out of it. Stéphane Ross's kart-racing imitator drifted off the track and had no idea how to recover. His DAgger algorithm fixes this by letting the learner drive and asking the expert what they would do in the learner's mistakes. Nvidia and a Swiss drone team got a similar effect with side-facing cameras labelled "steer back".
  • Possibilism vs. actualism. Philosophers ask whether Professor Procrastinate should accept a review he won't actually write. Kasparov's young student played a grandmaster's opening without knowing the follow-up plan. Copying what a better player would do can be a mistake if you can't do the rest. In reinforcement learning this is the difference between off-policy and on-policy learning.
  • Getting past the teacher. Deep Blue's evaluation was tuned on 700,000 grandmaster games. AlphaGo Zero learned with no human games by imitating its own search, which is slower and stronger than its intuition. Paul Christiano's "iterated distillation and amplification" proposes doing the same with human values: a person helped by AI assistants trains a better assistant, again and again.
What imitation gives you
  • Efficiency: someone else's trial and error, already done
  • Safety: no need to crash thousands of times
  • Tacit goals: behaviour that's hard to describe
Where it breaks
  • Cascading errors: no data on how to recover → ask the expert interactively (DAgger)
  • Different abilities: the expert's move fails without the expert's skill → be an actualist
  • Ceiling: you can't beat your teacher → amplify, then distil
08Inference

Inferring what we want

Instead of copying our actions, a machine can work out the goal behind them, then pursue it, possibly better than we do.

  • Toddlers help. Felix Warneken showed that 18-month-olds open a cabinet for an adult whose hands are full of magazines. Michael Tomasello calls humans "the world's experts at mind reading". Christian suggests machines might learn our values the same way.
  • Inverse reinforcement learning. In 1997, walking downhill to Safeway, Stuart Russell wondered what human gait is optimising for. Normal reinforcement learning turns a reward into behaviour. IRL works backwards from behaviour to the reward. It's an ill-posed problem, since any behaviour fits many possible goals, but it works well in practice.
  • The helicopter that beat its teachers. Pieter Abbeel and Andrew Ng had experts fly stunts and inferred the intended manoeuvre from many imperfect attempts. By 2008 their Stanford helicopter could fly the "chaos", a manoeuvre its inventor could no longer do himself, though it had never seen a perfect example.
  • Feedback instead of demonstrations. Many things are easier to judge than to do. Christiano, Leike and Amodei had a simulated robot try things while a human picked the better of two short clips. After a few hundred comparisons, about an hour, it did a clean backflip, a task nobody could have written a reward for.
  • Cooperative IRL. Russell's group made the human part of the problem. The robot's goal is the human's reward, and it doesn't know what that reward is. That gives humans a reason to teach and the robot a reason to ask. People use exaggerated "parentese" with babies, and robots can make "legible" movements whose intent is obvious. Learning alongside the human works better than learning everything first and then optimising. Julie Shah found that human–robot teams improve by swapping roles, as human teams do.
  • Being watched. A recommendation engine that infers your values from your clicks will learn your weaknesses. A friend of Christian's in recovery from alcohol addiction gets shown drink ads. Users may need a right to be modelled as the person they're trying to become.

09Uncertainty

Machines that know they might be wrong

A system that knows it might be mistaken avoids irreversible actions and lets us correct it. Overconfidence is the dangerous part.

  • Petrov's call. On 26 September 1983 a Soviet early-warning system reported five US missiles with "highest" reliability. Stanislav Petrov reported a false alarm because five missiles didn't make sense for a real first strike. He was right: the satellites had mistaken sunlight on clouds for launches.
  • Brittle and overconfident. Image classifiers must pick from known labels, so they label random static with total confidence. Adversarial noise can turn a "panda" into a "gibbon" with 99% confidence. Thomas Dietterich's insect classifier had no way to say "a species I've never seen". He calls for work on the "unknown unknowns".
  • Dropout as doubt. Yarin Gal and Zoubin Ghahramani showed that dropout, a standard training trick, approximates a Bayesian ensemble. Leave it on at prediction time, run the model many times, and the spread of answers measures its uncertainty. A diabetic-retinopathy model that referred its uncertain cases to doctors became more accurate on the rest.
  • Low impact. Doctors who found a "Do Not Resuscitate" tattoo on an unconscious patient first chose the option they could reverse. AI researchers are trying to formalise this caution: Stuart Armstrong's long list of things to leave alone, Victoria Krakovna's "relative reachability" (don't push the box into a corner you can't get it out of), and Alexander Turner's "attainable utility preservation", which tries to keep other goals achievable.
  • Corrigibility. Norbert Wiener warned in 1960 that we had better be sure the purpose we put into a machine is the one we really want. A machine certain of its objective has a reason to resist being switched off. In Berkeley's off-switch game, a robot unsure what we want will defer to us. If it becomes certain, or its model of us is wrong, that falls apart. Smitha Milli found that a robot should sometimes disobey an obvious human mistake, but only if its model of the human is rich enough.
  • Inverse reward design. Treat a reward someone wrote as evidence of what they wanted in the situations they had in mind, not as a law for every situation. The boat's "points" were a fine proxy in the ten games its designer had looked at.
  • Moral uncertainty. Will MacAskill and Toby Ord borrow from Catholic theology's debates over doubtful rules. If there's a real chance that eating meat is seriously wrong, the asymmetry of stakes counts. Nick Bostrom argues that the vast long-term future makes caution more important than speed. MacAskill wants a world that stays "un-locked-in" for as long as possible.

The off-switch game: uncertainty makes deference worthwhile

Deferring is worth at least as much as acting or switching off, but only while the robot is uncertain and trusts the human's judgement. A robot given a fixed, certain objective has no reason to let us correct it. In Hadfield-Menell's words, think very hard before giving a robot a deterministic reward function.

After Hadfield-Menell, Drăgan, Abbeel & Russell (2017), as described in ch. 9

Conclusion

The map is not the territory

Christian ends with a broken thermostat, a list of the assumptions built into machine learning, and a warning: we could lose control of the world to our models rather than to AI as such.

One Christmas Eve Christian woke up drenched in sweat. His guest room shared a thermostat with the empty room next door, where the door was open and the cold kept getting in, so the heater ran non-stop into his room. Two lessons: you don't measure what you think you measure, and sometimes the only thing that saves you is the system's own weakness. A stronger heater would have done more damage.

Wiener warned that a partial view of human purpose used to be harmless because our tools were weak. As they get stronger, our mistakes in specifying goals matter more. Christian argues we need to fix our folly before we fix our impotence.

He sees real progress: research on fairness, transparency and safety now takes up a large share of major AI conferences. But every model is wrong in some way. The biggest danger, he argues, isn't a rogue AI. It's institutions that treat formal metrics as if they were the world, and stop noticing what the metrics leave out. The Uber self-driving car that killed Elaine Herzberg in 2018 never classified her as a pedestrian, because its design didn't allow for people crossing outside a crosswalk. Systems can end up enforcing the limits of what they were designed to recognise.

In a 1952 BBC radio panel, Alan Turing described teaching a machine by intervening whenever it went wrong. Neurosurgeon Geoffrey Jefferson asked: "But who was learning, you or the machine?" Christian's answer is both. Trying to state our values precisely enough for a machine also shows us what those values are.

  • Proxies: points instead of winning the race, arrests instead of crimes, human labels instead of reality
Saved by weakness

  • As systems get more capable, an imprecise goal does more damage

Takeaways

The toolkit

The book is a history, not a manual. But each part suggests practical habits for anyone who builds, buys or is affected by a learning system, and some apply to managing people too.

Prophecy

Ask "accurate for whom?"Break results down by group, including combinations like darker-skinned women. Averages hide failures.
Audit the dataset before the modelEvery training set works like a Shirley card. Document who is in it and who decided the labels.
Choose which error you can live withCalibration and equal error rates can't both hold when base rates differ. Make that choice openly and on purpose.
Check what the target measuresArrest isn't crime and a click isn't a preference. Ask what you would do with the prediction.
Try a simple model firstChoose good variables and add them up. If a black box is needed, check its saliency maps for rulers.

Agency

Reward outcomes, not activityPay for reaching states you want, so loops and loopholes don't pay.
Expect gamingChildren, employees and agents all find the bucket of water. Before launch, ask how someone could game the incentive.
Build a curriculumBreak sparse goals into a sequence of achievable steps, with rewards that reflect long-term value.
Remember happiness comes from prediction errorsSatisfaction tracks "better than expected", so it fades. Lowering expectations won't solve that.
Value curiosity, watch for noisy TVsInternal motivation drives exploration, and endless novelty can hook people and agents alike.

Normativity

Show recoveries, not just perfect runsLearners need examples of getting back from a mistake. Give feedback on what they actually do.
Copy what you can carry throughBe an actualist: choose the step you'll actually follow through on over the ideal one you'd abandon.
Teach by comparisonIf a goal is hard to define, say which of two attempts is better. It's often enough.
Reversible firstWhen unsure, pick the option you can undo, and keep other options open.
Never give anything total certaintyA system or person sure of its objective won't accept correction. Stay open to being wrong.
