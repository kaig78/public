TheCoaching Habit

Say less, ask more and change the way you lead forever

Michael Bungay Stanier argues that coaching isn't a formal session. It's a ten-minute, everyday habit built from seven short questions.

The big idea

A little less advice, a little more curiosity

Everyone agrees managers should coach. Daniel Goleman found coaching had a clearly positive effect on performance and culture, yet it was the leadership style used least, because leaders said they didn't have time. Most managers who tried got training that was too theoretical, never turned it into new behaviour, and fell back into the old way the moment they got back to their desks.

Bungay Stanier's answer is to shrink coaching. It isn't an occasional "it's coaching time" event. It's something you do in ten minutes or less, every day, in ordinary conversations. And the change is one behaviour: stay curious a little longer, and rush to action and advice a little more slowly.

That's simple but not easy. You've been praised and promoted for having answers. Advice feels useful and keeps you in control. Asking feels slower and less certain. So the book is as much about building a habit as it is about the seven questions themselves.

Why it matters
73%

of managers had received some coaching training (BlessingWhite, 2006).

23%

of the people being coached said it had a significant effect on their performance or satisfaction.

10%

said the coaching they got was making things worse.

The payoff for you

Coaching isn't only good for your people. It lets you work less hard and have more impact, because it breaks three vicious circles that trap busy managers (below).

Three vicious circles a coaching habit breaks

The more you help, the more your team needs your help, and you become the bottleneck. The more you're pulled in different directions, the less you focus. The more time goes into work with no real purpose, the less engaged everyone is. Coaching builds autonomy, restores focus and points people back to Great Work: work with more impact and more meaning.

Before the questions

How to build a habit that sticks

About 45% of what we do while awake is habit, according to a Duke University study. Good intentions lose to autopilot. So the book starts with the mechanics of changing behaviour.

Five ingredients of a new habit

  1. A reason: make a vowLink the change to people you care about, not to your own gain. Picturing success too vividly actually lowers motivation.
  2. A triggerIf you don't know what sets off the old behaviour, you're already doing it before you notice. Make the trigger specific: not "in team meetings" but "when Jenny asks for feedback on her idea".
  3. A micro-habitShort and specific: something that takes under 60 seconds. Every one of the seven questions fits.
  4. Deep practicePractise small chunks, repeat them many times, and notice and celebrate when it works.
  5. A plan for slippingYou will miss a day. Decide in advance what gets you back on track, so one slip doesn't become giving up.

Expect resistance

As soon as you try something new, something pushes back. Start with someone easy (or someone it's going so badly with that you have nothing to lose). Start small and master one question before adding the next. Find a buddy to check in with. And when it slips, get back on the horse: the awkward stage of "conscious incompetence" is how you get to competence.

The core framework

Seven essential questions

Each one takes under a minute to ask. Together they make a conversation that starts fast, finds the real issue, and ends with something learned.

One conversation, start to finish

Questions 1 and 7 are the Coaching Bookends: start fast, finish strong. Question 2 is a booster you can bolt onto every other question.

What each question does

  1. KickstartOpens a conversation that is focused but leaves the choice of topic to them.
  2. AWEGets more options, holds back your Advice Monster, and buys you thinking time.
  3. FocusStops everyone solving the first problem instead of the real one.
  4. FoundationThe heart of adult-to-adult relationships: naming what you actually want.
  5. LazyTurns a vague call for help into a clear request, and saves you hours.
  6. StrategicSaves them hours by making every Yes carry a No.
  7. LearningTurns the conversation into something remembered and gets you feedback.

They work with your team, but also with bosses, colleagues, customers, suppliers and (with no guarantees) teenagers. Six of the seven begin with "What".

Part 1 · Questions 1–3

Open and focus

Start fast, keep the other person producing ideas, and find the real problem before anyone starts fixing the wrong one.

01The Kickstart Question

What's on your mind?

A Goldilocks opener: open enough to let them choose what matters, focused enough to skip the trivia.

  • Managers often don't coach because they don't know how to start. Three things stall the start of a conversation: the Small Talk Tango (eight of fifteen minutes on the weather), the Ossified Agenda (the same standing meeting reading out the same figures), and the Default Diagnosis (everyone assumes they know the issue and starts digging, often in the wrong hole).
  • "What's on your mind?" sidesteps all three. It says: let's talk about the thing that matters most to you right now, whether it's exciting, scary or keeping you up at 4 a.m.
  • It also works as a pressure valve. Whatever is on your mind uses energy (the brain is 2% of body weight but uses about 20% of your energy) and quietly narrows what you notice. Saying it out loud frees it.
  • Two kinds of coaching: for performance (fixing the fire) and for development (growing the person fighting the fire). Development conversations are rarer and the ones people remember.

Deepen the focus with the 3Ps

the contentProjectsThe work itself. The easiest, most familiar place to start.
the relationshipsPeopleNot really about them: about your role in a relationship that isn't ideal.
your own behaviourPatternsHow you get in your own way. Where development conversations happen.
Coaching for performanceCoaching for development

After "What's on your mind?", offer the three angles and let them pick: "We could look at the project side, the people side, or patterns, if there's a way you're getting in your own way. Where should we start?" When one is done, move to another.

From the lab

"What's on your mind?" is the prompt Facebook used for its status box. It was removed for a while, then brought back, presumably because nothing worked better at getting millions of people to reflect and share.

02The AWE Question

And what else?

The "best coaching question in the world". The first answer is almost never the only answer, and rarely the best one.

  • More options mean better decisions. In a study of 168 organisational decisions, 71% were framed as a simple yes or no. More than half of those failed. Adding even one more option cut the failure rate to about 30%.
  • It tames the Advice Monster. Organisations reward answers, and our brains crave certainty, so we leap in with solutions before we understand the problem. A 1984 study found doctors interrupted patients after 18 seconds on average. Many managers are just as quick.
  • It buys you time. When you're not sure what's going on, asking it gives you a moment to think. (The author calls this his professional secret.)
  • Ask it with real curiosity, not as a tic. Ask it at least three times, rarely more than five. Treat "there's nothing else" as success, not panic. When energy drops, switch to "Is there anything else?" to close the topic.
  • Don't overdo it. At a tasting table with 6 jams, shoppers were ten times more likely to buy than at one with 24. Three to five answers is plenty.

There's still a place for advice. It's just overused, and it's often advice for a problem you haven't understood yet.

From the lab: students given a second pass at true/false questions got more right. Three-year-olds who denied peeking at a toy mostly named it correctly when asked "What's the toy?" There's usually something else waiting.

03The Focus Question

What's the real challenge here for you?

Focus on the real problem, not the first problem.

  • What people first describe is rarely the actual problem. It may be a symptom, a side issue, a familiar old problem, or a half-baked solution to something unspoken.
  • Jump in to fix it and three things go wrong: you solve the wrong problem, you solve it yourself (doing your team's work for them), or you don't solve it at all because you're now the bottleneck, the "VP of Bottlenecking".
  • "For you" is the key. Adding it to almost any question shifts the conversation from performance to development. In a 1997 study, maths problems that included the word "you" were solved faster and more accurately.
  • Trust the pause after you ask. That moment of silence is the person thinking. And remember there is still a place for advice: if someone asks where the folder is, tell them.

How the question is built

Three "Foggy-fiers" and how the question clears them

PopcornProliferation of challengesOne problem pops, then another, then ten. You feel anxious, and secretly pleased at all the advice you could give."If you had to pick one of these, which would be the real challenge for you?"
GossipCoaching the ghost45 minutes on how impossible John is. Enjoyable, but you can only coach the person in front of you."I think I understand what's going on with John. What's the real challenge here for you?"
SeminarAbstractions & generalisationsA big-picture talk about culture and strategy. Lots of "we" and "us", no "I" or "me"."I have a sense of the overall challenge. What's the real challenge here for you?"

The 1-2-3 combination

open       What's on your mind?
check      Is there anything else on your mind?
focus      So what's the real challenge here for you?
widen      And what else is the real challenge?
probe      Is there anything else?
land       So… what's the real challenge here for you?

The first three questions make a robust script on their own. You'll be surprised how often it's exactly right.

Part 2 · Questions 4–5

Adult to adult

Two blunt questions about wants and requests. They make the other person say what they need, and stop you rescuing them from it.

04The Foundation Question

What do you want?

An adult relationship means being able to ask for what you want, knowing the answer may be No.

  • The definition is Peter Block's. The author calls this the Goldfish Question, because people's eyes widen and their mouths open and close with no sound.
  • It's hard for four reasons: we often don't know what we want; when we do, we don't ask; when we ask, we aren't clear enough to be heard (hints, passive-aggressive remarks); and when we're heard, we find it hard to accept a No, or to give one.
  • Separate wants from needs. Wants are the surface request. Needs are the human driver underneath. Guessing the need tells you how best to meet the want, and naming your own need makes your requests clearer.
  • Ask, then trade answers. The question is strong when you ask it, and stronger when you also say what you want. Like a contract, it's an exchange of value.

Wants sit on top of needs

Marshall Rosenberg's nine universal needs (drawn from Manfred Max-Neef). "Talk to the VP for me" might mean protect me, I'm too junior, or participate, do your part.

Five times a second: safe or dangerous?

The brain constantly scans for risk or reward, and when unsure it assumes danger. Four drivers (TERA) decide which way it reads a moment. Asking a question, instead of telling, moves three of them up.

From the lab: the miracle question

Solution-focused therapists ask: "Suppose a miracle happens tonight while you sleep. Tomorrow morning, how will you know things have got better?" It works because it puts the end before the means. "What do you want?" does the same thing more directly: once people see the destination, the route gets clearer.

05The Lazy Question

How can I help?

Be helpful, not "helpful". Make them name the request, and stop yourself jumping in to rescue.

  • Edgar Schein's insight: offering help "one-ups" you. It raises your status and lowers theirs, which is why unrequested help often meets resistance and annoyance.
  • The Karpman Drama Triangle describes three roles we slip into when triggered. They're behaviours, not identities, and we cycle through all three, sometimes in one exchange.
  • Most managers default to Rescuer. It feels virtuous but leaves you exhausted and them irritated, and it limits their growth. Rescuers create Victims.
  • You'll never fully escape the triangle. You'll just spot it sooner. "How can I help?" pulls you out faster: it makes them state a clear request, and it stops you assuming what they want. The worst case is being only slightly wrong, doing something close enough that nobody stops you, but not useful.
  • The blunt version is "What do you want from me?" Tone matters: from a Persecutor it sounds aggressive, from a Victim whiny, from a Rescuer smothering. "Out of curiosity…" softens any question.

Avoid the Rescuer sucker punch

When this happens…

Someone asks, "How do I…?" or "What do you think I should do about…?" (the cheese in the mousetrap)

Instead of…

giving them the answer

I will…

From the lab: doctors who opened with a general "How can I help?" heard longer, more specific descriptions of symptoms, and got better ratings from patients.

Part 3 · Questions 6–7

Commit and learn

Protect every Yes with a No, then end the conversation by pinning down what was useful, so it's remembered.

06The Strategic Question

If you're saying Yes to this, what are you saying No to?

"The essence of strategy is choosing what not to do." (Michael Porter)

  • Ban "it's a good busy". Busyness isn't success. Ban "work smarter, not harder" too: it's True But Useless. Strategy isn't a binder on the top shelf. It's the everyday choice of what not to do.
  • First get the Yes clear: "What exactly are you saying Yes to? What would being fully committed look like?" Half-hearted, misunderstood Yeses cause most "I never said I'd do that" moments.
  • Then find the two kinds of No. The No of omission is what the Yes automatically rules out. The No of commission is what you must actively stop to make room for it. Use the 3Ps: which projects to pause, which expectations to manage or relationships to let fade, which habits and old stories to drop.
  • It helps against two biases: the planning fallacy (we underestimate how long things take) and loss aversion (we overvalue what we already hold).

A Yes needs Nos around it

Say Yes more slowly

Bill Jensen's trick for saying No when you can't: stay curious before committing.

  • Why are you asking me? Who else have you asked?
  • When you say urgent, what do you mean?
  • To what standard, and by when?
  • If I could only do part, which part?
  • What should I take off my plate to do this?

They'll either have good answers (a thoughtful request), go and find answers (you've bought time), or find someone who says Yes faster. To say No without rejecting the person, write the request down and say No to the paper: Yes to the person, No to the task.

Going bigger: Playing to Win

For real strategy work the author recommends A.G. Lafley and Roger Martin's five questions, which work for one person or a global company: What is our winning aspiration? Where will we play? How will we win? What capabilities must be in place? What management systems do we need? Underneath all five sits the same question: what are you choosing not to do?

07The Learning Question

What was most useful for you?

People don't learn when you tell them, or even when they do something. They learn when they recall and reflect on it.

  • Chris Argyris called this double-loop learning. The first loop fixes the problem. The second steps back to find the insight. This question creates the second loop.
  • In the AGES model (Attention, Generation, Emotion, Spacing), Generation is what matters here: we remember what we work out ourselves much better than what we're told. That's why advice is overrated.
  • "To learn, retrieve." Forgetting starts immediately. Asking at the end of a conversation interrupts it. Asking "What have you learned since we last met?" at the next one-to-one does it again.
  • It ends on a high. By Kahneman's peak-end rule, how something ends colours how all of it is remembered. Colonoscopy patients whose procedure ended less painfully remembered about 10% less pain and were about 10% more likely to come back.

Why this wording beats "What did you learn?"

  • It assumes the conversation was useful
  • It asks for the one big thing, not a list of twelve
  • "For you" makes it personal, so it sticks
  • It gives you feedback on what to do more of
  • It's learning, not judgment (unlike "Was this useful?")
  • It reminds people how useful you are to them

Between the chapters

The question masterclass

Eight short lessons scattered through the book on how to ask. Each comes with its own micro-habit.

1

Ask one question at a time

A volley of questions feels like driving round the Arc de Triomphe: it's an interrogation, not a conversation.

I willAsk one question. Then be quiet and wait for the answer.

2

Cut the intro

No Bond film starts slowly. Skip the long wind-up before your question. If you need a lead-in, "Out of curiosity…" makes any question lighter.

I willAsk the question, then shut up and listen.

3

No fake questions

"Have you thought of…?" and "What about…?" are advice with a question mark attached.

I willAsk "And what else?" first. If I still have an idea, offer it openly as an option.

4

Ask "What", not "Why"

"Why?" puts people on the defensive and pulls you into fixing things. If you're not fixing it, you don't need the backstory.

I willTurn "Why did you do that?" into "What were you hoping for here?"

5

Get comfortable with silence

Three or four seconds of silence feels endless. But it usually means they're thinking, and that's where the value is.

I willTake a breath, stay open and keep quiet for another three seconds.

6

Actually listen

Nodding like a bobblehead while planning your next question (or dinner) isn't listening.

I willReally listen, and when I drift, come back.

7

Acknowledge the answers

Call and response, like the blues. A short "Yes, that's good" shows you heard before the next "And what else?"

I willAcknowledge each reply before moving on.

8

Use every channel

The questions work just as well by email, chat or text, and they save you writing long advice-filled replies.

I willReply to the rambling email with one question: "What's the real challenge here for you?"

Conclusion

Seven hours lost on a fifteen-minute walk

As a student in Australia, the author set out alone on a three-day bushwalk. His pack weighed 40 pounds, mostly first-aid kit. Within twenty minutes the path had vanished. Sure he knew the way, he refused to turn back and climbed straight up the mountain through waterfalls and dense scrub to find the trail.

Seven hours later, scratched and exhausted, he made camp. A fresh-looking hiker came by. Asked how his walk was going, the hiker said it was hard to tell: he'd only been walking fifteen minutes.

Too many manager conversations feel like that hike. The fix is not the seven questions as such. It's a habit of curiosity: a little less advice, a little more asking, in your own words.

Where conversations go wrong

Too much baggageArriving loaded with answers and fixes.
Too much certaintyAssuming you know the destination and the route.
Leaving the pathCharging off after the first problem.
Working too hardDoing the climb that should be theirs.

And ending up exhausted, having got far less far than you hoped. Build the habit, and you work less hard and have more impact.
