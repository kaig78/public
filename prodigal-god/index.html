TheProdigal God

Recovering the Heart of the Christian Faith
Timothy Keller

A short book on one parable, Luke 15, which Keller says is "one of the clearest spots where we can see all the way to the bottom" of Jesus' teaching. He first understood it through a sermon by his teacher Edmund Clowney, and says no passage has helped more people he counselled. The famous son is not the point. The story is aimed at the other son, the obedient one, and it shows that there are two ways to be lost and only one way home.

Dutton, 2008 (this edition Penguin, 2016) · 7 chapters · ~140 pages
prodigaladjective
  1. recklessly extravagant
  2. having spent everything

It doesn't mean "wayward." So the word fits the father better than the son: he refused to "reckon" his son's sin against him (2 Corinthians 5:19). Hence the title: God is the one who spends everything.

Chapter one · The people around Jesus

Who the story is for

"All gathering around to hear him."

Luke tells us who was listening. "Tax collectors and sinners" kept flocking to Jesus (the Greek tense means an ongoing pattern), and the Pharisees grumbled: "This man welcomes sinners and eats with them." The parable answers them. Its original hearers weren't moved to tears; they were offended. "Jesus's purpose is not to warm our hearts but to shatter our categories."

  • The same two brothers live in every family and culture. The oldest child is often the parent-pleaser who stays nearby; the youngest goes off to the hip neighbourhoods. In history: bourgeois respectability versus the bohemians, from Murger's Paris to the Beats and indie rock. Today: the culture wars.
  • Whose side is Jesus on? Like Tolkien's Treebeard: "I am not altogether on anybody's side, because nobody is altogether on my side." But he singles out religious moralism as especially deadly.
  • The early Christians were called atheists. No temple, no priests, no sacrifices; Jesus was all three. Christianity began as something else entirely, neither religion nor irreligion.
  • The test for churches. Outsiders were drawn to Jesus and the religious were offended. If our churches draw only buttoned-down, moral people and repel the broken and the liberated, "we must not be declaring the same message that Jesus did."

Chapter two · The two lost sons

A drama in two acts

"There was a man who had two sons."

Keller reads the story through the eyes of a first-century village. Every move would have shocked them.

Act 1

The lost younger brother

  1. "Give me my share." Asking for the inheritance while the father lives is wishing him dead. He wants the father's things, not the father.
  2. The father divides his bios. The Greek word means "life." He sells land, and his standing with it, and bears the pain of rejected love without retaliating.
  3. The pigsty plan. Become a hired man, earn a wage, pay back the debt.
  4. The father runs. Patriarchs never ran; he hitches up his robe like a boy. He interrupts the speech: best robe (his own), ring, sandals, the fattened calf, the whole village at the feast.

Grace is free. No sin is too big.

Act 2

The lost elder brother

  1. He refuses to go in to the biggest party his father ever threw: a public vote of no confidence.
  2. It's about the cost. Reinstating the brother makes him an heir again, out of what is left.
  3. "Look!" No "esteemed father." Today's equivalent: a son publishing a tell-all memoir to ruin his dad.
  4. The father comes out again and pleads tenderly: come in. The choice is yours.

And the story stops. We never learn his answer.

Act 1 on its own seems to say God simply accepts everyone, no cost, no atonement. That's why there is an Act 2: it shows what grace costs. And the open ending is deliberate. The Pharisees listening are the elder brother, and Jesus is pleading with his enemies.

Chapter three · Redefining sin

Two ways to be your own saviour

In the film Witness, the Amish father-in-law says the elders will judge Rachel; she answers, "I will be the judge of that." Most people live by one of those two lines, swing between them over a lifetime, or keep both under one roof (the respectable man with a secret life; the liberated person who looks down on believers like the worst Pharisee).

Careful obedience to God's law may serve as a strategy for rebelling against God.

Why doesn't the elder son go in? He says so himself: "I've never disobeyed you." It isn't his sins that keep him out but his righteousness. Flannery O'Connor's Hazel Motes had "a deep, black, wordless conviction… that the way to avoid Jesus was to avoid sin." Keep all the rules and God owes you; then Jesus may be your helper or example, but not your Saviour.

Salieri

In Amadeus, young Salieri bargains with God: make me a famous composer and I'll give you my chastity, industry and help to the poor. Then the vulgar Mozart arrives, with gifts far greater. "From now on we are enemies, You and I." His goodness turns murderous the moment it stops paying. More quietly, a woman who had served for years in Christian ministry fell into despair with a chronic illness. Deep down she felt God owed her better.

So sin isn't only breaking rules. It is putting yourself in God's place as Saviour, Lord and Judge, by being very bad or by being very good.

Both wrong, both loved

The gospel isn't somewhere between these two; it is something else. Everyone is wrong, everyone is loved, and everyone is called to change. Not "the good are in" or "the open-minded are in," but "the humble are in and the proud are out." When a newspaper asked "What's wrong with the world?", G. K. Chesterton reportedly replied: "Dear Sirs, I am."

The two are equally wrong but not equally dangerous. The younger son knew he was lost. The elder son would have been horrified at the suggestion. "I'm there every time the church doors are open." Jesus says, in effect: that doesn't matter.

Chapter four · Redefining lostness

Signs of the elder brother

"The older brother became angry and refused to go in."

Anger

If you live a good life you expect a good life. When it goes wrong you're furious with God, or with yourself, or you swing between "I hate Thee" and "I hate me." Elisabeth Elliot's (non-biblical) story: Jesus tells the disciples to carry a stone. Peter picks a pebble; at lunch the stones become bread. Next time Peter hauls a boulder. At evening Jesus says to throw them in the river. "Who were you carrying the stone for?"

Superiority

"This son of yours" — he won't call him brother. Self-worth by comparison breeds racism, classism and religious contempt, defended as "opposing God's enemies." Richard Lovelace: people unsure of God's love are "radically insecure," and it comes out as pride and criticism. And you can't forgive someone you feel superior to. Keller's example is the alcoholic's spouse who bails him out but keeps his record over his head; maybe the elder brother needed a wayward sibling.

Joyless, slavish duty

"All these years I've been slaving for you." An MBA ethics class taught honesty because you might get caught and it boosts morale: fear and pride. It evaporates the day a lie would pay. The king's gardener gave him an enormous carrot out of love and received land. A nobleman saw that, brought a fine horse and got nothing: "That gardener was giving me the carrot, but you were giving yourself the horse."

No assurance

"You never threw me a party." Every setback raises the question of what you did wrong; criticism devastates you; guilt lingers after repentance. The clearest sign is a dry prayer life. You talk to a business contact about tasks, to a friend about problems, to a lover about what you find beautiful. Elder brothers pray in the first mode, mostly when life goes badly.

Who needs to hear this? Elder brothers, because if you don't know you're sick you won't see a doctor. Younger brothers, because many left religion for exactly the reasons Jesus condemns, and he agrees with them. Keller's early Redeemer was about a third non-believers, a third believers and a third "recovering" believers, many of whom had come to New York to get away from churches back home. "The elder brothers had turned them into younger brothers." And Christians, because if you came to faith as an elder brother, that is the way you'll slide back.

Chapter five · The true elder brother

Who should have gone out?

"My son, everything I have is yours."

Luke 15 has three parables in a row, and the first two set up a pattern:

Lost sheepThe shepherd goes out and searches until he finds it.
Lost coinThe woman lights a lamp and sweeps the whole house.
Lost sonNobody goes out.

The listeners would have expected someone to go. Who should have? At the start of the Bible, God tells another resentful elder brother, Cain: you are your brother's keeper. Clowney told of an American soldier missing in Vietnam whose older brother flew out and searched the battlefields for him; soldiers on both sides called him simply "the brother."

Forgiveness always costs someone

"Everything I have is yours" is literally true: the younger son's third is gone, so every robe, ring and calf now belongs to the elder brother. The younger brother can only come back at his expense. If someone breaks your lamp, you either make them pay or you forgive and pay for it yourself. Forgiveness is free to the one forgiven and costly to the one who forgives.

By putting a flawed elder brother in the story, Jesus is inviting us to imagine and yearn for a true one.

We have one. He came not to the next country but from heaven, and paid not with money but with his life. On the cross he was stripped of his robe so we could wear one, treated as an outcast so we could be brought into the family, and drank the cup of justice so we could have the cup of joy.

What we need

  • The father's initiating love. He runs to the younger son before the confession, and goes out to the elder one too. Jesus is "not a Pharisee about Pharisees." If you're starting to sense your lostness and want out, that desire is a sign he's already at your side.
  • Deeper repentance. Pharisees repent of their sins and stay Pharisees. Christians also repent of the reasons they did anything right. A seminary teacher of Keller's put it this way: what separates Pharisees from God is "not their sins, but their damnable good works."
  • A sight of what it cost. In the Vietnamese film Three Seasons, the cyclo driver Hai wins a race and pays for the prostitute Lan to spend a night in a fine hotel, only to watch her sleep. Power used to serve, not to use, changes her. Selfless love dissolves the mistrust that makes us either kind of brother.

Chapter six · Redefining hope

Longing for home

"He set off for a far country."

Home pulls hard and never quite delivers. Kathy Keller spent thirty-nine summers at a cottage on Lake Erie; the memory nourishes her, but visiting the run-down place breaks her heart. Holidays sink under impossible expectations. The Germans call it Sehnsucht. C. S. Lewis, in "The Weight of Glory": beauty and memory are only reminders; mistake them for the thing itself and "they turn into dumb idols, breaking the hearts of their worshippers." Our lifelong longing to be on the inside of a door we've only seen from outside "is the truest index of our real situation."

The Bible explains why. We were made for a garden in God's presence, chafed under the Father's authority, and left. Since then the story is exile after exile: Cain wandering, Jacob fleeing, Israel in Egypt, David hunted, the nation carried to Babylon. Even the return from Babylon left Israel under one empire after another. Two things block the way home: the brokenness within us, and the brokenness around us, a world of disease and death.

Then Jesus came, and nothing fit the expected liberator. Born far from home, "nowhere to lay his head," crucified outside the city gate, crying "My God, why have you forsaken me?" He took the exile we deserved, cosmic homelessness, so we could be brought home. The parable ends in a feast, and so does history: the marriage supper of the Lamb, the tree of life back, every tear wiped away. Not floating consciousness but eating, embracing, singing and dancing.

Chapter seven · The feast of the father

Living from the gospel

"He heard music and dancing."

Jesus' first sign was 150 gallons of excellent wine to keep a wedding going. Salvation is pictured again and again as a feast, and a feast says four things about it:

ExperientialNot only a legal verdict but something tasted. Jonathan Edwards: believing God is gracious versus tasting it is like knowing honey is sweet versus tasting it.
MaterialGod loves the physical world and will renew it. Jesus' miracles restore nature rather than break it. Christianity isn't the opiate of the masses; "it's more like the smelling salts."
IndividualYou must keep eating. "Religion" is the heart's default mode (Luther), so you must reset it to the gospel again and again.
CommunalLewis lost part of Tolkien when Charles Williams died. If it takes friends to know a friend, it takes a church to know Jesus. Avoiding church because of elder brothers is its own self-righteousness.
Religion
I obey, therefore I'm accepted.
Gospel
I'm accepted, therefore I obey.

How change actually happens

Not by pressing harder on the will. Paul doesn't order the Corinthians to give or tug at their emotions; he reminds them that Christ "though he was rich, yet for your sakes became poor." To husbands he doesn't hold up a model marriage; he says, love as Christ loved the church. Christ's generosity is the cure for stinginess; his faithfulness, for adultery. The gospel is "not just the ABCs of the Christian life, but the A to Z."

"But without fear, why would anyone obey?" If losing the fear of punishment removes your reason to obey, fear was your only reason. The other one is awed, grateful love. A newcomer at Redeemer called free grace "good scary": earn it and there's a limit to what God can ask; receive it at infinite cost and there's nothing he can't. That's Bonhoeffer's answer to "cheap grace": not legalism, but seeing what it cost. Luther: we are saved by faith alone, "but not by faith that remains alone."

Babette's Feast

In Isak Dinesen's story, two pious sisters gave up a dashing lieutenant and the Paris Opera to keep their father's strict little sect going on the Jutland coast. Years later the village is bitter and many neighbours aren't speaking to each other. Their servant Babette, secretly a great Paris chef, wins the lottery and spends it all on one dinner. Old enemies soften and touch foreheads; the lieutenant, now a general who never found happiness, quotes Psalm 85: "righteousness and bliss have kissed one another." Dinesen sensed that something lies beyond both the sensual life and the strict ethical one, and could only picture it as a feast. Jesus names it: "I am the Bread of Heaven."

Keep

What to carry out of this book

  1. Read the parable as the Pharisees heard it. It's about the son who stayed home.
  2. There are two ways to run your own life: break the rules or keep them so God owes you. Both use the Father to get his things.
  3. Watch for the signs: anger when life doesn't pay, superiority, joyless duty, shaky assurance, prayers that are only requests.
  4. Repent of your righteousness too, not just your sins: the reasons you did anything right.
  5. Forgiveness costs the forgiver. Jesus is the elder brother who went out and paid.
  6. Change comes from looking at what it cost, not from more willpower. Accepted, therefore obey.
  7. Your homesickness is real. It points to a feast at the end of history; stay in a church to taste it now.
