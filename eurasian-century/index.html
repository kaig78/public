Introduction

The age of American power was really a fight over one landmass

Brands opens with four freeze-frames: January 1917, December 1941, March 1947, February 2022. In each, an autocracy is reaching for control of the Old World, and democracies on its edges and across the oceans are working out whether they can stop it.

Those scenes are his thesis. Since about 1900, he argues, world politics has turned on Eurasia, a space he defines as Europe, Asia, their offshore islands, and North Africa. Ambitious autocracies try to seize its commanding positions. Offshore democracies, first Britain and then America, team up with allies on the mainland to keep it divided. The two world wars and the Cold War were rounds of that one game, and the rivalry with China, Russia and Iran is the next.

War over Eurasia is ancient, so what made this era different? Brands points to frequency, ferocity and reach: two world wars and the opening of a generational cold war came within about thirty years, and each decided who would run the international system.

He has two reasons for retelling it. A hostile power that dominated Eurasia could leave even distant democracies choosing between their safety and their freedoms. And every past challenger lost, but none had to. The results turned on choices, so today's leaders need the lessons.

The central figure is a half-forgotten British geographer, and the book keeps returning to his 1904 talk. These notes report Brands's case. Most of it is contested argument about live politics, and the account stops around mid-2024.

The theory · Chapter 1

The board

Before the wars came the argument about them. A small group of strategists worked out why geography, technology and tyranny were about to collide, and some of their readers took the wrong lessons.

01Mackinder's World

Railways, closed frontiers and the modern tyrant

Mackinder's 1904 lecture spotted the forces that would make Eurasia the century's killing ground. It was wrong on details and right on the shape of things.

  • A railway as omen. The Trans-Siberian, nearly 6,000 miles from Moscow to Vladivostok, was finished in 1904. Japan attacked Russia rather than let it be completed unopposed. Brands follows the line through both world wars to 2022, when Putin used it to bring Far Eastern units west, including a brigade later linked to Bucha.
  • The man. Halford Mackinder (1861–1947) helped found academic geography, sat in Parliament, led the first ascent of Mount Kenya, and never reached the top of British policy. His ideas reached further than he did.
  • Three forces. On 25 January 1904, to a room with empty seats, he described them. The 400-year Columbian epoch of easy expansion was over, so the great powers would now turn on each other. Railways were cancelling sea power's mobility advantage. And regimes were starting to combine repression with industry, a theme that grew in his later work.
  • The stakes. Dominant land power would become dominant sea power, since a continental master could build fleets freely. So the century's main event would be continental aggressors against offshore balancers and their mainland footholds.
  • What he missed. Russia lost in 1905 and the rail line couldn't save it. The next bids came from Germany and Japan on the Crescent, not from the Pivot. His lecture also closed on a racist warning about China.
  • The American school. Alfred Thayer Mahan, a naval officer miserable at sea, called the ocean a highway and urged America to join Britain in keeping Eurasia balanced. Nicholas Spykman, writing in 1942, showed why a fortress America would slowly be strangled if the Axis held the Old World. Both shaped how Washington later thought.
  • The dark mirror. Karl Haushofer read Mackinder as a to-do list, and his ideas reached Hitler through Rudolf Hess. Brands says that link is sometimes exaggerated. Haushofer's own son was executed for plotting against Hitler. Aleksandr Dugin has revived the same inversion for Russia.

Three struggles · Chapters 2–4

The rounds

The same structure three times over: a central power lunges, a ring of enemies forms, and the outcome depends on how early and how well the offshore power commits.

02The Great Black Tornado

World War I was not pointless

Brands rejects the futile-slaughter story. The war decided whether an autocratic Germany would gain mastery on land and at sea, and it came close.

  • Crowe's warning. On New Year's Day 1907 Eyre Crowe, a German-born British diplomat, finished a 16,000-word memo arguing that the union of the top army and the top navy in one state would force the world to combine against it.
  • Now or never. Germany had become Europe's largest economy and had encircled itself by bullying its neighbours. By 1914 it was losing the arms race, and leaders steeped in expand-or-die thinking decided war would never be cheaper. Brands assigns several capitals their share of the July crisis, but says Berlin did little except instigate.
  • A failure of deterrence. Only a firm British commitment might have sobered Germany, and that wasn't politically possible. The full unity that could have prevented war appeared only after it began, which Brands calls a recurring problem of the century.
  • Why it ground on. Firepower favoured defence, and Germany and France each mobilised a fifth of their populations. Neither side could accept a peace that left Germany's early gains in place.
  • How Germany nearly won. It had tactical skill, railways that made its central position an asset, a radicalised total-war economy, and creative strategy: it shipped Lenin home to wreck Russia, and used submarines to starve Britain. By early 1918 it had won in the east.
  • Teamwork, slowly. The Allies won only by pooling shipping, finance and blockade. They agreed on a single commander, Foch, in the emergency of March 1918, as a German offensive tore into their lines.
  • America's gamble and arrival. Germany bet that U-boats would finish Britain before American troops counted, at a moment when Britain was close to insolvency. Brands wonders whether a more patient Berlin could have outlasted its enemies. By mid-1918 nearly 10,000 American soldiers were landing each day, and 2 million came in all.
  • Why the peace failed. Versailles, he argues, was far from crushing: Germany paid only 22 billion of its 132 billion gold marks, and it stayed united. The real failure was American withdrawal once the Senate rejected the League. Only Washington had the money and the guarantees to steady Europe.

The closing-window trap

Illustrative curves, no real data. The logic is the book's: a rising autocracy that sees rivals catching up may choose a desperate gamble over slow decline. Germany did so in 1914, and Japan in 1941. Brands argues Putin raced through a similar window in 2022, and warns against assuming that awful costs will deter anyone.

Mackinder in South Russia

In 1919–20 Mackinder got to test his ideas as British High Commissioner to the anti-Bolshevik forces in South Russia. He proposed a chain of armed buffer states, arrived as those armies were collapsing, and watched the plan die in Cabinet. He still called the next war's location: the belt between Germany and Russia.

03The Totalitarian Abyss

Blocking a challenger early is hard. Rolling one back is ruinous.

World War II was the grimmest version of Mackinder's forecast, and it showed how much a broken balance costs to repair.

  • Why fascism expanded. The war had left aggrieved losers and disappointed winners. Bigness seemed to equal strength, and the Depression closed markets, so autarkic empire looked like the only safety. With America disengaged and Britain drained, nobody was keeping order.
  • Why nobody stopped them. Democracies couldn't imagine how bad things would get, dreaded repeating 1914, were stretched across many fronts, and were divided. At Munich in 1938 Britain and France probably could have won, Brands says, and they folded.
  • Conquest paid. Hitler rearmed on plunder, a scheme Brands likens to a Ponzi. Czechoslovakia alone yielded 1.5 million rifles, 2,000 field guns, 750 aircraft and 600 tanks. Stalin supplied raw materials until June 1941.
  • Speed. Carriers and blitzkrieg compressed time and space. Germany took France with fewer tanks and aircraft than its enemies, and contingency played a big part. In four months Japan swept from Southeast Asia to the Central Pacific.
  • Genocide and geopolitics. Living space was to be cleared by mass murder. Six million Jews were killed and some 8 million people were enslaved as workers. Japan's forces may have murdered 200,000 civilians at Nanking alone.
  • Choices. Britain might have sought terms in 1940, but Churchill refused. Roosevelt moved a divided public by steps, through destroyers for bases and Lend-Lease. Hitler then declared war on America on 11 December 1941, believing the clash was coming anyway.
  • The danger year. Until late 1942 the outcome was open. The nightmare was an Axis link-up through the Middle East and Indian Ocean, which would have cut the alliance apart. Midway turned on codebreakers and a few accurate pilots.
  • The price. Sixty million dead, moral damage on the winning side too, and Stalin's troops holding half of Europe. In 1943 Mackinder proposed making the wartime alliance permanent around a Midland Ocean, the Atlantic. It was meant to contain Germany. It became the answer to Moscow.

The balance of power doesn't regulate itself. It fails without commitment and common purpose.

Why the Grand Alliance worked
  • It had land armies and command of sea and air. The Soviets tied down 75–80% of German manpower.
  • Britain and America fused staffs, intelligence and technology. A British engine in an American P-51 cracked the Luftwaffe.
  • Roosevelt and Churchill met ten times.
  • It accepted a sordid bargain with Stalin to win.
Why the Axis was less than its parts
  • One-man rule left plans untested. On D-Day, Rommel's reserves waited on a sleeping Hitler.
  • Cruelty turned possible collaborators into enemies.
  • Germany and Japan held each other in racial contempt and barely coordinated.
  • They neglected logistics in a war of movement.

04The Golden Age

The round that didn't end in catastrophe

This time the offshore power stayed. By arming and enriching the rim of Eurasia in peacetime, Brands argues, America turned a cycle of destruction into a free, prosperous order.

  • Containment. Mackinder died on 6 March 1947, six days before Truman's speech to Congress declared the Cold War in all but name. George Kennan's middle path between appeasement and war rested on a difference from Hitler: Soviet leaders believed history was on their side, so they could be deterred. Block them, and their inner failures would do the rest.
  • An improvised order. Nobody planned a global alliance system. It grew crisis by crisis, with Dean Acheson as chief builder. Former enemies Germany and Japan were remade as democracies.
  • Why it worked. Distance and democracy made America a tolerable hegemon. It couldn't conquer its allies, and it deliberately underused its power over them, opening its markets first. Open trade removed the motive for the old resource wars.
  • Terrifying deterrence. Unable to match Soviet land power, America threatened nuclear war to prevent a conventional one. Eisenhower's massive retaliation gave way to Kennedy's flexible response, and neither was comfortable.
  • Crises. Berlin, the Taiwan Strait and Cuba all posed the same problem: stand firm over small stakes without blundering into apocalypse. The method was firmness plus an off-ramp. In Cuba that meant a blockade, a no-invasion pledge and a private promise on missiles in Turkey.
  • The global south. The contest spread as nearly 100 new states appeared. America backed dictators and coups on the logic of lesser evils. Vietnam was the point of moral and strategic overstretch, and some 14 million people died in Asia's Cold War conflicts.
  • The home front. Critics feared a garrison state. McCarthyism and presidential secrecy did real harm, but Brands argues democracy survived, and the contest pushed along civil rights and funded the technology behind the digital age.
  • The turn. The Sino-Soviet split, widened by American pressure on the weaker partner, led to Nixon's opening to Mao. Andrew Marshall urged steering the rivalry toward American strengths. Reagan piled on military, proxy, economic and ideological pressure, then paired it with five summits after the war scare of 1983.

Why Moscow could not gamble
  • Allied commitments and arms closed the short-war escape that tempted Germany and Japan.
  • Military and technological trends were running against it in the information age.
Why Moscow could afford to quit
  • A democratic Germany and Japan, tied to America, weren't going to attack.
  • Reagan offered respect and a better relationship in return for change.
The achievement, as Brands sees it

The system let America's worst enemy feel safe in defeat. Nobody could have said that in 1914 or 1941. After 1991, for the first time in a century, no power could threaten to dominate Eurasia.

The return · Chapters 5–6

The rematch

The contest hasn't gone anywhere, Brands argues. The tools are newer and the alignments looser, but the stakes and the structure are familiar.

05The Second Eurasian Century

Fortress Eurasia against the free world

China, Russia and Iran want a world safe for autocracy. In Brands's view the best realistic outcome is a new cold war, and the alternative is worse.

  • Why the peace ended. America and its allies had about 70% of world GDP and 75% of military spending after the Cold War. Brands finds four flaws in the plan to lock that in. Integration made rivals rich, democracy failed to take root in Russia and China, American primacy blocked their ambitions and scared their regimes, and Washington took a peace dividend while expanding its goals.
  • Security wasn't the issue. He rejects the idea that NATO threatened Russia's survival. Moscow and Beijing had rarely been safer. What they lacked was empire, glory and regime security.
  • China's hybrid bid. It has both continental depth and an ocean front, the combination Mackinder feared. Its peacetime buildup is the largest since before World War II, it has the most ships and missiles, and its nuclear arsenal doubled from 2020 to 2023. Eastward, Taiwan is the prize: Xi told the PLA to be ready by 2027. Westward, the Belt and Road and digital networks build a Beijing-centred hinterland.
  • Russia's restoration. Too weak for global primacy, Moscow can still wreck the European order. Putin's strategy has four prongs: reclaim the post-Soviet space, split the West, project power into flanking regions, and partner with China.
  • Iran's project in disorder. Qasem Soleimani built a proxy network that may have reached 200,000 troops, and Tehran became dominant in Iraq, Syria, Lebanon and Yemen. Its costs at home are severe, and fear of it is drawing Israel and Gulf states together.
  • What's new. Cheap drones and missiles, cyber and disinformation, renewed ideological rivalry, a race for AI and chips, and interdependence turned into a weapon. AI-driven surveillance may lower the economic cost of repression.
  • Ukraine as Korea. Putin expected a quick decapitation. Incompetence, Ukrainian cohesion and Western arms stopped him. NATO added Finland and Sweden, European defence budgets rose 13% in a year, and Japan moved to nearly double its spending. Brands stresses how close it came, and how incomplete the response remains.
  • Reasons for hope and alarm. American alliances are deep, and China faces demographic decline and a leader who silences dissent. But when troubled autocracies have problems, he warns, they can get more dangerous.

The present-day board

Links show ties, not treaties: arms and chips, energy, drones, artillery shells, joint exercises, and routes built to dodge sanctions and fleets. Brands says these partners distrust each other and have clashing long-term aims, but it wouldn't take a formal alliance to change the balance. The swing states help decide the result by choosing whose side to lean toward, or by playing both.

06Lessons of the Past

Nine lessons for the next round

The threats are new but the problems aren't. Brands's prescription is hawkish and explicit: a long competition, paid for early.

He starts by sketching defeat: Taiwan taken, maritime Asia dominated by Beijing, allies isolated, and autocratic norms spreading outward. He adds that neither Xi nor Putin is Hitler, and that this isn't yet 1938. The front lines run through Ukraine and Taiwan, not Hawaii, which he reads as proof of how strong the American-led position still is.

  1. Regimes matterIdeology and geopolitics can't be separated. The worst aggressors were tyrannies, and their strengths were also their flaws. Dictators who silence debate get worse at judging.
  2. America is the balancerLate in both world wars, but lethal, and transformative once it stayed. He dismisses talk of an easy retreat into a multipolar world.
  3. Eurasia is one theatrePrioritise, as Germany-first did, but don't write regions off. The danger of seeing everything as linked was Vietnam. The danger of the opposite is a one-war military facing trouble in two or three places.
  4. Coalitions decideThey have to be built and tended. Japan may be to this century what Britain was to the last. Think flexible groupings like AUKUS, and an India that won't be anyone's ally but can still help.
  5. Virtue needs sordid helpDemocracies beat Hitler with Stalin and Moscow with Mao. Today that means Saudi Arabia, Turkey and Vietnam. Pressure on Russia may even push it into a dependence on China that later splits them.
  6. Competition is technologicalRun faster and slow the rival down. He supports restricting China's access to capital, markets and inputs, and accepts that this fragments the economy.
  7. A cold war is the rewardThat's the prize for deterring a hot one. Peace was the product of decades of effort, and the military balance has to be kept.
  8. Mind the home hemisphereEurasian rivals always stir trouble in Latin America. A tighter North American bloc adds strength.
  9. Nothing is destinedExpansion doesn't have to fail, and progress is what power makes of it. Every round could have gone the other way.

The chapter numbers most of these and leaves the third implicit

Takeaways

The toolkit

The book is a history with a policy argument attached. These are habits for reading the news through Brands's framework, stated as his positions rather than settled fact.

Read the board

Ask who controls whatLook at rims, inner seas and chokepoints. His examples include Ukraine, Taiwan and the South China Sea.
Look at the regimeBrands ties expansion to how a state is ruled, not only to where it sits.
Watch closing windowsA rising power that feels time slipping may be the most dangerous.
Link the theatresHis test: what does a move in one region do to options in another?

Build the coalition

Commit before the crisisUnity arrived too late in 1914 and 1939. He credits peacetime alliances with changing that.
Tend relationships constantlyA diplomat called it gardening. Alliances need upkeep between emergencies.
Lead without dictatingHe argues America gained by underusing its power over friends.
Expect awkward partnersHe sees moral compromise as the recurring cost of holding the balance, and doesn't hide it.

Pay early

Balance early, not lateGeorge Marshall's complaint: years with time but no money, then money but no time.
Compete on your strengthsAndrew Marshall's approach: steer the rivalry where it costs the other side most.
Firmness plus an off-rampThe Cold War pattern: hold the line and leave a way down.
Don't trust inevitabilityPast wins were narrow. He warns against treating them as a law of history.
