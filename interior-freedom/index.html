Jacques Philippe

Interior
Freedom

La liberté intérieure · 2002
translated by Helena Scott

A short, quiet book by a priest of the Community of the Beatitudes in France, written for Easter 2002. Its claim is that every Christian, even in the worst outward circumstances, carries "a space of freedom that nobody can take away, because God is its source and guarantee." Philippe doesn't argue from theory. He argues from Thérèse of Lisieux in her tiny convent, from Etty Hillesum on her way to Auschwitz, from St. Peter weeping in a courtyard, and from twenty-five years of community life with people he found hard to live with.

Scepter Publishers, English edition 2007 · Five parts · ~150 pages · Philippe also wrote Searching for and Maintaining Peace, Time for God and In the School of the Holy Spirit

"We gain possession of our interior freedom in exact proportion to our growth in faith, hope, and love."

The thesis, from the introduction. Philippe calls the whole book a commentary on the first beatitude: "Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven."

Part I · Freedom and acceptance

The sky within me is as wide as the sky above

Modern culture and Christianity agree on one word: freedom. It is close to the only value everyone still shares. Philippe takes that seriously. We long for freedom because we long for happiness, and there is no happiness without love and no love without freedom: "Love is neither taken nor bought." But he names three mistakes that keep people from the real thing.

  • Autonomy or dependence? For modern people freedom often means "neither God nor master." For the Gospel it is a gift received in "loving dependence" on God. "Whoever would save his life will lose it." The saints gave themselves away completely and came out feeling immensely free.
  • Outward or inward? We blame circumstances, push back limits, and find more limits behind them. Philippe's image is the last scene of Luc Besson's film The Big Blue: the hero swims off after the dolphins, and "the film omits to state the obvious: he condemns himself to certain death."
  • Choosing or consenting? We picture freedom as a supermarket or a mail-order catalogue: the more options, the freer. But the most important things in life (our sex, our parents, our mother tongue, much of our character) we never chose, and the range of options only narrows with age.

Thérèse's pocket-handkerchief

Philippe once helped carry Thérèse's relics out of the Lisieux Carmel and saw her world for the first time. Everything was smaller than he'd imagined. The great hayfield he had pictured from her memoirs was "a mere pocket-handkerchief." Ten years in a plain provincial convent, with sisters whose manners "often left much to be desired." Yet her writing feels vast: "infinite horizons," "oceans of graces," "torrents of mercy." Why? "Quite simply because Thérèse loves intensely."

"You are not restricted by us, but you are restricted in your own hearts."2 Corinthians 6:12, the verse that came to him in Lisieux

His conclusion: we blame our surroundings when "our lack of freedom stems from a lack of love." People who haven't learned to love will feel like victims wherever they are. And the reason we can't love, Thérèse taught him, is usually a lack of faith and of hope.

"And everywhere signs barring Jews from the paths and the open country. But above the one narrow path still left to us stretches the sky, intact. They can't do anything to us, they really can't… I find life beautiful, and I feel free. The sky within me is as wide as the one stretching above my head."Etty Hillesum, a young Jewish woman in occupied Amsterdam, 1942. She died at Auschwitz in November 1943.

Three ways to meet what we didn't choose

"There is a paradoxical law of human life here: one cannot become truly free unless one accepts not always being free!" The situations that really make us grow are exactly the ones we don't control. Facing them, we have three options.

first reactionRebellionRejecting reality. Sometimes a healthy first cry, but "it has never solved anything." It adds a second wrong to the first.
a stage, not a homeResignationMore realistic, less aggressive. "A virtue for philosophers," but not Christian, "since it doesn't include hope."
the aimConsentSaying yes to a reality we first saw as negative, because something good can come of it. The facts are unchanged; the heart already holds faith, hope and love "in embryo."

Consent is not passivity. Where something can and should be changed, we act. Consent is for what remains after that.

Accepting ourselves

"God is realistic." Grace does not work on our ideals or dreams of who we should be. "God doesn't love 'ideal persons' or 'virtual beings.'" What often blocks grace is less our sins than our refusal to accept our weakness. Sometimes a simple, trusting "yes" to something we've rejected in ourselves sets grace free. Wanting to grow and accepting ourselves are not opposites: "we can only transform reality fruitfully if we accept it first."

  • We need another's eyes. A girl who thinks she is plain begins to doubt it the day a young man looks at her "with the tender eyes of someone in love." Above all we need God's eyes: "You are precious in my eyes, and honored, and I love you" (Isaiah 43:4).
  • A double freedom. Under that gaze we are free to be sinners (not crushed by it, with "a sort of 'right' to be poor") and free to become saints. Never imprisoned in our mediocrity, never forced to be "winners."
  • Today's guilt is worse. The world left Christianity as a "religion of guilt," yet "girls feel guilty for not being as beautiful as the latest fashion model. Men feel guilty for not being as successful as the inventor of Microsoft."
  • Unmask limiting beliefs. "I'll never manage that" is often an old wound, not humility. "We don't all have in us the stuff of sages or heroes. But by God's grace we do have the stuff of saints."
"Above all, never despise yourself. It is difficult to despise ourselves without offending God in us."The old prioress to Blanche, in Bernanos' Dialogues of the Carmelites
"For a very long time I considered low self-esteem to be some kind of virtue… But now I realize that the real sin is to deny God's first love for me."Henri Nouwen, The Return of the Prodigal Son

Accepting suffering

"Peaceful suffering is no longer suffering."The Curé of Ars

The worst pain of suffering is rejecting it, and the worst kind of suffering is the one we only imagine. "Man suffers most through his fear of suffering" (Hillesum). Take the aspirin when there is one. But fleeing ordinary, acceptable suffering lands us in worse: Philippe has seen parents torment themselves for years because they couldn't accept a child's vocation.

  • Difficulties have advantages. They stop us owning our lives and our plans. "The worst thing that could happen would be for everything to go exactly as we wanted it."
  • The need to understand. It is good to want meaning. But the wish to understand can be a wish to control, or a search for security. Some seasons ask us to stop decoding and trust: "What I am doing you do not understand now, but afterward you will understand" (John 13:7).
  • When God's will is unclear. He treats us as adults, and uncertainty keeps us humble. Meanwhile, invest in what is certain: the duties of your state, prayer, loving the people around you today.
  • "I choose to be interrupted." When Thérèse finally found an hour for a painting or a play, she began it that way. If a sister came, fine. If no one came, a gift. "Her will was to accept everything."

Jesus' life was taken from him; yet "no one takes my life from me, but I lay it down of my own accord." Jacques Fesch, executed in 1957 at twenty-seven for killing a policeman, found Christ in his cell and wrote days before his death: "I cooperate with this execution by accepting it with all my soul." Out of this comes one of the book's central lines:

"We are not always masters of the unfolding of our lives, but we can always be masters of the meaning we give them."

Our freedom can turn what is taken into what is offered: "fate into free choice, constraint into love, loss into fruitfulness." And when we can do nothing at all, say for a grown child on drugs, we can still believe, hope and love. "The more devoid of means our love is, the purer and greater it is."

Accepting other people

It's easier to forgive a broken-down car than a wife who stayed an hour on the phone. Philippe asks two things.

  • Allow for temperament. Many "moral" conflicts are just order-lovers against freedom-lovers, early birds against night owls, talkers against the taciturn. "I have lived in a community for twenty-five years, and I may have received more from people I found it hard to get along with than from those I found congenial."
  • Forgive real wrongs. Forgiving isn't calling bad good. It means not identifying the person with the fault, leaving judgment to God, and hoping for him. Resentment chains us to the person we resent, like a stack of "bills" we keep to present later. "Those accumulated bills end up poisoning our lives."
  • Others' faults take nothing from us. Nobody can stop us believing, hoping and loving. "That others are sinners cannot prevent us from becoming saints."
  • Don't feed the gloom. A favourite trick of the devil is to make us dwell on everything wrong in the community until fervour dies. A priest told Philippe: "I'm not going to spend my life denouncing sin. That would be doing it too much honor." John of the Cross: "Where there is no love, put love, and you will harvest love."

"Evil comes to fill a gap." It enters a heart only where there is room. Maximilian Kolbe and his companions sang the Magnificat in the starvation bunker at Auschwitz. "The harm that other people do to me never comes from them, it comes from me," as the Fathers said. Part I ends with royal freedom, St. Paul's "All things are yours; and you are Christ's; and Christ is God's."

Part II · The present moment

"To love" has a present tense only

We can't change the smallest bit of the past, and very little of the future. "All we have is the present moment," and only there can we act freely. That sounds bleak until you add faith: God is the eternal present. The past is in the hands of his mercy; the future in the hands of his providence.

  • The ladder has one step. Spiritual writers count three, seven or twelve stages. Philippe jokes "that the ladder of perfection has only one step: the step we take today." St. Anthony of Egypt, at a hundred: "I haven't yet begun to be converted!" Thérèse: "To love thee, O Jesus, I have but today."
  • We can suffer for only one moment. Thérèse in her last illness: "I only suffer for one moment. It is because people think about the past and the future that they become discouraged and despair." Nobody can bear twenty years of pain at once; we get grace for today's.
  • Let the day's own trouble be enough. We pile regrets about yesterday and fears about tomorrow on top of today and then wonder why we're crushed. Once we've said sorry and meant it, "that is enough."
  • Grace is like manna. It can't be stockpiled. Most of our fears turn out imaginary; the real difficulties are ones we never thought of. "The best way to prepare for the future is to put our hearts into the present."
  • Live, instead of waiting to live. "We may spend our whole lives waiting to live." God asks one thing at a time, never two, whether it's sweeping the kitchen or speaking to forty thousand people.
  • Be available. In every encounter, make the other person feel you're there for them "one hundred percent." Children don't need constant attention, but they do need regular times when Mom or Dad have no other concern. Anxiety steals that.
"We have to fight them daily, like fleas, those many small worries about the morrow, for they sap our energies… Ultimately, we have just one moral duty: to reclaim large areas of peace in ourselves, more and more peace, and to reflect it towards others."Etty Hillesum
A prayer for a wasted past. When the sense of lost years threatens to swamp you, Philippe suggests: "Thank you, my God, for all my past. I firmly believe that you can draw good out of everything I have lived through. I want to have no regrets, and I resolve today to begin from zero, with exactly the same trust as if all my past history were made up of nothing but faithfulness and holiness."

He ends the part with two kinds of time. Head-time, measured and managed, always runs too fast or too slow. Heart-time is "God's time," a chain of full moments each done in communion with his will. The saints lived in it, ready to drop their plans in an instant. "The devil slips into time we live badly."

Part III · The dynamism of faith, hope and love

The fire that lights, burns, and transfigures

"Virtue" comes from virtus, strength. Faith, hope and charity are not decorations on the Christian life; they are its engine. "Christians are not people who follow a set of rules." Everything else (commandments, prayer, sacraments, even the highest mystical experiences) exists to make these three grow. Each is God's gift and our free act at once: "I believe what I want to believe," said Thérèse in her final darkness. "I have created you without you, but I will not save you without you," the Lord told Catherine of Siena.

Peter's three outpourings of the Spirit

Ask charismatics when Peter received the Holy Spirit and they say Pentecost. Philippe counts three times, like the joyful, sorrowful and glorious mysteries of the Rosary.

JoyfulThe call by the lakePeter leaves nets, boat and family, seized by "No man ever spoke like this man!" The Spirit lights things up. gives faith
SorrowfulThe courtyardA servant girl's question and three denials. Then Jesus' eyes: not condemned, loved. He weeps, and moves "from presumption to hope." teaches hope
GloriousPentecostPower from on high: the fire of love, the courage to give his life to tending the flock. gives charity

"Judas, why did you avoid Jesus' eyes…? Your sin was no worse than Peter's." Hope is "the virtue of people who know they are infinitely weak and easily broken," and it can only be learned by being impoverished. "As long as we are rich, we rely on our riches." The Desert Fathers: "Someone who sees his own sin is greater than someone who raises the dead."

John of the Cross pictured the same sequence as fire meeting a log. First it warms and lights the wood. Then the wood blackens, smokes, smells and oozes tar: the soul sees its own wretchedness. Finally the log becomes fire. So don't fear the smoking phase. "However far away we are, he will transform us into flames of love" (Thérèse).

Why hope is the key

Love is the greatest; faith and hope will pass away in heaven. But love "cannot exist without its two 'servants.'" Love needs room to grow, and that room is hope. When love goes cold it is usually choked by worry, fear and discouragement. Jesus to Faustina: "The greatest obstacles to holiness are discouragement and worry." The will only wants what it sees as possible; hope makes it possible. And hope stands on faith's bedrock.

The bottom row is Genesis 3. It starts with suspicion: is God really as good as he says? Doubt breeds distrust, and distrust tries to manage alone, which is where selfishness, lust, fear and violence come from. The top row runs the other way, which is why Jesus makes so much of faith.

"We obtain from God as much as we hope for from him."John of the Cross, the line that set Thérèse on her "little way." God gives "not according to our merits, but according to our hope."

"A man of faith is not one who believes that God can do everything, but one who believes he can obtain everything from God" (John Climacus). And the pure in heart, in the prophets' sense, are not people without faults or wounds; they are people who hope in God alone, instead of "shopping around for salvation."

But it's from sullied water, old water, stale water.
But it's from an impure soul that she makes a pure soul
and that's the most beautiful secret in the whole garden of the world.Charles Péguy, The Portal of the Mystery of Hope, on hope

Part IV · From law to grace

Love as a free gift

"For freedom Christ has set us free" (Galatians 5:1). Paul sees two ways Christians lose that freedom. The trap of the flesh is easy to see: licentiousness passes itself off as freedom and is really slavery. Philippe adds two notes for today. Our efforts alone never win that fight; prayer, patience and hope do. And "one passion can only be cured by another": a misplaced love by a greater love.

The trap of the law is subtler, because it can look like the strictest morality.

Under the lawUnder grace
Where salvation comes fromEarned by my good worksReceived freely in Christ, then answered with works the Spirit makes possible
If I succeedPride; contempt for those who don't (the Pharisee)Gratitude: "What have you that you did not receive?"
If I failDespair: I'm finishedI can always return to unconditional love
Its typical facesPaying a debt to God; fear and guilt; calculating merits; legalism that makes life impossible for othersGiving everything back out of love, not repayment
What it does to loveKills itLets it grow

Learning to love, Philippe says, is "extremely simple": learning to give freely and to receive freely. Both are hard. We give in order to get something back, and we can only receive what we think we've earned. Receiving freely takes trust and a lot of humility. He calls the Gospel an "evolutionary ferment" in human psychology, conditioned by thousands of years of survival, that slowly moves us toward a Kingdom with no "rights" and "duties," "no more opposition between 'yours' and 'mine.'"

Part V · Spiritual poverty and freedom

The truly free person has nothing left to lose

One of our deepest needs is to know who we are. We try three floors.

HavingThe motorbike, the yacht, the look. Lonely the day you realise people wanted your money, not you.
DoingTalents and achievements. But what if the footballer ends up in a wheelchair, or the literature professor loses his memory?
BeingA child of God, loved before doing anything. "If our treasure is in God, no one can take it from us."

Pride builds an artificial self to make up for a felt lack of being, and that self must be guarded at great cost. The same happens in spiritual life. Philippe has met religious who gave everything to an apostolate and, when illness or a superior stopped them, "no longer knew who they were." Identifying with the good we do is still a fragile identity. "Humility is truth. I am what I am in God's eyes: a poor child who possesses absolutely nothing, who receives everything, infinitely loved and totally free."

Why the dark nights come

The trials the mystics describe strip away whatever we lean on in ourselves. You can lose the feeling of love for God without losing the love. A priest once told Philippe in confession: "When you no longer believe in what you can do for God, continue believing in what God can do for you."

"…they begin to understand that God's greatness is not to be measured by the yardstick of man's futility… They no longer consider these things as the price of God's love, but as responses to His love and fatherly care."Matthew the Poor (Matta El-Maskeen), Egyptian monk, on souls God lets fall into spiritual lukewarmness

The same can happen through any trial: failure, sickness, depression, even the consequences of our own sins. "We can draw great spiritual profit from a trial with nothing spiritual about it." What's left is a person who relies on mercy alone, receives others' kindness gratefully but doesn't depend on it, and isn't thrown by their own weakness.

Behind which barbed wire?

In Solzhenitsyn's The First Circle, a powerful party official needs a prisoner-scientist for a project his career depends on. The free man in the room is the prisoner: he is ready to go back to Siberia. In Westerbork camp Etty Hillesum heard an old man look past the fence at the villas and say: "They are the ones who live behind barbed wire."

"Love is what remains when nothing remains."Christiane Singer, Du bon usage des crises, quoted by Philippe: beyond every disaster lies "an intact space, that of the love that was the foundation of our being"

The book closes with a dialogue between Jesus and an anonymous Spanish writer. Jesus' greatest joy, he says, is "when, in lucid freedom, you say yes to God's calls." And: "Don't be afraid of yourselves!… God's new name is Emmanuel, God with us: God with your reality… not as spectator, not as judge, but as someone who loves you."

Practice

Turning what is taken into what is given

Philippe's method is always the same: name what you can't change, then move from rebellion and resignation to consent filled with faith, hope and love. Pick a situation you're in today.

To keep

Seven things to remember

  1. Interior freedom grows exactly as faith, hope and love grow. Nobody can stop you believing, hoping and loving, so nobody can take it.
  2. Feeling hemmed in is usually a lack of love, not a lack of space. Thérèse's hayfield was a handkerchief.
  3. Freedom isn't only choosing. Its deepest form is consenting to what you didn't choose, with hope.
  4. God is realistic. His grace works on the person you are, not the one you wish you were. Say yes to yourself before you try to change.
  5. You can't always master what happens, but you can master the meaning you give it: what is taken can become an offering.
  6. Live today. Grace comes like manna, one day's worth. The ladder of perfection has only one step.
  7. Hope is learned in poverty. When you no longer believe in what you can do for God, keep believing in what God can do for you.
