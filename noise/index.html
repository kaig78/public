Noise

A Flaw in Human Judgment

Same case, same facts, different professional, different answer. The authors argue that this scatter does as much harm as bias and gets a fraction of the attention.

The big idea

Two kinds of error, and we only talk about one

Bias is error that leans one way. Noise is error that scatters. The authors are Daniel Kahneman, a psychologist, Olivier Sibony, a strategy professor, and Cass Sunstein, a legal scholar. Bias, they complain, stars in every discussion of judgment while noise goes unmeasured.

Noise here means unwanted variability in judgments that ought to match: two doctors, one patient; two underwriters, one risk. It isn't diversity of taste or competition between ideas. It matters where an organisation treats its professionals as interchangeable and assigns a case to whoever is free.

The book has a surprising practical core. You can measure noise without knowing the right answer, and you can reduce it without knowing which errors you're preventing. They call that second part decision hygiene.

Part I · Chapters 1–3

Finding noise

A courtroom shows noise can be unjust. An insurer shows it can be expensive. A one-off decision shows that seeing no scatter doesn't mean there isn't any.

01Crime and Noisy Punishment

A sentence shouldn't depend on the judge you draw

Discretion once looked humane. Then people measured what it produced.

  • In 1973 the US judge Marvin Frankel collected real cases to show how much a sentence depended on which judge you drew. Two men, both first offenders, were both convicted of cashing small bad cheques. One got fifteen years for $58.40; the other got 30 days for $35.20. What it shows: nearly the same crime, a huge difference in punishment. The difference came from the judge, not from the case.
  • To test this properly, a 1981 study sent 208 federal judges the same sixteen made-up case files and asked each for a sentence. On only three of the sixteen cases did all the judges even agree that prison was the right outcome. What it shows: every judge saw identical facts, so all of that disagreement is noise.
  • Judges are also moved by things that should play no role. Studies link harsher rulings to the local football team losing the weekend before, milder ones to the day being the defendant's birthday, and harsher ones to hot weather. What it shows: the same judge can rule differently depending on the day. The book calls this occasion noise (chapter 7).
  • Congress reacted with the Sentencing Reform Act of 1984: binding guidelines with 43 offence levels told judges which range to use, and studies found that judges disagreed less. In 2005 the Supreme Court made the guidelines advisory, and most judges welcomed the freedom. Economist Crystal Yang's data on nearly 400,000 defendants shows what happened next: the extra prison time for drawing a harsh judge instead of an average one was 2.8 months under the binding guidelines and roughly doubled once they became optional. What it shows: when the rule that limited discretion was removed, the noise came back.
02A Noisy System

The noise audit

Give many professionals the same file separately, then compare. The result is nearly always a shock.

  • A large insurer asked its underwriters (who set the price of a policy) and its claims adjusters (who estimate how much to pay out) to work on the same realistic cases, each on their own. Pick any two of them: the typical gap between their numbers was 55% for underwriters and 43% for adjusters. In money: if one underwriter quoted $9,500, a colleague would typically quote $16,700 for the identical policy, not the $10,500 managers had guessed. One executive put the cost at hundreds of millions a year, because overcharged clients leave and undercharged ones lose the company money. What it shows: the price a client pays depends heavily on which employee happens to handle the file.
  • Executives expected far less. Asked beforehand, 828 senior managers guessed a gap of about 10%. At an investment firm, 42 investors who valued the same stock differed by 41%. What it shows: people inside an organisation hugely underestimate how much their colleagues disagree.
  • Why random errors still hurt: in real life each case is judged once, by one person. If one client is overcharged and another undercharged, the company has made two mistakes. The errors only cancel out in an average on paper, never for the client in front of you.
  • Why nobody notices: the authors call it the illusion of agreement. Experienced staff rarely see a colleague judge the same case, and they feel sure of themselves because they agree with their own earlier judgments. Organisations rarely check.
03Singular Decisions

One shot still has a scatter

A one-off choice is a repeated decision that happens to be made once.

  • In 2014, during the Ebola outbreak, President Obama decided not to close US borders and instead sent about three thousand people to West Africa to fight the disease there. It was a one-time decision. With different advisers, a different mood in the room or a different first speaker, it could easily have gone another way.
  • You can't measure noise in a decision that is made only once, because there is nothing to compare it with. But the same sources of noise are at work: who is in the room, their mood, the order of the arguments. A single shot from an unsteady hand is still unsteady, even if you only see one hole. What it shows: the methods that reduce noise in repeated decisions (chapters 19–25) also help with big one-off decisions.
Part II · Chapters 4–8

Your mind is a measuring instrument

A judgment assigns a value on a scale, and the instrument is a person. Like any instrument it can be off-centre, shaky, or both.

04Matters of Judgment

Between arithmetic and taste

A judgment call is a question where we allow some disagreement, but not much.

  • In a class exercise, 115 MBA students read the same profile of a fictional CEO candidate and rated, on a scale from 0 to 100, how likely he was to succeed. Their answers ranged from 10 to 95. What it shows: even with identical information, people differ at every step: which facts they notice, how they weigh them, and how they turn an impression into a number.
  • How do we know a judgment is finished? Not because we have checked the answer, but because it suddenly feels right and the pieces fit. The authors call this the internal signal of judgment completion. It feels like confidence, but it says nothing about whether the answer is correct.
  • There are two kinds of judgment. Predictive judgments have a true answer that will be known later, such as whether a candidate succeeds; if two people disagree, at least one is wrong. Evaluative judgments have no single correct answer, such as a fair sentence or the grade an essay deserves. There is no bull's-eye, but if two people get different outcomes for the same case, that is still unfair. Because single outcomes are often unknowable, the authors suggest judging the quality of the process rather than one result.
05Measuring Error

Bias and noise count the same

Overall error is bias squared plus noise squared. A given cut to either buys the same improvement.

  • The standard way to measure total error goes back to the mathematician Gauss in 1795: take each error, square it, and average the squares (the mean squared error). Squaring makes big misses count far more than small ones. Getting from 11 cm off to 10 cm off lowers the squared error by 21 (121 to 100); getting from 1 cm off to a perfect hit lowers it by only 1. What it shows: this is why a wide scatter of judgments is so costly.
  • The formula splits neatly into two parts: total error = bias² + noise². Bias is how far the average judgment is from the truth. Noise is how widely the judgments scatter around their own average.
  • The book's example: at the fictional firm GoodSell, employees forecast a product's market share. Their forecasts averaged 44% and scattered with a standard deviation of 10 points; the real share turned out to be 34%. So bias was 10 (44 against 34) and noise was also 10. The boss wanted to fix the bias first. The formula shows that this is a mistake: cutting noise by some amount improves accuracy exactly as much as cutting bias by the same amount, and you don't need to know the true answer to reduce noise.
  • Two more points. Bias has to be very large before it does as much harm as noise: with normally distributed errors, only when 84% of forecasts miss in the same direction. And the formula applies only to predictions that have a true answer. For mixed questions, split them: first estimate the facts neutrally (how much weight can this lift carry?), then decide the values question separately (how much safety margin do we want?).

06The Analysis of Noise

Level noise and pattern noise

Some judges are harsher overall. They also disagree about which cases deserve it.

  • Back to the 1981 sentencing study: 208 judges each sentencing 16 cases gives a table of 3,328 sentences. The judges' personal average sentences ran from 1 year for the most lenient to 15.3 years for the harshest. For a typical case the average sentence was 7.0 years, and the standard deviation across judges was 3.4 years. That 3.4 years is the system noise: roughly how much your sentence depends on which judge you get.
  • Part of it is level noise: some judges are simply harsher than others across every case. Here it accounted for 2.4 years. It behaves like a personality trait.
  • The rest is pattern noise: judges also disagree about which cases are serious. One is especially tough on fraud, another on violence. Here it was about as large as level noise. What it shows: even if every judge had the same average severity, they would still disagree a lot, case by case.

07Occasion Noise

You disagree with yourself

Your answer is one draw from a cloud of answers you could have given.

  • Same person, different day: wine experts who tasted the same wines twice, without knowing it, gave the identical score only 18% of the time. What it shows: a judgment is not one fixed answer. It is one draw from all the answers you might have given. This is occasion noise.
  • The crowd within: psychologists Vul and Pashler asked people for an estimate and later asked them again. The average of a person's two guesses was more accurate than either guess. Asked straight away, the second guess was worth about a tenth of a second person's opinion; three weeks later, about a third; and if people were told to assume their first answer was wrong and argue against it, about half. What it shows: sleep on a judgment, or argue against yourself, and then average the two answers.
  • What shifts us from moment to moment: mood (people in a good mood are more easily fooled), fatigue (doctors prescribe more opioids late in the day) and order (US asylum judges are 19% less likely to grant asylum right after granting it in the two previous cases, as if to make up for being lenient).
  • The reassuring part: we resemble ourselves more than we resemble our colleagues. Wherever it could be measured, occasion noise was smaller than the differences between people. It is also hard to pin down: in one tightly controlled memory study, all the measured factors together explained only 11% of a person's day-to-day variation.
08How Groups Amplify Noise

Discussion can make it worse

Averaging independent views removes noise. Letting people hear each other first can add it back.

  • Sociologist Matthew Salganik set up eight parallel online music worlds with the same 72 unknown songs. In some of them, listeners could see how often each song had been downloaded. In those worlds the final rankings came out very different from one another: apart from the very best and the very worst songs, early random downloads decided which songs became hits. What it shows: when people can see what others think, small early accidents get amplified, and group judgment becomes noisier.
  • On a news website, researchers gave some comments a single fake up-vote at the start. Five months later, those comments had an average rating 25% higher than comparable ones. What it shows: one click at the beginning shaped thousands of later judgments.
  • Two group effects drive this. In a cascade, people follow whoever spoke first, because they reasonably trust that person or don't want to look difficult. In polarisation, a group ends up more extreme after discussion than its members were at the start.
  • In the authors' study of mock juries deciding damages, juries that talked it through were much noisier than “statistical juries” made by simply taking the middle value of six people's separate opinions. And in 27% of cases, the deliberating jury ended at or above the amount its harshest member had wanted alone. What it shows: averaging independent views reduces noise; discussing first can increase it.

Part III · Chapters 9–12

Noise in predictive judgments

Predictions can be checked, so they show how much noise costs. The measure used is percent concordant (PC): the chance that the person rated higher really does come out ahead. 50% is a coin toss.

09Judgments and Models

The model of you beats you

Most of what feels like subtlety in expert judgment turns out to be noise.

  • In 1954 psychologist Paul Meehl compared the predictions of clinicians (for example about patients or students) with simple statistical formulas that used the same information. The formulas usually did better. A 2000 review of 136 studies counted: the formula won 63, there were 65 ties, and the humans won only 8.
  • Lewis Goldberg went a step further. He built a simple formula that imitated each individual expert, based on how that person had weighed the facts in earlier cases. The surprise: this crude copy usually predicted better than the expert it copied. In one replication it did so for all 98 participants. The model keeps the expert's general rules and drops the random inconsistency. What it shows: much of what feels like subtle expert judgment is actually noise, and removing it makes predictions better.
  • Yu and Kuncel tested this with 847 candidates for executive jobs. Even 10,000 formulas with random weights for the factors beat the human experts 77% of the time in one sample and 100% of the time in two others. What it shows: being consistent matters more than being clever.
  • Why we resist: the illusion of validity. When a case looks convincing, such as a coherent, impressive candidate, we feel sure of our prediction, even though how good someone looks is only weakly linked to how they will actually do.

10Noiseless Rules

From back-of-envelope to machine learning

Rules at both ends of the sophistication scale beat people, and being noise-free is a large part of why.

  • Even very simple rules work. In 1974 Robyn Dawes showed that just adding up the relevant factors with equal weight (after putting them on a common scale) predicts about as well as a carefully fitted statistical model. A fitted model also learns the accidents of the data it was fitted on: in one example its accuracy (as a correlation) dropped from .69 to .57 on new data, while the simple equal-weight sum reached .60.
  • For bail decisions, a rule with just two inputs, the defendant's age and the number of court dates they had missed before, predicted who would fail to appear better than nearly all human judges.
  • Sendhil Mullainathan and colleagues trained a machine-learning model on 758,027 real bail decisions. Used instead of the judges, it could either cut crime by released defendants by up to 24% while jailing the same number of people, or jail up to 42% fewer people with no rise in crime. Matched to the judges' crime rate, it would also jail 41% fewer people of colour. The authors add a warning: algorithms can also lock in discrimination if they learn from biased data.
  • When should a person overrule a model? Meehl's broken leg example: a formula predicts that someone will go to the cinema tonight, but you know he has just broken his leg. Override the model when you have decisive information it could not consider, not because you dislike its answer.
11Objective Ignorance

The biggest limit is the world

What can't be known caps every forecast, and gut feeling acts as if it doesn't.

  • There are two kinds of not knowing: what simply cannot be known, because the future depends on events nobody can foresee (the authors call this objective ignorance), and what could be known but you don't know. Acting as if you can predict well anyway is the denial of ignorance.
  • Philip Tetlock tracked the predictions of almost 300 political and economic experts over two decades. On average they did hardly better than chance, which he compared to a chimp throwing darts. The experts with one big, clear theory were the most confident and the least accurate.
  • So models beat people, but only by a small margin, because the world is hard to predict. In the review's terms, clinicians were right 68% of the time and formulas 73%. A heart-attack model with more than 2,400 variables still found only 30% of actual heart attacks among the patients it rated as highest risk.
  • Why use a model that is only slightly better? Nobody would buy a lottery ticket with a 59% chance of winning when one with 65% costs the same. What really makes people resist is having to give up the satisfying feeling of “I know” (the internal signal from chapter 4).
12The Valley of the Normal

Why everything looks explainable afterwards

Events we could not have predicted still feel understood once they've happened.

  • In the Fragile Families challenge, 160 research teams received thousands of data points on almost five thousand children and had to predict how they would be doing at age fifteen, for example whether the family would be evicted. The best model reached a correlation of only .22, which means it picked the right one of two children just 57% of the time, barely better than a coin toss. For comparison, a typical finding in social psychology has a correlation of about .21. What it shows: even with rich data, individual lives are very hard to predict.
  • Yet after the fact, everything seems explainable. When something happens, our mind finds a cause and stops looking, so the result feels as if it had to happen. Had the opposite happened, we would have found an equally convincing story. The authors call this the valley of the normal: in hindsight, events look normal and expected.
  • Causal thinking tells a story about this one case; it is natural and easy. Statistical thinking, also called the outside view, sees the case as one of many similar ones and asks how such cases usually turn out; it takes effort. What it shows: noise only shows up in the statistical view, which is why we so easily overlook it.
“Causally, noise is nowhere; statistically, it is everywhere.”
Kahneman, Sibony and Sunstein, ch. 17
Part IV · Chapters 13–17

How noise happens

The machinery of fast thinking produces shared error when everyone trips the same way, and noise when each person trips differently.

13Heuristics, Biases, and Noise

Three families of bias

Psychological bias becomes statistical bias when shared, and noise when it differs from person to person.

  • Substitution: we answer an easier question than the one asked. Asked how likely something is, we judge how much it looks like a typical example, and forget how common it is in general (the base rate). Example: about 15% of CEOs are replaced each year, so roughly 72% of new CEOs are still in post after two years (85% of 85%). Few people think to start from that number; they judge the individual's story instead.
  • Conclusion bias: we start from the answer we want and then look for reasons. Anchoring belongs here: a number we have just seen pulls our estimate toward it. Excessive coherence: first impressions colour everything after them. The same four adjectives about a job candidate create a very different impression depending on which one comes first.
  • How this turns into noise: if everyone makes the same mistake in the same direction, it is bias. If each person makes it differently, one anchoring high and another low, it is noise.
14The Matching Operation

Too extreme, and too coarse

We translate intensities across scales effortlessly, and the translation ignores how weak the link is.

  • The Julie example: you are told only that Julie could read fluently at age four, and asked to guess her university grade average (GPA, on a scale up to 4.0). Most people say 3.7 or 3.8. They are matching: her early reading was unusually good, so they pick an unusually good GPA, as if early reading predicted grades perfectly. In reality the link is weak; the authors put the correlation at .20 at most.
  • Such predictions are nonregressive: they forget that extreme results tend to be followed by more ordinary ones. A director's next film after a huge hit is usually less of a hit.
  • People can reliably tell apart only about seven categories on a rating scale. Comparing two cases side by side (is A better than B?) is far more precise than giving each an absolute score, though it takes more effort. 15Scales

Jurors agree on outrage and not on dollars

Much disagreement lies in the ruler, not in what people think.

  • Kahneman, Sunstein and David Schkade gave 899 people descriptions of injury lawsuits against companies. Some rated how outrageous the company's behaviour was, some how severely it should be punished (both on a scale from 0 to 6), and some how many dollars of punitive damages to award. Average outrage and average punishment ratings matched almost perfectly (correlation .98). What it shows: when people decide how much to punish, they are really answering “how angry am I?”, and on that they largely agree.
  • The disagreement comes from the dollar scale. It has no upper limit and no natural reference point, so the first amount a person picks is arbitrary, and their other awards follow from it. Turning each person's dollar awards into ranks (which case gets the most, which the second most) removes most of the disagreement: the chart shows the noise share falling from 94% to 49%.
  • US law forbids telling juries what was awarded in similar cases. The authors argue that this assumes jurors can translate outrage into dollars consistently, which they cannot, and that an agreed reference case would anchor the scale.

16Patterns

Your judgment personality

Pattern noise isn't random. It's as personal and persistent as character.

  • Give people a more complicated life story for Julie and they latch onto different details: one notices her parents' divorce, another her talent for maths. Their predictions then differ more.
  • Pattern noise has a passing part, which is occasion noise such as today's mood, and a stable part: your personal way of reacting to certain kinds of cases. A defendant who reminds a judge of a relative will remind him of that relative next week too.
  • Each judge has a judgment personality, a signature set of reactions. What it shows: in a friend that is charming; in a professional who is supposed to be interchangeable with colleagues, it is error.
17The Sources of Noise

The biggest part is the one they missed

The authors began by thinking of residual noise as random. It turned out to be mostly stable and personal.

  • Researchers built a model of each of 173 bail judges from 141,833 of their real cases, so they could compare the judges on identical cases. Stable pattern noise, meaning each judge's consistent personal reactions to particular kinds of defendants, made up 26% of all the variation in decisions. Level noise, some judges simply being stricter, made up only 7%.
  • Why that matters: level noise is the only kind an organisation can spot without a special test, because you can see that one judge's average is harsher. So managers try to even out the averages, and the larger part, pattern noise, stays untouched.
  • Why noise stays invisible: after a failure we look for a cause in that one case, and bias makes a good story (“the judge was prejudiced”). Noise cannot be seen in a single case, only by comparing many cases. That is what the quote after chapter 12 means.

Improving judgments

First audit, then pick better judges, then adopt hygiene. Five case studies each feature one strategy, and a final protocol combines them.

18Better Judges for Better Judgments

Skill, intelligence, and how you think

The best judges keep looking for reasons they might be wrong.

  • In some fields you can check who is good, because outcomes become known, as with weather forecasters or chess players. In many others you cannot, so we rely on what the authors call respect-experts: people trusted because colleagues respect their training, experience and confident explanations, not because their track record has been tested.
  • What does make better judges: general mental ability predicts job performance, at about .50 for complex jobs, and it keeps making a difference even among the top 1%.
  • And one thinking style: the only measure that predicted good forecasting was actively open-minded thinking, deliberately looking for evidence that you might be wrong and changing your mind when you find it.
19Debiasing and Decision Hygiene

Treatment versus handwashing

Debiasing needs you to know the error in advance. Hygiene doesn't.

  • Debiasing targets a specific bias you already know about. It can correct afterwards, as the UK Treasury's Green Book does by telling planners to add a set margin to cost estimates because projects routinely run over. Or it can act beforehand, through nudges such as automatically enrolling employees in a pension plan, or through training, although training rarely carries over from one field to another.
  • The authors' own proposal is a decision observer: someone the decision makers invite into the meeting to watch, with a checklist, for signs of bias as they happen, for example whether alternatives were considered. They admit the evidence for this is so far informal.
  • Decision hygiene is the other approach, named after handwashing: you wash your hands without knowing which germ you are stopping. Hygiene steps likewise prevent errors you will never identify. The downside is that you never see the error you avoided, so the routine feels thankless. Chapters 20 to 25 each show one hygiene technique.
20Sequencing Information in Forensic Science

Hygiene 1: control what people see, and when

Accurate information can still damage a judgment if it arrives too early.

  • What happened in Madrid: after the 2004 train bombings, which killed 192 people, investigators found a fingerprint on a bag of detonators. The FBI ran it against its database, and a respected senior examiner declared a match with Brandon Mayfield, a lawyer in Oregon. Other examiners then checked the print and agreed, but they already knew what the first expert had concluded, so they were checking with that answer in mind. In fact Mayfield had not left the US in ten years, and Spanish police later identified the real owner of the print. Mayfield was held for two weeks and later received $2 million in compensation. What it shows: the confirmations looked independent but were not. Each examiner leaned on the respected first opinion, a cascade like the ones in chapter 8. Knowing a colleague's conclusion too early steered the checks.
  • How common is this? Psychologist Itiel Dror showed experienced examiners fingerprints they themselves had matched years earlier, without telling them, this time with a story attached, such as “the suspect has a solid alibi”. In one study four of five examiners changed their own earlier conclusion; in another, 4 of 24 decisions changed. Even with no story at all, a 2012 study found that about one decision in ten changed when examiners saw the same prints again seven months later. What it shows: context and mood change expert judgments that feel purely technical.
  • Fingerprint errors are rare: a 2011 FBI study found false matches in roughly one case in six hundred. But that is far more often than juries assume, because most people believe fingerprints never lie.
  • The fix is linear sequential unmasking. Examiners first study the print from the crime scene on its own and write down what they see, and only then look at the suspect's print. They receive case information only when they need it, and a second examiner checks without knowing the first one's conclusion. What it shows: the principle applies anywhere: control what information people see, and when, so that irrelevant facts cannot steer them.

21Selection and Aggregation in Forecasting

Hygiene 2: pick well, then average

Averaging independent judgments is the closest thing to a guaranteed improvement.

  • Forecasters are overconfident. In a long-running survey, the chief financial officers of US companies were asked each quarter to forecast the stock market for the next year and to give a range they were 80% sure would contain the actual return, such as “between 2% and 10%”. If their confidence were justified, the real result would land inside their range about 80% of the time. It did so only 36% of the time. What it shows: their ranges were far too narrow. They were much more certain than their knowledge justified.
  • The most reliable improvement is to average independent judgments. Averaging four people's estimates halves the noise, and nine cut it to a third, because noise shrinks with the square root of the number of judgments. But averaging does nothing against a bias everyone shares, and it only works if people judge independently, not after discussing.
  • Ways to combine views include a select crowd of the best performers, prediction markets where people bet on outcomes, and the Delphi method, in which people estimate privately, discuss, then estimate again. Its one-meeting version is estimate, talk, estimate.
  • In Philip Tetlock's Good Judgment Project, tens of thousands of volunteers gave probabilities for real events (“Will X happen by June?”) and were scored on accuracy and on daring to commit. About 2% were consistently excellent. These superforecasters reportedly beat US intelligence analysts who had access to secret information.
  • Their edge is less raw intelligence than habit: breaking a question into parts, starting from how often such events usually happen (the base rate), and updating often in small steps. Tetlock calls this being in perpetual beta, always a work in progress.
  • Training, teamwork and selecting the best people all improved accuracy. When Ville Satopää and colleagues analysed why, all three worked mainly by reducing noise rather than bias, even the training that had been designed to fight bias.

The square-root law

Four judgments halve the noise, if truly independent.

22Guidelines in Medicine

Hygiene 3: decompose into simple sub-judgments

A good guideline replaces one hard judgment with several easy ones.

  • Doctors disagree more than patients assume. In early studies of angiograms, X-ray images of the heart's arteries, two doctors disagreed 31% of the time on whether a major artery was more than 70% blocked, a key question for treatment. For mammograms, the share of cancers missed ranged from 0% for some radiologists to over 50% for others.
  • A classic remedy is the Apgar score from 1952, still used on newborns worldwide. Instead of one overall judgment (is this baby healthy?), the midwife or doctor rates five things, colour, pulse, reflexes, muscle tone and breathing, each from 0 to 2, and adds them up. A total of 7 or more means the baby is doing well. What it shows: one hard, noisy judgment becomes five easy ones and a sum. Breaking a judgment into parts is a core hygiene technique.
  • Psychiatry shows the limits: in early studies two psychiatrists agreed on a diagnosis only 50 to 57% of the time, and later editions of the diagnostic manual (the DSM) with more precise criteria have improved this only modestly. The chart below shows yet another source of noise: how often doctors order cancer screening falls as the day goes on, probably because they run behind schedule and drop preventive conversations.

23Defining the Scale in Performance Ratings

Hygiene 4: a shared scale, anchored in real cases

Most of what a performance rating measures is the rater.

  • In 360-degree reviews, where bosses, colleagues and staff all rate an employee, the person's actual performance explains only about 20 to 30% of the differences between ratings. The other 70 to 80% is noise, mostly reflecting who the rater is. This holds even when the feedback is only meant for personal development, not for pay.
  • The effort is huge: Deloitte calculated that it spent 2 million hours a year rating its 65,000 people. And ratings often say little: one industrial company rated 98% of its managers as fully meeting expectations.
  • Ranking people against each other is more reliable than rating them on an absolute scale, because a lenient and a tough rater will still put people in a similar order. The flaw in forced ranking (for example, every team must label exactly 20% as top performers) is the forcing: in a random group of ten, the chance that exactly two are top-20% performers is only 30%, so most teams end up with wrong labels.
  • The authors favour frame-of-reference training and case scales: raters are shown concrete example employees as anchors (“this is what a 4 looks like”) and compare each person with them. It works, they say, but has seldom caught on.
24Structure in Hiring

Hygiene 5: break it up and delay the verdict

The unstructured interview is a confident ritual with modest predictive power.

  • Interviews mislead. Interviewers form an impression in the first minutes, down to the handshake, and then ask questions that confirm it. In one experiment some candidates were secretly told to answer at random, and none of the interviewers noticed; they still felt they had learned a lot. What it shows: an unstructured interview feels informative but predicts job performance only modestly.
  • Google found that its interviews did not predict who would do well and rebuilt them on three principles. Decomposition: decide in advance what to assess, for example four defined qualities. Independence: each interviewer asks set behavioural questions (“tell me about a time when…”), scores the answers against a written rubric and files the scores before talking to colleagues. Delayed holistic judgment: only at the end does a committee decide, starting from the average of the scores. Four interviews now predict as well as up to twenty-five did before.
25The Mediating Assessments Protocol

Options are like candidates

Run a strategic decision the way a good structured interview runs.

  • The idea is to treat the options in a big decision like job candidates: define in advance which aspects to assess, have each one assessed separately, and form the overall judgment only at the end. In the book's composite case, CEO Joan uses it to decide on an acquisition. What mostly changes is the meeting agenda: each assessment is discussed on its own before anyone gives an overall view.
  • The analysts' report no longer paints the usual uniformly rosy picture: some aspects score high, others low. What it shows: that unevenness is the benefit. The board sees where the real risks are instead of one confident story.

The protocol, step by step

  1. List the assessmentsShort, complete, non-overlapping, agreed before any analysis.
  2. Use the outside viewBase rates and reference classes; ratings relative to peers.
  3. Keep them apartSeparate analysts; every chapter ends in its own rating, with awkward facts kept in.
  4. One agenda item eachThe board reviews assessments in turn and holds back its overall view.
  5. Estimate, talk, estimatePrivate anonymous rating, discussion of the gaps, then a second rating.
  6. Then decideWith the profile in view, judgment takes over.

Joan doesn't simply average the ratings. People game formulas. In the authors' phrase, “delay intuition, but don't ban it.”

Part VI · Chapters 26–28

Optimal noise

The right amount of noise isn't zero. Still, the authors conclude that nearly every reason to tolerate today's levels is either weak or specific to one crude remedy.

26The Costs of Noise Reduction

When the cure is too blunt

If one remedy is bad, the authors say, look for a better one and don't settle for the noise.

  • Reducing noise has costs, and sometimes they are not worth it: no school will pay five teachers to grade each essay just to average out their differences. But “it's too expensive” is often an excuse.
  • Rules can be consistently wrong. Three-strikes laws, which impose very long sentences after a third conviction, remove noise but produce harsh and sometimes absurd results for minor crimes.
  • Algorithms can be free of noise and still biased, for example if their inputs are linked to race or gender, or if they learned from biased past decisions. The authors accept that risk but argue that algorithms, unlike human minds, can be tested for bias and corrected, and that good ones can be both more accurate and fairer than people. They stress that they can be, not that they will be.
27Dignity

Five more objections, taken seriously

Most of these target rigid rules, and not the milder forms of hygiene.

DignityPeople want an individual hearing. True, the authors say, but it isn't worth more deaths and unfairness. Three judges hearing a case still give one.
Changing valuesRigid rules can freeze old norms. Rules can be revisited, though, and shared scales and aggregation still adapt.
GamingClear rules have edges to slip past, as with tax. The question is how much evasion against how much noise.
DeterrenceA penalty lottery scares the risk-averse and not the risk-seeking. Better, they suggest, to raise the penalty and drop the noise.
Morale and creativityTaking away discretion can feel like becoming a cog. Pick methods like structuring that keep people thinking.
28Rules or Standards?

Who does the thinking, and when

Rules settle the judgment in advance. Standards hand it to whoever applies them.

Rules
  • "The limit is 65 mph"
  • Remove discretion, so reduce noise
  • Costly to write, cheap to apply
  • Suit many repeat decisions and agents you can't fully trust
  • Can drive discretion underground: three-strikes prosecutors quietly avoided felony charges
Standards
  • "Drive prudently"
  • Delegate judgment, so invite noise
  • Cheap to write, costly to apply
  • Suit trusted agents, or groups that can't agree on specifics
  • Need hygiene around them to stay consistent
  • The choice between rules and standards depends on two costs: the cost of making each decision (standards make every decision effortful) and the cost of errors (rules go wrong when they don't fit the case). Facebook, for example, publishes broad community standards but gives its moderators a detailed internal rulebook of about 12,000 words, so that thousands of moderators decide alike.
  • In the US, people with similar disabilities used to get different benefit decisions depending on which official handled the claim. The agency moved to a grid based on age, education, work experience and remaining capacity. Legal scholar Jerry Mashaw called this bureaucratic justice: less individual, but similar people are treated alike.
  • The authors conclude that the law should pay far more attention to noise, and that treating similar people very differently for no reason can sometimes count as a violation of their rights.
Review and conclusion

Taking noise seriously

The closing chapter reduces the book to six principles of hygiene. Judgment is for accuracy, not self-expression. Think statistically. Split the problem. Hold off intuition. Collect independent views. Prefer comparisons.

The epilogue pictures organisations where noise audits are routine and disagreement is open.

“Wherever there is judgment, there is noise, and more of it than you think.”
