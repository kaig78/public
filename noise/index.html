Same case, same facts, different professional, different answer. The authors argue that this scatter does as much harm as bias and gets a fraction of the attention.

The big idea

Two kinds of error, and we only talk about one

Bias is error that leans one way. Noise is error that scatters. The authors are Daniel Kahneman, a psychologist, Olivier Sibony, a strategy professor, and Cass Sunstein, a legal scholar. Bias, they complain, stars in every discussion of judgment while noise goes unmeasured.

Noise here means unwanted variability in judgments that ought to match: two doctors, one patient; two underwriters, one risk. It isn't diversity of taste or competition between ideas. It matters where an organisation treats its professionals as interchangeable and assigns a case to whoever is free.

The book has a surprising practical core. You can measure noise without knowing the right answer, and you can reduce it without knowing which errors you're preventing. They call that second part decision hygiene.

Part I · Chapters 1–3

Finding noise

A courtroom shows noise can be unjust. An insurer shows it can be expensive. A one-off decision shows that seeing no scatter doesn't mean there isn't any.

01Crime and Noisy Punishment

A sentence shouldn't depend on the judge you draw

Discretion once looked humane. Then people measured what it produced.

  • In 1973 Judge Marvin Frankel set out cases like two first offenders passing bad cheques: $58.40 earned one man fifteen years, $35.20 earned the other 30 days.
  • A 1981 study gave 208 federal judges the same sixteen hypothetical cases. On only three did all agree that prison was right.
  • Irrelevant details also move outcomes. Studies link rulings to the local football result, the defendant's birthday and the temperature outside.
  • The Sentencing Reform Act of 1984 produced guidelines with 43 offence levels, and studies found the gap between judges shrank. In 2005 the Supreme Court made them advisory. Judges mostly welcomed this. Crystal Yang's data on nearly 400,000 defendants showed the harsh-judge penalty, 2.8 months before, doubling afterwards.
02A Noisy System

The noise audit

Give many professionals the same file separately, then compare. The result is nearly always a shock.

  • At an insurer, the median gap was 55% for underwriters and 43% for claims adjusters. When one quoted $9,500, the other said $16,700, not the $10,500 people imagined. One executive put the yearly cost in the hundreds of millions.
  • 828 senior executives expected about 10%. Forty-two investors at one firm, valuing the same stock, came in at 41%.
  • Errors don't cancel. A noisy system judges different cases once each, so one overpriced policy and one underpriced policy are two mistakes, not a correct average.
  • The illusion of agreement hides all this. Veterans gain confidence by agreeing with their past selves, and offices avoid contact with disagreement.
03Singular Decisions

One shot still has a scatter

A one-off choice is a repeated decision that happens to be made once.

  • In 2014 President Obama kept borders open during Ebola and sent three thousand people to West Africa. Different advisers might have led elsewhere.
  • You can't measure noise in a decision made once, but its sources are still present, as with a single shot from an unsteady hand. So the methods that help recurrent judgments should help unique ones too.
Part II · Chapters 4–8

Your mind is a measuring instrument

A judgment assigns a value on a scale, and the instrument is a person. Like any instrument it can be off-centre, shaky, or both.

04Matters of Judgment

Between arithmetic and taste

A judgment call is a question where we allow some disagreement, but not much.

  • Asked to rate a fictional CEO candidate, 115 MBA students ranged from 10 to 95 on a 100-point scale. Variability enters at each step: noticing cues, blending them, turning an impression into a number.
  • What ends the task is an internal signal: the answer feels coherent. It isn't evidence of accuracy.
  • Predictive judgments aim at a true value, so disagreement means someone is wrong. Evaluative judgments, like sentences or grades, have no bull's-eye, but scatter still breaks fairness. The authors would grade the process, not the single result.
05Measuring Error

Bias and noise count the same

Overall error is bias squared plus noise squared. A given cut to either buys the same improvement.

  • Gauss's mean squared error, dating from 1795, is the standard yardstick. Squaring makes big misses count far more: going from 11cm off to 10cm is worth 21 times going from 1cm off to a perfect hit.
  • In the fictional firm GoodSell, forecasts of market share averaged 44% with a standard deviation of 10, and the truth turned out to be 34%. Bias and noise are both 10. The boss wants to see the bias before touching noise. The equation says he is wrong: the two are independent.
  • Bias has to be large to match noise. With a normal curve the two tie only when 84% of judgments fall on one side of the truth.
  • The equation covers predictions, not evaluations. Hence the advice to separate facts from values: estimate an elevator's load neutrally, then choose the safety margin.

06The Analysis of Noise

Level noise and pattern noise

Some judges are harsher overall. They also disagree about which cases deserve it.

  • In the 1981 audit, 208 judges times 16 cases gives a 3,328-cell table. Mean sentences ran from 1 year to 15.3. For the average case, the mean was 7.0 years and the standard deviation 3.4: that is the system noise.
  • Level noise, the spread of judges' personal averages, was 2.4 years. It behaves like a personality trait.
  • Pattern noise is the rest: each judge's own reaction to a type of case. Here it was about as large as level noise, and the squares add up again.

What each kind of noise does to the table

07Occasion Noise

You disagree with yourself

Your answer is one draw from a cloud of answers you could have given.

  • Wine experts tasting the same wines blind scored just 18% identically. This second lottery picks your mood and the moment.
  • The crowd within: Vul and Pashler found that averaging two guesses from one person beats either guess. Asked straight away, it's worth a tenth of a second opinion; after three weeks, a third; arguing against your first estimate, about half.
  • Triggers include mood (good moods make people more gullible), fatigue (doctors prescribe more opioids late in the day) and sequence (US asylum judges are 19% less likely to grant after two grants).
  • Reassuringly, you resemble yourself more than you resemble a colleague: occasion noise was the smaller part wherever it could be sized. In a tightly controlled memory study, every measured factor together explained only 11% of a person's variation.
08How Groups Amplify Noise

Discussion can make it worse

Averaging independent views removes noise. Letting people hear each other first can add it back.

  • Salganik's music experiment ran history eight times with 72 unknown songs. When people could see download counts, rankings diverged wildly: beyond the very best and worst, early luck decided.
  • A single artificial up-vote raised a comment's mean rating by 25% after five months.
  • Cascades spread because people reasonably trust those who spoke first, or don't want to look difficult. Polarisation pushes a group past its own starting point.
  • In the authors' jury work, deliberating juries were far noisier than "statistical juries" built by taking the median of six separate opinions. And 27% of juries landed at or above the award of their harshest member.

Part III · Chapters 9–12

Noise in predictive judgments

Predictions can be checked, so they show how much noise costs. The measure used is percent concordant (PC): the chance that the person rated higher really does come out ahead. 50% is a coin toss.

09Judgments and Models

The model of you beats you

Most of what feels like subtlety in expert judgment turns out to be noise.

  • Paul Meehl argued in 1954 that simple formulas generally outdo clinicians at combining information. A 2000 review of 136 studies: formulas won 63, 65 were ties, humans won 8.
  • Lewis Goldberg built simple statistical models of individual judges and found the crude copy usually predicted better than the original. In one replication the model beat its human for all 98 participants. A model strips out your subtleties and your noise, and losing the noise wins.
  • Yu and Kuncel went further with 847 executive candidates, testing 10,000 linear formulas with random weights. These beat the human experts 77% of the time in one sample and 100% in two others.
  • The illusion of validity confuses how good a case looks with how it will turn out.

10Noiseless Rules

From back-of-envelope to machine learning

Rules at both ends of the sophistication scale beat people, and being noise-free is a large part of why.

  • Equal weights. In 1974 Robyn Dawes showed that simply adding standardised predictors did about as well as regression. Regression fits flukes in its sample: in one case .69 shrank to .57 on new data, while equal weights managed .60.
  • Frugal rules. A two-input bail score (age, missed court dates) did better than virtually all human judges at predicting flight.
  • Machine learning. Trained on 758,027 bail decisions, Mullainathan's model could cut crime by up to 24% with the same number jailed, or detention by up to 42% with no more crime. The authors report 41% fewer people of colour jailed at the judges' crime rate, while warning that algorithms can also entrench discrimination.
  • Meehl's broken leg: override a model when you hold decisive information it couldn't consider, and not because you dislike its answer.
11Objective Ignorance

The biggest limit is the world

What can't be known caps every forecast, and gut feeling acts as if it doesn't.

  • Ignorance has two parts: what cannot be known, and what could be known but isn't. Believing you can predict anyway is the denial of ignorance.
  • Philip Tetlock tracked almost 300 experts over two decades. The average roughly matched a chimp throwing darts, and those with the clearest theories were the most confident and the least accurate.
  • Models win, but modestly: across the review's studies, clinicians were right 68% of the time and formulas 73%. A heart-attack model with more than 2,400 variables still found only 30% actual heart attacks in its top risk decile.
  • Why bother, then? Nobody would pick a 59% lottery ticket over a 65% one at the same price. The real price is giving up the warm internal signal.
12The Valley of the Normal

Why everything looks explainable afterwards

Events we could not have predicted still feel understood once they've happened.

  • In the Fragile Families challenge, 160 teams used thousands of data points on almost five thousand children to predict outcomes at fifteen. The best model reached .22 for eviction (PC 57%). Across social psychology a typical correlation is about .21.
  • Memory finds a cause and stops, so the result feels inevitable. The opposite outcome would have had an equally good story.
  • Causal thinking is easy and tells stories about cases. Statistical thinking, the outside view, takes effort and sees cases as members of a class. Noise is visible only to the second.
“Causally, noise is nowhere; statistically, it is everywhere.”
Kahneman, Sibony and Sunstein, ch. 17
Part IV · Chapters 13–17

How noise happens

The machinery of fast thinking produces shared error when everyone trips the same way, and noise when each person trips differently.

13Heuristics, Biases, and Noise

Three families of bias

Psychological bias becomes statistical bias when shared, and noise when it differs from person to person.

  • Substitution: we answer an easier question. Asked how probable something is, we judge how similar it seems, and ignore base rates. With CEO turnover near 15% a year, about 72% of new CEOs last two years, a number few readers think to seek.
  • Conclusion bias: we start from what we want. Anchoring belongs here too.
  • Excessive coherence: first impressions stick. The same four adjectives about a candidate read very differently depending on which comes first.
14The Matching Operation

Too extreme, and too coarse

We translate intensities across scales effortlessly, and the translation ignores how weak the link is.

  • Told only that Julie read fluently at four, people guess a college GPA of 3.7 or 3.8. They've matched one percentile to another as though reading age predicted grades perfectly.
  • Matching predictions are nonregressive: they forget that extremes drift toward the mean, as with the hit director's next film.
  • People reliably label only about seven categories on a scale. Comparing cases side by side discriminates far better, though it costs more effort.

Correcting a matching prediction

Start from the outside-view average, then move toward your intuition only in proportion to the evidence's real correlation with the outcome. The authors put reading age at .20 at most, so you travel a fifth of the way.

15Scales

Jurors agree on outrage and not on dollars

Much disagreement lies in the ruler, not in what people think.

  • In Kahneman, Sunstein and Schkade's study, 899 people rated injury cases on outrage, punitive intent or dollars. Mean outrage and punitive intent correlated at .98. People swapped "how much punishment?" for "how angry am I?"
  • A dollar scale has no top and no natural reference, so the first number you pick is arbitrary, and later numbers keep in proportion to it. Convert awards to ranks and most of the disagreement disappears.
  • US law bars telling juries what other cases got. The authors argue that this assumes an ability people lack, and that an agreed anchor case would tie the scale down.

16Patterns

Your judgment personality

Pattern noise isn't random. It's as personal and persistent as character.

  • Give Julie a messier life story and people pick different cues, so disagreement grows.
  • Pattern error splits into a stable part and a passing part (occasion noise). The stable part comes from your history: a case reminds you of a relative, and would again next week.
  • Judges, like personalities, have signature reactions. That is delightful in a friend and an error in a professional.
17The Sources of Noise

The biggest part is the one they missed

The authors began by thinking of residual noise as random. It turned out to be mostly stable and personal.

  • Bail models of 173 judges applied to 141,833 cases showed stable pattern noise at 26% of total variance against 7% for level noise.
  • This matters because level noise is the only kind organisations can sometimes monitor without an audit. Equalising average severity leaves most of the problem in place.
  • Noise escapes notice because bias makes a good story after a failure, and noise can't appear in a story about one case.

Part V · Chapters 18–25

Improving judgments

First audit, then pick better judges, then adopt hygiene. Five case studies each feature one strategy, and a final protocol combines them.

18Better Judges for Better Judgments

Skill, intelligence, and how you think

The best judges keep looking for reasons they might be wrong.

  • Where outcomes can be checked, you can identify true experts. Elsewhere you have respect-experts, trusted because peers respect their training, experience and confident stories.
  • General mental ability predicts performance, at about .50 for complex jobs, and keeps mattering even inside the top 1%.
  • The one style measure found to predict forecasting was actively open-minded thinking: seeking out evidence against your own view.
19Debiasing and Decision Hygiene

Treatment versus handwashing

Debiasing needs you to know the error in advance. Hygiene doesn't.

  • Debiasing can come after the fact (the UK Treasury's Green Book adjusts for optimism) or before it, through nudges like pension auto-enrolment or through training. Training is hard to carry across fields.
  • So the authors propose a decision observer, invited by the decision makers, who uses a checklist to spot signs of bias in real time. They say the evidence so far is informal.
  • Decision hygiene works like handwashing. You prevent errors you'll never identify, so the win is invisible and the routine is thankless.
20Sequencing Information in Forensic Science

Hygiene 1: control what people see, and when

Accurate information can still damage a judgment if it arrives too early.

  • After the 2004 Madrid train bombings, which killed 192 people, FBI examiners matched a print to Oregon lawyer Brandon Mayfield. He had not left the US in a decade. He was held for two weeks and later paid $2 million. A respected first examiner set off a cascade.
  • Itiel Dror re-showed examiners their own past prints with stories attached. Four of five changed an identification in one study. In another, 4 of 24 decisions moved. With no story at all, a 2012 study still found about one decision in ten altered after seven months.
  • A 2011 FBI study put false positives near one in six hundred. That is low, and much higher than jurors would likely believe.
  • The fix: linear sequential unmasking. Reveal only what's needed, document each step before seeing the next, and keep verifiers unaware of the first conclusion.

21Selection and Aggregation in Forecasting

Hygiene 2: pick well, then average

Averaging independent judgments is the closest thing to a guaranteed improvement.

  • Forecasters are overconfident: returns landed inside US finance chiefs' 80% ranges only 36% of the time.
  • Averaging divides noise by the square root of the number of judgments. It does nothing for shared bias, which is why independence matters.
  • Options include a select crowd of top performers, prediction markets, and the Delphi method with its one-meeting version: estimate, talk, estimate.
  • In the Good Judgment Project, tens of thousands of volunteers gave probabilities, scored on calibration and on taking a stand. About 2% stood out as superforecasters, reportedly beating intelligence analysts who could read secret data.
  • Their edge is less raw intelligence than habit: breaking questions into parts, starting from base rates, updating relentlessly. Tetlock calls this perpetual beta.
  • Training, teaming and selection all improved accuracy. Satopää and colleagues found that all three worked mainly by cutting noise, even the training built to fight bias.

The square-root law

Four judgments halve the noise, if truly independent.

22Guidelines in Medicine

Hygiene 3: decompose into simple sub-judgments

A good guideline replaces one hard judgment with several easy ones.

  • Early angiogram readers disagreed 31% of the time on whether a major vessel was more than 70% blocked. Mammogram false negatives ranged from 0% to over 50% across radiologists in one study.
  • Virginia Apgar's 1952 newborn score rates colour, pulse, reflexes, muscle tone and breathing from 0 to 2 each. Since a total of 7 or more signals good health, the judgment is now five small steps and a sum. Other scores work similarly.
  • Psychiatry is the hard case. Early studies found two psychiatrists agreeing just 50–57% of the time, and DSM revisions have helped only modestly.

23Defining the Scale in Performance Ratings

Hygiene 4: a shared scale, anchored in real cases

Most of what a performance rating measures is the rater.

  • In 360-degree reviews, true performance usually explains 20–30% of variance. The rest, 70–80%, is noise. It stays high even for purely developmental feedback.
  • Deloitte counted 2 million hours a year on its 65,000 people. One industrial firm rated 98% of managers as fully meeting expectations.
  • Rankings beat ratings, because lenient and tough raters use the same ranks. The flaw in forced ranking is the forcing: in a random ten, the chance of exactly two top-20% performers is just 30%.
  • The authors favour frame-of-reference training and case scales: raters compare each person with shared anchor examples. It works, and has seldom caught on.
24Structure in Hiring

Hygiene 5: break it up and delay the verdict

The unstructured interview is a confident ritual with modest predictive power.

  • First minutes drive outcomes, down to the handshake, and interviewers then steer toward confirmation. In one experiment candidates answered at random and no interviewer noticed.
  • Google found its interviews predicted nothing, then rebuilt them on three principles. Decomposition: four defined assessments. Independence: behavioural questions scored against a rubric, with ratings filed before colleagues talk. Delayed holistic judgment: a committee decides at the end, anchored on the average. Four interviews replaced as many as twenty-five.
25The Mediating Assessments Protocol

Options are like candidates

Run a strategic decision the way a good structured interview runs.

  • In the book's composite case, a CEO, Joan, applies this to a possible acquisition. It mostly changes the agenda.
  • The analysts' report comes back without the usual rosy unity, as some ratings are strong and others aren't. That unevenness is the benefit.

The protocol, step by step

  1. List the assessmentsShort, complete, non-overlapping, agreed before any analysis.
  2. Use the outside viewBase rates and reference classes; ratings relative to peers.
  3. Keep them apartSeparate analysts; every chapter ends in its own rating, with awkward facts kept in.
  4. One agenda item eachThe board reviews assessments in turn and holds back its overall view.
  5. Estimate, talk, estimatePrivate anonymous rating, discussion of the gaps, then a second rating.
  6. Then decideWith the profile in view, judgment takes over.

Joan doesn't simply average the ratings. People game formulas. In the authors' phrase, “delay intuition, but don't ban it.”

Part VI · Chapters 26–28

Optimal noise

The right amount of noise isn't zero. Still, the authors conclude that nearly every reason to tolerate today's levels is either weak or specific to one crude remedy.

26The Costs of Noise Reduction

When the cure is too blunt

If one remedy is bad, the authors say, look for a better one and don't settle for the noise.

  • Cost is sometimes a fair objection: nobody needs five teachers grading each essay. It's often an excuse too.
  • Rules can be consistently wrong, as with three-strikes laws.
  • Algorithms can be noiseless and biased, through predictors tied to race or gender or skewed training data. The authors accept the risk and argue that algorithms can be tested in ways human minds can't, and that good ones can beat people on both accuracy and fairness. They stress "can", not "will".
27Dignity

Five more objections, taken seriously

Most of these target rigid rules, and not the milder forms of hygiene.

DignityPeople want an individual hearing. True, the authors say, but it isn't worth more deaths and unfairness. Three judges hearing a case still give one.
Changing valuesRigid rules can freeze old norms. Rules can be revisited, though, and shared scales and aggregation still adapt.
GamingClear rules have edges to slip past, as with tax. The question is how much evasion against how much noise.
DeterrenceA penalty lottery scares the risk-averse and not the risk-seeking. Better, they suggest, to raise the penalty and drop the noise.
Morale and creativityTaking away discretion can feel like becoming a cog. Pick methods like structuring that keep people thinking.
28Rules or Standards?

Who does the thinking, and when

Rules settle the judgment in advance. Standards hand it to whoever applies them.

Rules
  • "The limit is 65 mph"
  • Remove discretion, so reduce noise
  • Costly to write, cheap to apply
  • Suit many repeat decisions and agents you can't fully trust
  • Can drive discretion underground: three-strikes prosecutors quietly avoided felony charges
Standards
  • "Drive prudently"
  • Delegate judgment, so invite noise
  • Cheap to write, costly to apply
  • Suit trusted agents, or groups that can't agree on specifics
  • Need hygiene around them to stay consistent
  • The choice comes down to the cost of decisions and the cost of errors. Facebook backed its public standards with a private rulebook of about 12,000 words.
  • US disability benefits moved to a matrix after look-alike claimants got different results. Jerry Mashaw named this bureaucratic justice.
  • The authors argue that the law should do far more about noise, and that it can sometimes count as a rights violation.
Review and conclusion

Taking noise seriously

The closing chapter reduces the book to six principles of hygiene. Judgment is for accuracy, not self-expression. Think statistically. Split the problem. Hold off intuition. Collect independent views. Prefer comparisons.

The epilogue pictures organisations where noise audits are routine and disagreement is open.

“Wherever there is judgment, there is noise, and more of it than you think.”
Kahneman, Sibony and Sunstein, ch. 2
