Preface & introduction

One restless traveller changed the world

Culture normally moves like a glacier, Wright says, which is why Paul is so startling. He went from city to city talking about the One God and his son Jesus, setting up small communities and writing them letters that are still explosive.

After decades of scholarship on Paul, Wright asks a biographer's question: what made him tick? He works with all the evidence, including Acts and the disputed letters, and tries to fit the pieces together rather than ruling some out in advance.

His answer starts with zeal. Paul never lost it. What changed was what it was for.

The biography

From persecutor to apostle

01Zeal

The boy who wanted to be Phinehas

Saul of Tarsus grew up on stories of zeal for the One God, and he believed that violence in God's cause could be righteous.

  • A Torah prodigy. Wright pictures a boy steeped in Israel's scriptures, in a tradition of resisting pagan pressure and of heroes like Phinehas, Elijah and the Maccabees.
  • Why he persecuted. The Jesus movement seemed to attack Torah and Temple, and so to risk God's anger. Saul set off with documents from the chief priests, hoping to be counted as a true covenant member.
02Damascus

The vision, and the years nobody sees

Paul didn't leave Judaism. He came to believe that Israel's God had acted in the crucified and risen Jesus.

  • Merkabah mysticism. Wright reads the Damascus road against Jewish meditation on the throne in Ezekiel: Saul looked for the figure on the throne and saw Jesus.
  • Arabia and Tarsus. After Damascus, Paul went to Arabia, Wright suggests in the manner of Elijah going to Sinai. Then came about a decade in Tarsus, thinking through what it meant. The zeal stayed, but its shape changed.
  • Temple and Torah. Both now pointed beyond themselves to Jesus and the spirit, and the One God was the God of the whole world.
03Antioch

One table for Jews and Gentiles

The great fight of Paul's life was about whether the Messiah's family had one table or two.

  • Barnabas. The "son of encouragement" fetched Saul from Tarsus to Antioch, where the followers were first called Christianoi.
  • Cyprus and Galatia. The first journey, around 47–48, brought in Gentiles and met violent resistance.
  • Confronting Peter. When Peter stopped eating with Gentiles, Paul opposed him to his face. If righteousness came through the law, the Messiah died for nothing. Galatians, which Wright dates to about 48, comes out of this crisis.
  • A painful split. After the Jerusalem conference Paul and Barnabas fell out over John Mark, a blow to the apostle of reconciliation.
04Europe

Into the Greek world

Paul took a Jewish message into cities built around other gods, and each one responded differently.

  • Philippi and Thessalonica. The charge was political: a rival king to Caesar. Paul's love for these communities shows in his letters.
  • Athens. Before the Areopagus, under the Parthenon, Paul gave a speech that turned the trial into an argument about the true God.
  • Corinth. He stayed eighteen months, from about 51 to 52, in a lively, class-divided city. On the "meeting in the air", Wright says the picture is of citizens going out to welcome a visiting ruler. Heaven and earth come together, and nobody is snatched away.
05Ephesus

The darkest years

Paul's most despairing moments may have produced some of his finest writing.

  • Prison in Ephesus. Wright argues for an imprisonment around 55–56 that nearly broke him, and dates Philippians, Philemon, Colossians and Ephesians to it, against the fashion of doubting the last two.
  • The new Shema. In 1 Corinthians Paul reworks Israel's central prayer around Jesus: one God, one Lord.
  • Prayer as therapy. Wright speaks of a breakdown. Paul's long habit of Jesus-focused prayer through the scriptures helped him find his way from despair back to hope.
06Romans

The masterpiece

Dictated in Corinth to Tertius around 57, Romans draws Paul's whole thought together.

  • Corinth healed. After the battered letter of 2 Corinthians, he returned to the city.
  • Written for unity. Paul foresaw the danger of Gentile Christians despising Jews, and Wright notes that Marcion later did just that.
  • The collection. Money from Gentile churches for Jerusalem expressed two passions: remember the poor, and one family in the Messiah.
07Rome

Prisoner in charge

The last journey shows a man who is weak and strong at once.

  • Jerusalem. The collection trip ended in a riot, arrest and two years in Caesarea. Paul appealed to Caesar.
  • Shipwreck. He is a prisoner, and yet he more or less takes charge of the voyage to Rome.
  • The end. Acts stops with him under house arrest. Wright raises a journey to Spain as a possibility and imagines his death around or after 64, in the persecution under Nero, praying the Shema.
Chapter 15

The challenge of Paul

Why did he keep going back to synagogues that kept beating him? Why did he run himself into the ground? Wright's answer is a new kind of community, one that crossed the lines of ethnicity, class and gender, held together by loyalty (pistis) to Jesus.

Paul's success came from those small communities, not from a system of ideas alone. They offered a new way of being human in the Roman world, and the church's survival depended on them.

The book ends with Wright imagining Paul at his execution, praying the Shema he had reshaped: one God, one Lord, loved now with his life.
