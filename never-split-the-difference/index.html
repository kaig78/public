A former Kansas City beat cop who became the FBI's lead international kidnapping negotiator. At a Harvard course he took the Harvard negotiation professors apart in a role-play by asking "How am I supposed to do that?" over and over. His argument: people aren't rational problem-solvers, and the Getting-to-Yes school underrates emotion. The FBI's answer was tactical empathy: listening as a martial art. Each chapter opens with a real kidnapping or siege and ends with the same tools applied to salaries, car prices and children's bedtimes.

Ch. 1 · The new rules

Life is negotiation

After the sieges at Ruby Ridge and Waco, two FBI veterans asked a room of 35 police negotiators how many had ever faced a classic, rational bargaining situation. No hands. How many had dealt with someone in emotional crisis with no clear demands? Every hand. Kahneman had shown the same thing: System 1 (fast, emotional) drives System 2 (slow, logical). So the FBI rebuilt crisis negotiation from counselling techniques.

Listening is the cheapest, yet most effective concession we can make.

Negotiation, for Voss, is simply "communication with results": every "I want" between two people, from a $1 million contract to "I want you in bed by nine." Ch. 2 · Be a mirror

Slow. It. Down.

His first real hostage case was a 1993 bank robbery in Brooklyn. The robber claimed to be one of many, scared of his volatile partners; in fact he was the ringleader, and his partner had been tricked into a robbery he thought was an ATM job. Lesson one: assumptions blind, hypotheses guide. Keep several hypotheses alive and use every new detail to test them. (Watch pronouns: the less important someone makes himself, the more important he probably is.)

Quiet the voices

Most people argue with the voice in their own head while the other person does the same: four people talking at once. Treat both with one pill: make the other person your only focus. If someone is talking, they're not shooting.

Three voices

Late-night FM DJ: deep, slow, downward inflection, for when something isn't up for discussion ("We don't do work-for-hire."). Positive/playful: your default, smile even on the phone. Direct/assertive: almost never; it's like slapping yourself.

Mirroring

Repeat the last one to three words, with an upward, curious tone, then stay silent. People elaborate. Waiters who simply repeated orders got 70% more in tips than those who praised ("Great choice!"). A student's impulsive boss demanded two paper copies of thousands of documents:

  • Boss"Let's make two copies of all the paperwork."
  • Her"I'm sorry, two copies?"
  • Boss"Yes, one for us and one for the customer."
  • Her"I'm sorry, so you're saying the client is asking for a copy and we need one in-house?"
  • Boss"Actually, I'll check with the client… I definitely want a copy though."
  • Her"Where would you like it stored? There's no room left in the file room."
  • Boss"It's fine. You can store it anywhere."
  • Her"Anywhere?"

A long pause, and then two digital back-ups would do. A week of work saved. The recipe: DJ voice, "I'm sorry…", mirror, at least four seconds of silence, repeat.

Ch. 3 · Don't feel their pain, label it

Tactical empathy

Three armed fugitives in a Harlem apartment, no phone, six hours of Voss's DJ voice through the door: "It looks like you don't want to come out. It seems like you worry that if you open the door, we'll come in with guns blazing. It looks like you don't want to go back to jail." They came out: "You calmed us down." Empathy is recognising the other's perspective and saying it out loud. It isn't agreement or sympathy.

Labelling

Spot the emotion (words, music, dance), then name it with "It seems like… / It sounds like… / It looks like…", never "I'm hearing that…" (the "I" puts people on guard). Then stop talking. Naming fear moves brain activity away from the amygdala, and people almost never object; if a label misses, "I didn't say it was, I said it seems like it."

Go after the negative

Labelling negatives defuses them; labelling positives reinforces them. Behind a grumpy grandfather at Christmas is loneliness: "We don't see each other often. It seems like you feel we don't pay you any attention, so why should you make time for us?" Pause. "For us this is a real treat." Voss's own apology after a blunder: "Look, I'm an asshole."

Clear the road first

A fund-raiser's donor rejected every project. "I'm sensing some hesitation." Out came: she wanted the money to go straight to the girls. Then: "It seems you're really passionate about this, and want a project that reflects what Girl Scouts gave you." She wrote the cheque without picking a project: "You understand me."

The accusation audit

List every terrible thing they could say about you, and say it first. Denying a negative ("I don't want this to sound harsh, but…") only gives it weight; naming it takes the sting out. Anna had to cut a small partner firm from 5.5 paid staff to 3. She opened: "We understand we brought you on board with the shared goal of having you lead this work. You may feel we've treated you unfairly and changed the deal. We acknowledge you believe you were promised this work." Later: "It sounds like you think we're the big, bad prime contractor pushing out the small business." "No, no, we don't think that." The cut went through, worth $1 million, and the other side thanked her for acknowledging it.

All at once, in two minutes

Ryan, stranded in Dallas, followed a screaming couple to the gate agent. "Hi, Wendy, I'm Ryan. It seems like they were pretty upset." (label) "The weather?" (mirror) "It seems like it's been a hectic day." "The big game?" "Booked solid?" Then, only then: "It seems like you've handled the rough day pretty well… maybe someone affected by the weather will miss this connection. Is there any possibility a seat will open?" Thirty seconds of silence. A boarding pass, in Economy Plus.

Ch. 4 · Beware "yes", master "no"

"No" starts the negotiation

The telemarketer's "Do you enjoy a nice glass of water?" makes you want to shout "No!" Being pushed for yes makes people defensive. There are three yeses: counterfeit (to get rid of you), confirmation (reflexive) and commitment (the real one). "No" gives people a feeling of safety and control; it often just means "not yet," "I don't understand," or "I need more information."

Ask for no

"Is now a bad time to talk?" beats "Do you have a few minutes?" A colleague about to be demoted by a jealous boss asked: "Do you want the FBI to be embarrassed?" "No." "What do you want me to do?" She kept her job. A political fund-raising script built on "no" questions ("Do you think America's best days are ahead if nothing changes?") raised 23% more than the "yes ladder."

The email nobody ignores

When a contact goes silent: "Have you given up on this project?" It plays on loss aversion and implies you might walk away, and the urge is to reply "No, we've just been busy…". Like a parent saying "Fine, I'm leaving" at the park.

At a suicide hotline, Voss once logically talked a regular caller out of his fears and got "Thanks for doing such a great job." His supervisor: "That was one of the worst calls I've ever heard. They should be congratulating themselves." The caller's yes was counterfeit; people only act on conclusions they believe are their own.

Ch. 5 · "That's right"

The two sweetest words

Abu Sabaya, holding the American Jeffrey Schilling in the Philippines, demanded $10 million in "war damages" for 500 years of oppression. Logic got nowhere. So the Filipino negotiator stopped arguing and summarised Sabaya's whole world: the history, the grievances, the anger. Nearly a minute of silence. Then: "That's right." The money demand vanished. A summary is paraphrase + label, and its goal is a "that's right": agreement without the feeling of having given in.

"You're right" is the worst answer

It means "please stop talking." Voss's son kept smashing into blockers as a linebacker and kept saying "you're right." Then: "You seem to think it's unmanly to dodge a block. You think it's cowardly to get out of someone's way." Pause. "That's right." He changed.

In business

A pharma rep stopped pitching a cold doctor and said: "Last time we spoke, you seemed very passionate about tailoring treatment to each patient." "That's right." He opened up and tried her product. A Korean student uncovered that his ex-boss needed an ally at headquarters: "So you could approve my move to any division, as long as I'm at HQ?" "That's right."
Ch. 6 · Bend their reality

Don't compromise

A wife wants black shoes with his suit, he wants brown, and they compromise on one of each. Splitting the difference is often the worst outcome; we compromise because it's easy and saves face. No deal is better than a bad deal. Haitian kidnappers demanding $150,000 turned out to want party money by Friday; the family paid $4,751 and a CD player.

Deadlines and "fair"

Deadlines are mostly arbitrary, and disclosing yours speeds up a deal. "Fair" is the most powerful word. Answer "We just want what's fair" with "Okay, I apologise. Let's go back to where I started treating you unfairly and fix it." Answer "We've made a fair offer" with a mirror ("Fair?") and "It seems you're ready to show the evidence." Use it positively: "Stop me any time you feel I'm being unfair."

Anchors and loss

People take more risk to avoid a loss than to win a gain. Voss had to cut contractors from $2,000 to $500 a day. He opened: "I've got a lousy proposition. By the end of this call you'll think I can't budget and might even have lied to you… Still, I wanted to bring this opportunity to you before I take it to someone else." All accepted. Let the other side name a price first, cite a range ("people in this role get $130–170k"), pivot to non-money terms, and use odd numbers ($37,263 looks calculated).

On salary: be pleasantly persistent about non-salary terms, define what success looks like and the metrics for your next raise, and ask "What does it take to be successful here?" Whoever answers becomes your unofficial mentor.

Ch. 7 · Create the illusion of control

Calibrated questions

After the failed Dos Palmas case, a Pittsburgh drug dealer whose girlfriend had been kidnapped blurted: "Hey, dog, how do I know she's all right?" Ten seconds of silence. "Well, I'll put her on the phone." An open question made the kidnapper solve the problem as if it were his own idea. Start with "what" or "how", rarely "why" ("why" sounds like an accusation in every language).

OpenersWhat is the biggest challenge you face? What about this is important to you? What are we trying to accomplish here?
SolversHow can we solve this problem? How would you like me to proceed? What is it that brought us into this situation?
The gentle noHow am I supposed to do that? (Deferential, a real request for help.)

"You can't leave" becomes "What do you hope to achieve by going?": a senior doctor kept a patient from walking out that way. "Why did you do it?" becomes "What caused you to do it?" None of it works without self-control: a consultant had a perfect script to recover an unpaid invoice from a condescending CEO, lost her temper in the first minute, and didn't even get half.

Ch. 8 · Guarantee execution

"Yes" is nothing without "how"

A prison surrender almost fell apart because one officer confiscated the walkie-talkie the first inmate was meant to use to say "they didn't beat me." An agreement only counts once it's carried out. Ask "How will we know we're on track? What will we do if we're off track?" and be wary of "You're right" and "I'll try" (= "I plan to fail"). Find out who's behind the table: "How does this affect the rest of your team?"

Read beneath the words

Words carry 7% of a message, tone 38%, body 55% (Mehrabian). When they clash: "I heard you say yes, but it seemed like there was hesitation in your voice." Get agreement three times in different forms. Liars use more words and more third-person pronouns. And say your own name: "My name is Chris. What's the Chris discount?" (10% off).

Saying no four times without "no"

  1. "How am I supposed to do that?"
  2. "Your offer is very generous; I'm sorry, that just doesn't work for me."
  3. "I'm sorry, but I'm afraid I just can't do that."
  4. "I'm sorry, no."
  5. "No." (Gently, downward.)
Ch. 9 · Bargain hard

Know the type, then use Ackerman

Voss wanted a Salsa Red Pearl 4Runner priced at $36,000. "I can pay $30,000, cash, today. I'm sorry, I just can't pay more." "How am I supposed to do that?" "Your offer is very generous… I simply can't." $34,000, $32,500 with a "YOU WIN" sign, then $30,000.

AnalystMethodical, hates surprises, silence means thinking. Bring data. If you are one: smile when you speak.
AccommodatorRelationship first, loves win-win, hides objections. Silence means anger. Turn talk into action.
AssertiveTime is money, wants to be heard first, silence means "your turn to talk." Mirror, label, summarise. If you are one: soften your tone.

The golden rule is wrong here: treat others the way they need to be treated. When hit with an extreme anchor, deflect ("What are we trying to accomplish?"), pivot ("Let's put price aside: what would make this a good deal?"), and never treat the other person as the enemy. The problem is the issue, not the person.

The Ackerman plan

Between each step: empathy, calibrated questions and the different ways of saying no, so they move before you do. The final, oddly precise number comes with a small non-monetary extra to signal you're at your limit. A student renegotiated his rent this way and settled at $1,829 instead of the proposed $2,000.

Ch. 10 · Find the Black Swan

The unknown unknowns

In 1981 a gunman in Rochester took bank hostages with no demands; no hostage-taker in US history had ever killed on a deadline. He did, then stepped in front of a sniper's window. Every clue that he wanted to die had been there. Voss's hypothesis since: in every negotiation each side holds at least three Black Swans, pieces of information that would change everything if known.

  • Leverage comes in three kinds: positive (what they want from you), negative (what they could lose; label it rather than threaten) and normative (their own standards).
  • Know their "religion." A tobacco farmer threatened to blow up his tractor on the National Mall. A colleague noticed he was devout and a veteran: "Tomorrow is the dawn of the third day." He came out.
  • Use similarity and hopes. To a Christian CEO who felt misunderstood: "This is really stewardship for you, isn't it?" "Yes! You're the only one who understands."
  • It's not crazy, it's a clue. "Crazy" counterparts are usually ill-informed, constrained, or following interests you can't see. A Coca-Cola contact went silent because he'd lost his influence and was embarrassed; a dinner "where we don't talk business" brought it out.
  • Get face time, and watch the unguarded moments at the start and end of meetings.

Every conversation is a series of small conflicts that, managed well, can rise to creative beauty.

His closing plea is to stop fearing conflict: couples who never fight still divorce. The person opposite is a partner; the situation is the adversary.
