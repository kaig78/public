The Tearsof Things

Prophetic wisdom for an age of outrage

A Franciscan in his eighties rereads the Hebrew prophets, not as angry doom-sayers but as people who began in rage, learned to weep, and ended in love for everyone, and invites us to take the same path.

The big idea

Anger is where prophets start, not where they end

Picture an Old Testament prophet and you probably see a wild-haired man ranting about sin and doom. Rohr thinks that image misses the most important thing. Yes, the prophets called Israel back to the covenant and named injustice. But the mature ones didn't stay angry.

His thesis: prophets begin with legitimate, righteous anger, often sharply us-versus-them. Then, through confusion, doubt, love and above all moments of revelation, they are changed. They move into lament, grieving with and for the people they were attacking. And they end in praise and a love that refuses to exclude anyone.

Israel invented something unusual: an officially licensed critic inside its own religion, a "watchman" whose job was to expose the group's own shadow. Most cultures only criticise the other side.

Rohr writes for a time of outrage, when anger is everywhere and often justified. His point isn't to suppress it. It's that anger alone can't heal anything. Only tears move us beyond paralysing anger without denying that it was right.

The prophetic path

Rohr sees this movement in Amos, Jeremiah, Isaiah and Ezekiel. Some prophets, in the text we have at least, never leave the first stage. He thinks the pattern applies to anyone who cares about justice today.

Part one · Chapters 1–4

There are tears at the heart of things

The title comes from Virgil. In the Aeneid, the hero looks at a mural of the Trojan War, sees his dead friends, and speaks of lacrimae rerum. The Latin has no preposition, so it means both: tears in things and tears for things.

1The Tears of Things

It's hard to attack while you're weeping

Almost every situation carries some tragedy. Tears are the opposite of our usual tools: willpower, force, retribution.

  • Prophets and mystics see what most of us miss: everything has tears, and everything deserves tears. Grief becomes a doorway to seeing life without the ego in the centre.
  • Tears come from awe and from empathy, and they produce more of both. They pull us out of ourselves and into communion.
  • A test for whether it's God speaking or just me: the fruits of the Spirit. Without love, joy, peace, patience and kindness, it's my voice.
  • Jesus blesses those who mourn. We can't order tears, only allow them and let them soften us. They move us forward not by shame, but by sadness and empathy.
  • Rohr remembers a stooped Latin teacher who walked into class sighing "lacrimae rerum" before every quiz. It would have been funny if it hadn't been so sad.
2Amos

A shepherd's "bias toward the bottom"

The people at the top assume things are as they should be. The poor know in their bones that they aren't.

  • Amos was a herdsman and fig-tree pruner from Tekoa, not a professional prophet. He spoke during Israel's greatest prosperity under Jeroboam II, when success was read as God's approval.
  • He attacked complacency, military reliance, unjust business and shallow religion, including the wealthy women he called "cows of Bashan" for living off the poor.
  • Expelled from the royal sanctuary, he went home and wrote it down, and set the pattern for the writing prophets after him.
  • He ends with the promise to rebuild David's fallen hut. Rohr calls it a revolution: from punishment to restorative justice, the pattern Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel and Jesus will follow.
3A Critical Mass

God works from the edges

The whole group never gets the message. A small remnant carries it forward, and that seems to be God's method.

  • Hebrew slaves against an empire, David against Goliath, a messiah from the smallest clan of Judah: the biblical theme of themes is that the victims become the victors.
  • Power distorts truth, so God plants it at the margins, where the powerful least expect it. He is patient and humble, content if a few get the point in each era.
  • Jesus' promise to be present where two or three gather assumes this remnant theology.
  • Rohr suggests that seeking truth at the edges, rather than at the centres of power, could have spared history many religious wars. Today's prophets will never be mainstream; they care about the message, not their reputation.
4Welcoming Holy Disorder

Order, disorder, reorder

Nothing goes straight from the old order to a better one. The old has to show its cracks first, and that in-between time needs trust.

How reform actually happens

Rohr's example is his own church: the Second Vatican Council in the 1960s opened the windows when everything seemed fine, and the disorder of implementing it still continues. Healthy religion teaches us to "die early", so we can keep moving through change.

  • Reformers from outside are easy to dismiss. Pope Leo X refused to hear any truth in Luther's theses, and lost the chance. Communities need trained, blessed critics on the inside.
Part two · Chapters 5–9

Five prophets, one path

Rohr walks through the major prophets to show the same movement in each: from anger, through lament, toward praise and grace.

5Jeremiah

Always out ahead of his people

Jeremiah warned of the exile before it came, then told the people to trust it once it did.

  • Called in 626 BC, he condemned idol worship and predicted famine and conquest. He was imprisoned, then thrown into a cistern to starve.
  • He was rescued by Ebed-melech, an Ethiopian eunuch at the palace: another sign that God's people are often saved by those on the margins.
  • He moved from anger to lament and then, with no evidence yet, into praise: Jerusalem would become his joy.
  • Rohr links this to the "second half of life": great love and great suffering undo people and remake them, and fear gives way to love. The prophet's anguish, he writes, is our refusal to let ourselves be loved for nothing.
6Unfinished Prophets

Elijah, Jonah and John the Baptist

Some prophets, as far as the text shows, stay in anger and blame. God still uses them.

  • Elijah is dramatic and violent; Jonah resents God's mercy to Nineveh; John the Baptist preaches purity and a baptism of water. Jesus brings fire and Spirit: inner transformation, not just outer cleansing. That's why the least in the kingdom is greater than John.
  • Rohr suspects they grew too; we just aren't shown it. There's mercy in this: you can act as a prophet sometimes without being a finished one.
  • He reads Job as the consummate prophet: through suffering he comes to trust that God loves us because we are God's, not because we are right.
7The Alchemy of Tears

The gift of tears and the gift of healing

After fifty-four years in ministry, Rohr thinks they are almost the same gift.

  • Lamentations is the only biblical book named after an emotion. It names almost no specific events: it's sadness at the human condition itself, an invitation to universal solidarity.
  • Early biographies show Saint Francis crying all the time. Rohr now thinks he healed people because he could weep over them.
  • In Jeremiah, rage at sin turns into pity for suffering and woundedness, with a promise of healing still held out. What is truly felt is wept, and what is wept becomes compassion.
  • Grace isn't produced by morality or piety. Like tears, it arrives uninvited. But we still have to cooperate, as an Irish nun told Rohr's seventh-grade class almost every day.
8The Three Isaiahs

The whole path in one book

Isaiah, written over generations, shows the movement from anger to lament to praise more clearly than any other book.

Three authors, three moments

I IsaiahChapters 1–39, from around 742 BC, under the Assyrian threat. Anger at injustice, then lament, even for Israel's enemies like Moab.
II IsaiahChapters 40–55, written in exile in Babylon, yet already trusting rescue: can a mother forget her baby? I will not forget you. The servant songs.
III IsaiahChapters 56–66, a school of prophets after 538 BC. Salvation widens to eunuchs, foreigners and the barely interested. Jesus quotes it in Nazareth: good news to the poor, freedom for captives.

Rohr argues for reading the Bible with historical context. Read literally and without study, it has been used to justify war, slavery and genocide. The goal is to let the Bible read us.

  • He traces love widening in Isaiah: from love of self, to love of neighbour, to love of the other and even the enemy.
9Ezekiel

Dry bones can live

The strangest prophet moves from chaos to a world where "the Lord is there".

  • Ezekiel eats a scroll of lament that tastes like honey, lies on his side for 390 days, sees wheels full of eyes, and watches God leave the temple. Rohr jokes he might have been on an early psychedelic experiment.
  • He is both priest and prophet, keeping his identity in exile while stressing personal responsibility and pure grace at once.
  • Rohr sees him as a prophet for our noisy, opinion-saturated age. The valley of dry bones still applies: breath can come from the four winds, and our love matters.
Part three · Chapter 10

It all comes down to love

William of Ockham, a Franciscan, taught that the simplest explanation is usually the right one. Rohr applies the razor to faith: shave away every assumption except one, God's infinite, self-giving love.

10Love

If it's love at all, it's love for everybody

The prophets reduce every question of justice, reward and punishment to one thing: divine love, which can be experienced but not proved.

  • Without people in our midst who play this role, we build insular groups blind to their own shadow, and that shadow is always an inability to love the other.
  • Evil isn't an interruption of an otherwise smooth order caused by someone else. It's mixed into all life. Becoming conscious means seeing it in ourselves too.
  • Rohr's conclusion, paraphrased: love God above all, let yourself be loved by God above all, and that will lead to a love that rescues the very people we'd rather condemn.
Start with judgment; end with divine pity.
Rohr's summary of the mature prophet, paraphrased
Practice

Walking the prophet's path today

Rohr closes with a list of marks of a true prophet. Here they are grouped as questions to carry, for anyone angry about the state of the world.

With your anger

Keep it, but don't stop thereAnger at injustice is legitimate. Ask what it would look like to weep about the same thing.
Criticise your own side firstProphets perfected self-criticism. Where is my group's shadow?

With your tears

Allow themYou can't order tears, only let them come. They soften judgment into empathy.
Go where suffering isLike Jesus, look for the pain and stand there, with and for, not against.

With change

Trust the disorderWhen the old order cracks, it may be the start of reorder.
Listen at the edgesTruth often comes from the margins and from a small remnant.
Let failure teachDarkness, relapse and wounds teach more than ideas do.

Toward love

Shave it downAsk what's left if you remove everything except God's love.
No exceptionsAs soon as you parcel out love, you've left it.
Salvation starts nowUnion with God in this life, not only reward or fear later.
