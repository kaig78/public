The Tearsof Things

Prophetic wisdom for an age of outrage

At eighty, the Franciscan teacher reads the Hebrew prophets as people who start out angry at their own nation and religion, are broken open by grief, and end up speaking of a love that leaves nobody out. He argues that their path, from anger through tears to love, is also the path God takes in the Bible, and the one we need.

The big idea

Anger, then tears, then love

Most people picture a prophet as a wild-haired man shouting doom. Rohr thinks that's the least true part of the picture. Israel had more than thirty prophets, seven of them women, and their real job was something no other ancient religion had: an officially tolerated critic of one's own people, priests and kings. Not fortune-tellers, but truth-tellers who name their own group's shadow.

His central claim: the prophets almost all begin in anger and a sense of superiority, pass through grief, and arrive at compassion. They move from accusing to weeping, and from weeping to a love that includes the enemy. The title comes from Virgil's lacrimae rerum: there are tears in things, and tears for things.

Without such voices, Rohr says, groups get violent revolutions instead of evolutions, and religion ends up sleeping comfortably with every empire. "For the untransformed self," he warns, religion is the most dangerous temptation of all. Group narcissism is more dangerous than the individual kind, because nobody inside can see it.

He offers the book as an antidote for an "age of outrage": not by dropping anger at injustice, but by letting it mature. Most anger, he observes from years of leading men's retreats, is sadness that hasn't been felt yet.

How Rohr asks you to read a prophet

From chapter 1. Jeremiah, for example, goes from his call (order) to resentment at God (disorder) to joy without evidence (reorder). Rohr adds that conservatives love order and progressives love reorder; both need to be disordered first.

Part one · Introduction and chapters 1–4

A critic from inside

Prophets love their tradition and criticise it mercilessly at the same time. They work from the edge of the inside, not from the centre and not from outside throwing stones.

IntroGood Trouble

The licensed critic

Israel invented something new: a voice, tolerated by the system, whose task was to expose the system's own blind spots. Without the prophets, Rohr says, you can't understand Jesus.

  • The prophets cover roughly 1300 BC to the return from exile. They are pictured as sentries on the wall (Isaiah, Ezekiel 3). After the exile their voice fades into moralising, so by Jesus' day scribes and Pharisees set the tone: straining gnats, swallowing camels.
  • He borrows John Lewis's phrase "good trouble". Catholics, he notes, have a sacrament of holy orders but none of holy disorder, though Paul ranks prophecy second among gifts (1 Corinthians 12:28).
  • Ken Wilber names four tasks: cleaning up, growing up, waking up, showing up. Churches mostly settle for cleaning up, the purity code. Priests enforcing purity let Christianity sit comfortably with Roman, Spanish, English and American empires.
  • The scapegoat. With René Girard, Rohr reads the "sin of the world" (John 1:29) as our habit of uniting by blaming someone: once Indigenous people, then Black Americans, now immigrants. Christianity sought its own innocence instead of solidarity with sinners. He names the abuse cover-up as structural sin, the kind we can't see while we treat social analysis as "mixing politics and religion".
  • The golden calf. Aaron the priest gives people what they want. Moses grinds the calf and makes them drink it, like confronting an addiction. Moses brings the law and smashes it: order and disorder in one person. Miriam dances the liberation.
  • Lesson number one: learn to love by watching how God loves. In nature, as Francis, Hildegard and Mary Oliver did. In Hosea, where God marries Israel. Rohr, "Dickie Boy" to his mother, believed he was her favourite of four children: feeling chosen draws you into goodness.
  • He moves "from the Ten Commandments to the eight Beatitudes", calling it a way of tears. In footnotes he admits his own privilege as a white male priest and that God is beyond gender.
1The Tears of Things

Grief is the doorway

It's hard to attack when you're weeping. Tears replace control with empathy, and the prophets end in tears, not rage.

The story · a Latin teacher, and turning eighty

Rohr's Latin teacher used to moan Virgil's phrase sunt lacrimae rerum. Without a preposition, it means both tears in things and tears for things. Now past eighty, Rohr writes that he has had to forgive almost everything for not being perfect: Christianity, the United States, politics, himself. His line: if you don't transform your pain, you will transmit it.

  • At men's initiation rites at Ghost Ranch, New Mexico, men's jaws dropped when they discovered that most of their anger was deep sadness. Jesus sobbed three times: over Jerusalem, at Lazarus' tomb, in Gethsemane.
  • The marginalised see systemic evil first. With Choctaw elder and bishop Steven Charleston he calls prophets a culture's early-warning system. What religion called "original sin" overlaps with what we now call trauma, passed between generations. God promises a heart transplant: flesh for stone (Ezekiel 36:26).
  • Except Nahum and Obadiah, the prophets all moved beyond anger. Zephaniah's opening is the dies irae of old funerals, but by chapter 3 God is dancing over you with joy. Habakkuk rages for two chapters, then makes his "great nevertheless".
  • Not every verse is mature (Jeremiah 19:9 speaks of eating children). Only the whole narrative is inspired; "grace builds on nature". Isaiah may be too anti-temple, Amos may have enjoyed being contrarian. We prophesy imperfectly (1 Corinthians 13:9).
  • He warns against a childish literal reading, the kind that asks where Cain got his wife. Tears can't be mandated, only allowed.
2Amos

Messenger to the collective

A herdsman and fig-tree dresser with no schooling sets the tone for every writing prophet: sin is not only private, it lives in systems.

The story · the cows of Bashan

Under Jeroboam II (786–746 BC), Israel was at its richest and read its prosperity as God's approval. Amos came up from Tekoa, called the wealthy women "cows of Bashan", mocked ivory beds and the music of the idle rich, and was thrown out of the royal sanctuary by the priest Amaziah. So he went home and wrote it down.

Our cultural contradictions, as Rohr lists them

We say it's wrong……but we admire
killing→war
greed→luxury and capitalism as ideals
pride→nationalism (never praised in the Bible)
lust→seduction
envy→advertising as a way of life
anger→the angry getting their way
sloth→the rich taking it easy
murder→guns as a right

The only sins we all agree on, he says, are sexual. Jesus himself showed little interest in sex except where justice and honesty were at stake.

  • Amos first condemns the neighbours (Damascus, Gaza, Tyre, Edom, Ammon, Moab), applying universal values, before turning on Israel itself.
  • Peter Drucker said culture eats strategy for breakfast; Rohr adds that culture eats religion for lunch. Catholicism became more Roman than catholic. We read the Bible as Egyptians, Babylonians and Romans, not as enslaved Hebrews. "Every viewpoint is a view from a point."
  • Jesus healed individuals but asked systemic questions: why is this man chained in a cemetery, why did this woman spend everything on doctors? Pope Francis calls it structural sin. Billy Graham's crusades converted many, but didn't add up to fewer lynchings or less poverty.
  • "Let justice flow like water" (5:24). Justice is not jailing bad guys; Jesus' image of the end is a wedding banquet. Shaming alone has produced an ocean of backlash.
  • Amos ends with wine running down the mountains and God rebuilding David's hut: restorative justice. Rohr sees Alcoholics Anonymous as a prophetic critique of Sunday-only Christianity: responsibility, a real experience of a Higher Power, a small group.
3A Critical Mass

The secret of the remnant

God reforms from the edges. A small group that lets itself be loved keeps the whole sane, like the critical mass in a chemical reaction.

  • Hebrews out of Egypt, David against Goliath, the smallest clan of Bethlehem (Micah 5:1), barren Sarah and Elizabeth, twelve fishermen rather than the temple team. Power distorts truth, so God plants it at the margins. "The victims are the victors."
  • Zephaniah's humble and lowly remnant is "chosen" because it trusts a universal love, not because God plays favourites. "Where two or three are gathered" assumes this theology. Searching for truth at the edges instead of claiming the "one true church" might have prevented the religious wars.
  • In the 19th century enslaved Black Christians understood the liberating gospel, while the white majority kept an imperial Christianity. Rohr finds two or three people in every church who keep the group sane.
  • Prophets rarely hold office, but don't dismiss priests either: Jesus sends the healed leper to show himself to the priest. Rare combinations of power and prophecy include David after Nathan, Thomas Becket, Óscar Romero and Pope Francis.
  • Protection. When Francis stripped naked before his father, Bishop Guido covered him with his cape. Francis then always wanted a "cardinal protector"; Rohr had one in Cincinnati and Santa Fe. Prophets today have no institutional protection.
  • Hosea is told to marry Gomer, a prostitute: love with skin in the game, unreturned. He is the first to move from law to heart, with images of a parent lifting an infant to the cheek, a first husband, a treaty with the animals. Circumcision becomes a circumcised heart, and Jeremiah later speaks of "uncircumcised ears".
  • Hidden prophets don't shout (Isaiah 42:2). Dorothy Day opposed America's entry into the Second World War and refused the 1950s air-raid drills, sitting in a park instead.
4Welcoming Holy Disorder

Trust the disorder too

Every order must eventually show its shadow. The summary word for all three stages is trust: in order (parents, religion), in disorder (prophets), in reorder (love, community).

Ten qualities of a true prophet

1 · Problem inside the solutionNames what's wrong within a bigger hope, as the Vatican II bishops tried to.
2 · DetachedFrom their own opinion and status. Desmond Tutu at the Truth and Reconciliation Commission.
3 · Loves opponentsGandhi, Martin Luther King.
4 · No need to be firstDorothy Day living alongside the homeless.
5 · Knows the traditionSome theology and Scripture; an "alternative orthodoxy" from the edge of the inside.
6 · Thinks beyond egoNot just "the Bible says".
7 · A team playerLoyal to a couple of communities. Viktor Frankl went to synagogue and to Mass with his wife.
8 · The issue is the issueNot a vehicle for fame.
9 · Pure motivesMother Teresa through her dark night.
10 · Fruits of the SpiritLike the women who founded religious communities.

Counterexample: Jonathan Edwards' "Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God". Intensity is not the same as truth.

  • Vatican II never fully took hold because the church looked fine in 1965. Only those who grew up in the old church, Rohr says, know how broken it was.
  • He founded the Center for Action and Contemplation in Albuquerque in 1987 to train a "loyal opposition": contemplative activists. The most important word is "and".
  • Those most invested in order often need the biggest dose of disorder: the rule-follower whose rules fail, the homophobe whose child comes out, the literalist who meets contradictions.
  • Money corrupts. The prophet's reward (Matthew 10:41) is nothing but delivering the message. Francis refused ordination. Franciscan stipends became salaries; institutions hire you to domesticate you. Rohr, who joined the friars in 1961, never needed employment and taught for free.
  • Obedience, from ob-audire, means deep listening. His forty-day hermitages were listening stations. His personal consolation is the Heart Sutra's "gate, gate, paragate": gone over to the other shore.
  • Jesus himself accepted holy disorder: the Syrophoenician woman argued back ("even the dogs eat the crumbs"), and he praised her great faith. The church excommunicated many would-be reformers, from Origen and Jan Hus to the Beguines and Leonardo Boff.

5Jeremiah

The patterns that carry us across

The reluctant prophet warns before the exile, tells people to trust it during, and receives the Bible's highest peak: a covenant written on the heart.

The story · rescued from the cistern

Jeremiah was active from 626 BC until the temple fell in 586. Officials accused him of seeking the city's ruin and dropped him into a muddy cistern. He was pulled out by Ebed-melech, an Ethiopian eunuch, and three men: rescued from the edges. Rohr chose Jeremiah 1 ("I am only a boy") as the reading for his own first Mass in 1970.

Two covenants

First covenant
  • Two-sided: "if you obey" (Exodus 19:5)
  • Law on stone tablets
  • Deserving and earning
New covenant (31:31–34)
  • One-sided: God simply does it
  • Law written on the heart
  • "I will never call your sins to mind"

The new covenant surpasses the old rather than destroying it. Christians repeat "new and everlasting covenant" at every Eucharist, yet stay tit-for-tat. Certainty, Rohr notes, often comforts more than love does.

  • "You have misled me… a fire in my bones" (20:7–9). Only Jacob wrestling and Teresa of Ávila match this intensity. Rohr finds it speaks to depression and doubt.
  • Jesus quotes Jeremiah's "den of robbers". Both move the sacred from a place to a person: Jesus calls his body the temple. God doesn't need worship or he'd be codependent; Isaiah 1 is sick of burnt offerings and pleads for the widow instead.
  • Watch out for "defenders of the metaphors", who love the bread and water but not what they point to. Good rituals point to the good, the true and the beautiful; check who is being scapegoated.
  • Biblical punishments happen in this world and to groups (famine, war), with corporate grace too. Christians moved punishment to individuals and the next world, and now ignore climate, war and racism.
  • Rohr's wedding homilies: honour the plank of order, walk the plank of disorder, fall into the ocean of everything. Reorder shows itself as less violent and more universal: "I will rejoice in doing them good, with all my heart" (32:41).
  • "A personal decision for Christ" can be ego assertion. A heart of flesh means I'm not the main actor in love. The anguish of every prophet: people refusing to be loved undeservedly. King Jehoiakim cut up Jeremiah's scroll and burned it.
6Unfinished Prophets

Elijah, Jonah, John

Some prophets stay in dualistic anger. They can still be used by God, in clay jars, but they show only half the message.

ElijahWins the contest on Carmel, then slaughters 450 prophets of Baal. Only later, at Horeb, does he meet God in a gentle breeze, and covers his face.
JonahFlees, is swallowed, preaches, and Nineveh repents. He's furious that his message works. God cares for people who "can't tell right from left", and for the animals.
John the BaptizerHumble and ready to step aside, yet a judgmental outsider ("brood of vipers") who never heard the parables. Beheaded as a martyr for the purity code.
  • Passion isn't prophecy; smug isn't holy; contrarians are just contrarians. A true prophet leaves success to God and doesn't lose heart if no one listens.
  • Rohr collects images of a man inside a whale. Jonah went down before going up; the Ninevites converted, Jonah didn't.
  • Maybe two thousand years of Christianity has been more John's religion than Jesus': baptism as an identity badge and a "cult of innocence", not initiation into death and solidarity. Luther, he notes, was more aware of grace than Rome, yet stayed angry.
  • "You're making it political." MLK, Catherine of Siena, Sojourner Truth and Cesar Chavez were concrete but spiritually driven. True prophets are internationalists, never partisan. Ask yourself: do I need to win or humiliate? Is it Jesus or John Wayne?
  • Job belongs among the prophets (the Qur'an lists him so). He refuses to believe God is punishing him and rebukes his friends' logic of retribution: "I knew you by hearsay, now I have seen you." For Rohr, Job is the summit of the Old Testament. God loves us because God is good, not because we are.
7The Alchemy of Tears

You cry yourself into new thinking

Lamentations is the only biblical book named after a feeling. Anger can't make the switches that change minds and churches; tears can.

The story · caves of grief

For ten years Rohr led male initiation rites; day three was the "day of grief", where men broke through or didn't. In Kenya, two Maasai warriors showed him their caves of grief, where boys had to cry for the whole world before they could become men. He wishes the same for politicians, executives, soldiers and clergy.

  • Lamentations (587 BC) has almost no anecdotes: a universal sadness that invites universal solidarity. Midway through comes "his mercies are new every morning" (3:22–23). Rohr suggests reading all five chapters when grieving, as a blank-slate prayer.
  • Tears changed stances anger never could: the church's view of suicide, AA's view of addiction as illness, Pope Francis blessing gay couples. Has God changed, or have we grown up?
  • Trauma lives in the body, so Jesus touches when he heals. "If we do not mythologise our pain, we pathologise it." The communion of saints was the last phrase added to the creed; one day, Rohr hopes, a communion of sinners too.
  • Francis is always crying in the early biographies. After 54 years of ministry Rohr thinks the gift of tears and the gift of healing are nearly the same. He admits he sobs at family reunions, even at people finding lost dogs.
  • Mary at the foot of the cross is prophecy without words. Hospice and grief work are undervalued: doctors over nurses, priests over nuns. Simone Weil and Saint Albert Chmielowski chose solidarity over innocence.
  • Jung's alchemy. Hold the ingredients (desire and rejection, life and death, Republican and Democrat) without forcing an outcome until a free yes arises. Gerald May calls it willing rather than willful. Hezekiah, healed, twitters like a swallow: "you have thrown my sins behind your back."
  • Grace arrives uninvited, like tears. His seventh-grade teacher, Sister Clara, told him: you must cooperate with grace. What he's left with now isn't anger but "a sadness I can love with".

It all comes down to love

Isaiah and Ezekiel end where Rohr wants us to end: with grace that comes before we ask and includes those we'd rather leave out.

8The Three Isaiahs

Love keeps widening

Read in context, the book of Isaiah spans three eras and shows love expanding: from self, to neighbour, to the stranger and even the enemy.

One book, three moments

I Isaiah · chapters 1–39Called around 742 BC under the Assyrian threat. Anger turns into lament, even for enemy Moab ("my heart quivers like a lyre"). The vision of "holy, holy, holy" undoes and remakes his ego at once.
II Isaiah · chapters 40–55Written in Babylonian exile, the "book of consolation": can a mother forget her baby? Four servant songs show a vulnerable, nonviolent servant. God groans like a woman in labour.
III Isaiah · the late chaptersA school after 538 BC. Eunuchs and foreigners are welcomed, and God says "here I am" to people who weren't looking. New heavens and a new earth.

Read literally and without study, Rohr says, the Bible has justified war, slavery and genocide. Historical reading lets the Bible read us.

  • "Divine perfection is the ability to include imperfection." Forgiveness means "yes, and". Love and suffering are the two wrecking balls of the ego.
  • Exile was Israel's needed holy disorder: it had a healthy sense of being loved, but had to lose its national superiority. A group's shadow is the most hidden of all. Conservatives try to enforce order better, liberals add more disorder; neither works alone.
  • The servant songs feed a line of nonviolent resisters: Gandhi, Rosa Parks, Bayard Rustin, Simone Weil, Dorothy Day. Redemptive suffering, not redemptive violence.
  • In Nazareth, Jesus reads Isaiah 61, good news to the poor and freedom to captives, but stops before "the day of vengeance", rolls up the scroll and sits down.
  • The book's last verse mentions the fire of Gehenna, later read as hell. But biblical fire is a refiner's fire, and a God who revels in eternal punishment couldn't be imitated. Matthew, Rohr notes, likes to add threats.
9Ezekiel

Grace before we ask

The strangest prophet starts in chaos and ends with a city named "the Lord is there". In between, God forgives people who never asked.

The story · a young friar's surprise

Ezekiel 16 is a long, harsh picture of Israel as an unfaithful lover. Then, out of nowhere, God says he will forgive everything. As a young friar, Rohr was shocked that nobody had ever pointed out this break. Later God acts "for my name's sake, not as your conduct deserves" (20:44): God acts, never just reacts.

  • Ezekiel eats a scroll of lament that tastes like honey, sees wheels covered in eyes, and lies on his side for 390 days. Rohr calls it an early psychedelic experiment. God leaves the temple in a huff, as if Jesus walked out of St Peter's.
  • He was priest and prophet, orthodox but untraditional. Judaism, remarkably, kept its critics in its scriptures. Catholics, Rohr says, usually burned theirs, and Protestants tuned them out.
  • He condemns usury, which Christianity treated as a mortal sin for its first thousand years.
  • Chapter 36: total disqualification and total requalification are both God's doing, with a new heart and clean water, no reciprocity demanded. Like trauma, grace runs through generations: whole families' love traces back to one holy grandparent. Make that your vocation.
  • The valley of dry bones: "Can these bones live?" Our job is to breathe with God over dry bones. Rohr notes it has been his job for over eighty years.
  • Eating the scroll means taking it all in: bitter truth, sweet love. Watching a film about the James Webb telescope, Rohr felt Ezekiel's awe. Universal responsibility, he says, is a better lesson than universal damnation.
10It All Comes Down to Love

Shave it down to love

Like Occam's razor, the prophets reduce every question of justice, reward and punishment to one assumption: God's self-giving love, which can be experienced but not proved.

The parable · the angel with the torch and the pail

Rohr told this story in his first years of preaching in the 1970s, and an old friend recently said it changed his life. An angel walks through the world with a torch and a bucket of water. Asked why, the angel says: with the torch I'm burning down the mansions of heaven, with the water I'm putting out the fires of hell. Only then will we know who really loves God.

  • Become conscious. Evil flourishes when denied and when it seems rational and necessary to good people. "Out the demon" and it loses power. Holiness is years of struggling with your shadow, not moral perfection. Divine light shows our sameness; human glare shows our superiority.
  • Jesus' "woes" in Matthew 23 are an invitation to what AA calls a fearless moral inventory. "Surely not I?" ask the apostles. Anger hardens, sadness saves. The last veil falls when you see your own projections and want to weep.
  • God's love is the standard. In the prophets, God too moves from anger to tears to love. We made Jesus every nation's mascot: theologian for the Greeks, warrior for Rome, conquistador, capitalist ally, therapeutic best friend. The real questions are what God is doing and how God acts.
  • Obstacles. "I don't believe in the God they say doesn't exist either." Certainty, not doubt, is the opposite of faith: it reveals a need for control, and colliding certainties make religious violence.
  • Faithful critics. Many secular organisations protect whistleblowers better than churches. We need a better name than "devil's advocate": loyal opposition, wounded healers rather than wounded wounders. Ecclesia semper reformanda.
  • Can pro-lifers see war and the death penalty as life issues? Can the left see its individualism and lack of transcendence? If it's love at all, it has to be love for everybody.
Start with judgment; end with divine pity.

After the last chapter

The prophet's way, in Rohr's own synthesis

Rohr closes with three short appendices: a list of marks of a true prophet, a timeline of Israel's prophets, and the seven themes behind all his teaching.

Marks of a true prophet

The priest builds the container and the prophet makes it worth keeping. They aren't enemies, but they aren't codependent either. Analysis without synthesis is just criticism, and synthesis without analysis is false comfort. Prophets do both. Condensed from his list:

  • They look for where suffering is and go there, building communities of solidarity.
  • They are mystical and unitive, not argumentative; for and with, never against.
  • They make self-criticism their first priority.
  • They don't try to prove themselves worthy, pure or innocent.
  • They include opposites, live with paradox, and learn from reality and creation.
  • They focus on growth rather than sin, and on salvation now rather than later reward or punishment.
  • They don't reject the priest's way; they have just moved beyond it.
  • They begin with judgment and end with divine pity, drawing out tears more than anger, mostly tears of gratitude and joy.

Seven themes of an "alternative orthodoxy"

MethodScripture checked by experience, experience checked by tradition.
FoundationIf God is Trinity and Jesus is God's face, the universe is benevolent.
FrameBecause of the Incarnation, no real split between sacred and profane.
Everything belongsNo one needs to be scapegoated; darkness shows itself in the light.
TransformationThe separate self is the problem, not the shadow.
ProcessThe way down is the way through: failure and wounds teach.
GoalDivine union, not private perfection.

Practice

Walking the prophet's path today

Questions and habits drawn from the whole book, for anyone angry about the state of the world or their church.

With your anger

Keep it, but don't stop thereAnger at injustice is legitimate. Ask what it would look like to weep about the same thing.
Criticise your own side firstProphets perfected self-criticism. Where is my group's shadow, the one nobody inside can see?
Jesus or John Wayne?Do I need to win or humiliate? Is there space between me and my message?

With your tears

Allow themYou can't order tears, only let them come. You cry yourself into new thinking.
Pray LamentationsWhen grieving, read all five chapters in one sitting, as a blank-slate prayer.
Go where suffering isLike Jesus, look for the pain and stand there, with and for, not against.

With the Bible

Read in three coloursMark threat red, inner conflict yellow, the word suffered through to green. Follow the arc.
Trust the disorderWhen the old order cracks, it may be the start of reorder.
Listen at the edgesTruth often comes from the margins and from a small remnant.

Toward love

Shave it downAsk what's left if you remove everything except God's love.
No exceptionsAs soon as you parcel out love, you've left it.
Be a loyal criticStay inside, love the tradition, and say the uncomfortable thing with respect.
