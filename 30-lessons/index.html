30Lessons for Living

Tried and true advice from the wisest Americans

A Cornell gerontologist asked more than a thousand people over 65 what they'd learned about life. Their answers were surprisingly consistent, and often not what younger people expect.

The big idea

Ask the people who have already lived it

Pillemer spent thirty years studying older people's problems: caregiving, chronic pain, nursing homes. At fifty he started to wonder whether he'd missed the most useful question. Instead of asking what goes wrong in old age, why not ask old people what they know about living?

He calls them the experts. They're not gurus or celebrities. They've simply been where younger people haven't, and many lived through the Depression, World War II, poverty and loss. From the end of the road, they can see which choices worked out and which ones didn't.

What they say often goes against conventional wisdom. Some of it sounds liberal (tolerance, skepticism about money and status), some conservative (marriage as a lifelong commitment). The real gap is in how they think about time.

Where the lessons came from

Pillemer read the transcripts again and again and pulled out the advice that came up most often. It's a qualitative study, not a vote count, and all the names in the book are pseudonyms.

Part 1 · Chapters 2–3 · Lessons 1–10

Love and work

Freud said love and work are the cornerstones of a happy life. The experts agree, and they're blunter than most advice books about what goes wrong in both.

02Great Together

Lessons for a happy marriage

Marriage is hard even when it works. Each lesson is a way of making it a little easier.

01Marry someone a lot like you

This was close to unanimous, across politics, class and race. What counts is having the same core values: money, children, honesty, how to live. A fight over an expensive golf club is really a fight about values. To find a match you have to know your own values first. And forget about changing your partner after the wedding. It almost never happens.

02Friendship is as important as romantic love

"I married my best friend" was the most common secret. The intense passion fades, so ask yourselves: if we weren't in love, would we be friends? One expert put it this way: your spouse should be the kid you most wanted to play with on the playground.

03Don't keep score

"Give and take" doesn't mean fifty-fifty. Both partners give 100 percent, and in a hard year it may be 90/10. Ask yourself each morning what you could do to make your partner's day a little happier.

04Talk to each other

The most common regret about a marriage was a spouse who couldn't or wouldn't talk. Fights are inevitable. One couple married 67 years still has two a week. What matters is how you have them, and you can find out whether someone can communicate before you marry them.

05Don't just commit to your partner, commit to marriage itself

The experts married expecting it to be for life, and that expectation carried them through bad stretches. Many came close to leaving and are grateful they stayed. The exceptions are clear: abuse, repeated infidelity, or conflict that truly can't be resolved.

BONUSDon't go to bed angry

Never go to sleep without saying "I love you", even through gritted teeth. You never know what will happen in the night.

Not 50/50, but 100/100

Albert Folsom, married 59 years, pointed to a statue in his town museum: two workhorses straining against one load, titled "As of One Mind". That, he said, was his marriage.

How long-married couples fight

  • Get out of the houseA drive or a walk takes the heat out of it.
  • Write it, then bin itWrite the angry letter, reread it the next day, throw it away.
  • Talk to the sheepLet off steam alone first. One farmer went to the barn.
  • Ban the teasingOne couple agreed to stop for good when it turned nasty.
  • Take the floorOne talks for five minutes. The other repeats it back until they get it right.
  • Who cares more?Ask which of you this matters to more, and let the other one give way.
The story

Sandy Hudgens married just before her husband shipped out. He fought in Patton's army at the Battle of the Bulge and came home a different man. He was withdrawn, couldn't sleep and was no longer the cheerful singer she'd married. There were years when leaving seemed reasonable. She stayed and kept trying, and they had twenty good years together at the end. She calls them a bonus for both of them.

03Glad to Get Up in the Morning

Lessons for a fulfilling career

On no topic were the experts more adamant: years spent in a job you dread are a tragic waste.

06Choose a career for the intrinsic rewards, not the money

People who chose a field for the pay tend to look back and ask "What have I done?" Earning less and enjoying the work is, to the experts, an easy trade. There will always be someone richer, so if money is the goal you'll always lose the comparison.

07Don't give up on looking for a job that makes you happy

Taking the wrong job isn't the tragedy. Staying in it is. Spend a year or two learning a field, and if it's wrong, move on, whatever your age.

08Make the most of a bad job

You can't always love your job, but you can learn from any job. Watch everything, do the work well anyway, and take pride in it. Even a terrible boss is useful: he's a bad example.

09Emotional intelligence trumps every other kind

Technical brilliance doesn't protect you if you can't get on with people. Pillemer saw a star academic voted out by his own colleagues. The experts value patience, humility, holding back judgment, and seeing the issue from the other side.

10Everyone needs autonomy

Asked to choose between a regimented job and a lower-paid one where they could plan their own day, the experts chose freedom. Purpose and autonomy go together. A man who worked his way up from changing tires to owning the store called it his best job: he was finally his own boss.

BONUSThe first thing in the morning

The test is simple. If you dread leaving for work most mornings, take it seriously. It may be time for a change.

Look out the window, not in the mirror

Father Jim gave this advice to priests going through dark periods. Pillemer found it useful for anyone whose ego is caught up in their job. Comparing yourself to others clouds your judgment. Turn your attention to the people you work with.

The story

Martin Sanderson grew up poor during the Depression and dreamed of flying after Lindbergh crossed the Atlantic. The military was segregated, and at first Black men couldn't fly for the air force. He became one of the Tuskegee Airmen and flew combat missions. He says he was afraid, and anyone being shot at who wasn't afraid had no sense. His advice is to refuse to let other people's limits decide what you do.

Part 2 · Chapters 4–5 · Lessons 11–20

Family and growing old

Raising kids looks completely different from the other end, when what you most want is for your adult children to enjoy your company. Aging looks different too: much better than the young fear, if you prepare for it.

04Nobody's Perfect

Lessons for a lifetime of parenting

Pillemer pushed past platitudes and asked: what do you know about raising children that younger parents don't?

11It's all about time

Children want you far more than anything money can buy. The happiest memories are of shared activities: building a shed, fishing trips, ball games. It's worth earning less to have that time. One mother's regret was being so lost in work thoughts on the drive home that she didn't hear the little voices in the back seat.

12It's normal to have favourites, but never show it

In Pillemer's research, about 70% of mothers can name the child they feel closest to. That's natural, so don't feel guilty about it. But keep it to yourself. Ninety-year-olds still remember a parent saying "she's the pretty one."

13Don't hit your kids

Pillemer expected a "spare the rod" generation. Most experts instead said they regretted any spanking they'd done, and research backs them up. A third or more never hit their children, and those children turned out fine.

14Avoid a rift at all costs

An estranged child was among the deepest pain anyone described. Rifts usually start over something that felt huge at the time, like a remarriage, a lifestyle or a child coming out, and was almost never worth losing the relationship.

15Take a lifelong view of relationships with children

The years from 30 to 50 pass in what Pillemer calls the "middle-aged blur". After that come decades of relationship with adult children. Make today's decisions with that future in mind: children who like you and want to be around you.

BONUSAbandon perfection

They tried to raise perfect children by Dr. Spock and found it doesn't work that way. Be good enough. Let children make mistakes and learn from them.

The blur is short; the relationship is long

Essie plays cutthroat pinochle every other Sunday with her daughter. Nobody lets anybody win. That's the payoff the experts mean. Illustrative timeline, not data.

The story

Mike Hoyt was as traditional as the experts come: work, family, sports. When his son came out, his first reaction was to worry about what people in town would say. He now calls that selfish and stupid. He and his wife changed their views and kept their son. Gwen Hagerman faced worse: her daughter had an affair with Gwen's second husband. After a few estranged years she decided it was the only daughter she had. Helping with the grandchildren brought them back together, and they're friends today.

05Find the Magic

Lessons for aging fearlessly and well

The young fear old age most. The old fear it least.

16Being old is much better than you think

Some experts said they're happier now than ever. They have more clarity, they've let go of things that once seemed vital, and they no longer feel responsible for their grown children's choices. Each decade brought opportunities the previous one didn't have.

17Act now like you'll need your body for a hundred years

Their point isn't about dying sooner. It's about living for decades with chronic illness. "You've got to die sometime" ignores the long, painful middle, which the whole family lives through too. Smoking and heavy drinking came up again and again.

18Don't worry about dying, the experts don't

Believers and atheists alike described acceptance and curiosity, not terror. What worried them was practical: getting the will done, the boxes of papers and the photo albums nobody will want.

19Stay connected

Retirement, widowhood and moving away all shrink your social circle. The experts are deliberate about it: see someone every day, accept every invitation, stay curious, keep learning.

20Plan ahead about where you'll live (and your parents too)

Decide before a crisis forces the decision. Many who moved into senior communities said it brought more company and freedom, not less. Readers under sixty should read this one for their parents.

BONUSAbandon the fight against aging

Skip the gimmicks. Accept and adapt. If you can't run as fast, run slower, but keep running.

Accept and adapt

Priscilla Linares and four friends meet every year in the Adirondacks. She could have stayed angry that she can't climb the tall peaks anymore. Instead she hikes the ones she can. Illustrative diagram, not data.

Staying connected, on purpose

See someone every day Accept every invitation Stay curious Keep learning Fill the weekend by Wednesday

After her husband died, Sandy Hudgens noticed she was getting isolated and made a rule to accept every invitation, whatever it was. Another widow no longer likes an open weekend. If nothing is planned by Wednesday, she starts calling people. Both habits work at any age, and the experts warn that circles start to shrink in middle age.

Part 3 · Chapters 6–8 · Lessons 21–30

How to live

The last two chapters zoom out: what people regret at the end, and the worldview underneath all thirty lessons, which comes down to knowing that time is short.

06I Can Look Everyone in the Eye

Lessons for a life without regrets

You probably can't avoid every regret, but you can make the big ones less likely.

21Always be honest

Out of every possible virtue, honesty came up again and again. Dishonesty costs you sleep. One man who cut corners on import duties still remembers lying awake. Infidelity came up as a lifelong regret. The chapter's title is the payoff: being able to look everyone in the eye.

22Say yes to opportunities

Say yes unless you have a really solid reason to say no. Don't turn down a promotion, a move or a chance just because it scares you or because you don't meet every requirement on paper. People trap themselves inside what they think their limits are.

23Travel more

Travel was one of the most common regrets. Many grew up never leaving their county, and the war showed them the world. Don't wait for retirement, because your partner may not be there. Make a list of places, and line it up against the years you'll be able to travel comfortably.

24Choose a mate with extreme care

A bad match causes decades of regret. Don't rush. People in love are on their best behaviour, so take the time to see what's underneath. Look as far into the future as you can, and ask yourself honestly why you're marrying.

25Say it now

The lasting regrets were about things left unsaid, not things said. Say "I love you", apologise, ask your parents the questions only they can answer. "Send flowers to the living."

BONUSLighten up

When an old choice still bothers you after many years, the experts give you permission to forgive yourself. Be kind to others, and gentle with yourself.

Some regrets can be fixed later. Some can't.

Pillemer's point is that unmet goals like education can often be made up late in life. Words left unsaid can't be once the person is gone. Travel falls in between: plan for comfortable travel until about 80, unless you turn out to be the exception. Illustrative diagram, not data.

The story

Hal Phipps was married 55 years and is still grieving his wife three years after her death. She was stoic and so was he, and they rarely said the big things out loud. His one regret is that he never told her often enough that she was the only woman he ever wanted to marry. His advice to younger people: say it.

07Choose Happiness

Lessons for living like an expert

The final five aren't about one area of life. They're the outlook behind all the others.

26Time is of the essence

This is the heart of the book. The experts know deeply that life is short, and it shapes every other lesson. Stop saying "I can't wait until…". There are no wheelchair ramps to the bottom of the Grand Canyon, so go while you can walk.

27Happiness is a choice, not a condition

Instead of being "happy if only…", choose to be happy anyway, every day, despite your problems. Nearly everyone over seventy has lived through tragedy, which is why this advice carries weight from them. Many could name the moment they decided.

28Time spent worrying is time wasted

The experts were unambiguous here: looking back, worry is time they wish they'd spent on something else. Worry dwells on threats that haven't arrived. Problem solving acts on ones that have. Prepare for what you can, then let the rest go.

29Think small

Waiting for big milestones to make you happy is a trap. Enjoy small daily pleasures instead: a morning coffee, deer outside the window. Gratitude comes naturally once you know your days are limited. Why wait until you're eighty to learn it?

30Have faith

For most experts, faith was central. It gave them community, ritual and strength in the worst times. Even non-believers valued the rituals. Cora Jenkins, 97, described the ideal as a faith that is "deep but unfanatical".

BONUSThe golden rule

In the end it comes down to who you helped. One expert said people who lived only for themselves leave no ripples, and nobody comes to their funerals.

Worrying vs. preparing

Worry
  • Happens when there's nothing concrete to deal with
  • Goes over what might happen, again and again
  • Uses up energy and changes nothing
  • Regretted as wasted time
Problem solving
  • Deals with something that's actually happening
  • Writes the speech, practises the piece
  • Then lets go: "This too will pass"
  • Leaves you free

One expert's version: if the doctor might have bad news, don't worry yet, because you don't know. If he gives you bad news, worrying still won't help, so deal with it.

The story

Ruth Helm, whose love story opens the marriage chapter, lost her 21-year-old daughter Sherry in a plane crash in the 1970s. For two years she went through each day mechanically and escaped into sleep. Then her other daughter came home from college and told her: Mom, you're always so sad, it makes me want to run away from you. That day Ruth chose to be happy, for the family she still had. Her neighbours now assume she's never had a bad day in her life.

Chapter 8 · The last lesson

Go and ask an elder

Most of the experts were pessimistic about the book. They believed their advice was good, but they'd come to think the young see them as irrelevant. Our social lives are sorted by age: almost all our friends are within ten years of us, and few thirty-year-olds invite a seventy-year-old over to watch the game.

Pillemer's last lesson is to close that gap yourself. The average age of his interviewees was about the average American life span, and within ten years fewer than a quarter of them will still be alive. His daughter and son-in-law took his question list to a 94-year-old aunt they'd known for 35 years. They spent an hour on it and learned more about her than in all the years before.

Asking for lessons rather than stories gets you deeper answers.

Three lives back to the Revolution

Pillemer's oldest interviewee was born in 1908. Her grandfather, born around 1850, told her about the Civil War first hand. The oldest people he knew as a child would have remembered the Revolutionary War. That's how practical wisdom used to be passed on: through lives that overlap.

Ten questions to ask the experts in your life

  1. What are the most important lessons you've learned over the course of your life? (If they're stuck: if a young person asked what you've learned in your years, what would you say?)
  2. What advice do you have about getting and staying married? What mistakes should young people avoid? What would you tell a couple thinking of calling it quits?
  3. What advice do you have about raising children, and what mistakes should parents avoid?
  4. What have you learned about finding fulfilling work and succeeding in a career?
  5. Did a difficult or stressful experience teach you something important? What?
  6. Looking back, were there turning points that changed the course of your life? Which choices taught you the most?
  7. What do you know now about living a happy life that you didn't know at twenty?
  8. What are the major values or principles you live by?
  9. Have you learned any lessons about staying healthy?
  10. What advice would you give people about growing older?
