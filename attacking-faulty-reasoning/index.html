AttackingFaultyReasoning

A practical guide to fallacy-free arguments

Damer's claim is that every fallacy is the same mistake underneath: the argument fails one of five tests. Learn the tests and you can spot bad reasoning, fix it, and build arguments that hold up.

The big idea

A fallacy is a broken test, not an item on a list

Most logic books hand you a catalogue of mistakes to avoid. Damer thinks that is backwards. A list of don'ts doesn't tell you what a good argument looks like, and it doesn't explain why the mistakes are mistakes.

His alternative: a good argument meets five criteria. Its structure is sound, its premises are relevant and acceptable, together they are sufficient, and it rebuts the strongest objections. A fallacy is any violation of one of those five. The 60 named fallacies are just the violations that happen often enough to have earned a name. Most faulty reasoning you meet has no name at all, and the five criteria still catch it.

The book also teaches how to respond. Each fallacy comes with an "attacking the fallacy" section, and the aim is never to score points. It's to steer the discussion back toward finding the most defensible position.

The classroom experiment

Near the end of his reasoning course, Damer has the class pick a moral issue it is split on, from gays in the military to vegetarianism. The two sides face each other and argue for three class periods while he only calls out breaches of the code. For more than twenty years, the class has almost always reached complete agreement. It is usually on a third position that is better than either side's starting point.

The core framework

Five tests every argument must pass

An argument that meets all five is a good one, and you should accept its conclusion. Each test comes in degrees, so applying them is a judgment call. But they give that judgment a shape.

A good argument is an umbrella with no missing panels

The picture borrows the book's cover. Take away any panel and objections get through. The fifth, rebuttal, is the one Damer says is weakest in almost everyone's arguments, his own included.

Part 1 · Chapters 1–4

How arguments work

Before naming a single fallacy, Damer sets out the ground rules for discussion, what counts as an argument, what makes one good, and a theory that ties every fallacy back to those standards.

01A Code of Intellectual Conduct

Twelve rules for arguing fairly

The code is both a method and an ethic. Discussions that follow rules like these settle issues more often, and arguing fairly is something we owe each other.

  • Procedural: research in speech communication finds that discussions with ground rules like these resolve more disputes than those without. Damer's classroom results agree.
  • Ethical: we get indignant when an opponent won't play by the rules. That reaction is moral, so we should hold ourselves to the same standard.
  • Fallibility comes first. If you won't admit your view might be wrong, you are saying no argument could change your mind, so there's no point talking. With hundreds of conflicting religious positions, the odds that any one person's is the true one are low. Most past knowledge claims in science later turned out flawed or false.
  • Try it in a heated discussion: be the first to admit you might be wrong. Others will usually follow. If they won't, at least you know further talk is futile.
  • One definition of a critical thinker that Damer likes: someone who has changed their mind about something important at least once in the past year, by force of argument.
  • Clarity means confusing language is where the discussion should start working, not where it stops. "It's just semantics" ends too many debates that matter.

The code at a glance

1FallibilityAccept that your starting view may not be the most defensible one.

Principles 1 to 3 are the standard rules of any serious inquiry. Principles 4 and 5 are about how to present and reconstruct a case. Principles 6 to 10 are the five criteria. Principles 11 and 12 tell you when a discussion is over.

02What Is an Argument?

A claim supported by other claims

An argument isn't a quarrel. It's a conclusion backed by at least one premise. Without the backing, it's just an opinion.

  • Premises can be facts, observations, expert testimony, common knowledge, definitions or principles, or the conclusions of earlier arguments. Signal words like "therefore" and "because" help, but real arguments rarely use them neatly.
  • A letter or an editorial can have a point without being an argument. If nothing supports the claim, there's nothing to evaluate.
  • "That's just your opinion" misses the target when the opinion is the conclusion of an argument. Criticize the argument instead.
  • Burden of proof sits with whoever makes the claim, like a drug maker before regulators or a prosecutor in court. "Proof" here means a preponderance of evidence, not certainty. You can avoid the burden honestly with the agnostic option: "I have no reason to believe X" commits you to much less than "X is false".
  • Deductive vs. inductive. In a valid deductive argument, true premises guarantee the conclusion. An inductive argument only makes the conclusion probable, however many premises pile up. Recasting an inductive case around a general principle can give it deductive force.
  • Value arguments (moral, legal and aesthetic) need a value premise: a moral principle, a governing law, or an aesthetic standard. You can't get from "is" to "ought" without one. "Beauty is in the eye of the beholder" is usually what people say when they don't know how to make the aesthetic case.

Standard form: lay out the parts before you judge

Strip out the clutter, state any unstated parts in [brackets], keep subpremises under the premise they support, and put the conclusion last. If you need more than four or five premises, you've probably missed the core. Few real arguments include a rebuttal premise, but a good argument needs one.

The principle of charity in practice

// Kate: "Lisa's Brahms was terrible.
//        She didn't play it the way he intended."

Since [a performance is only good if it
       follows the composer's intention],
                         (implicit premise)
and   Lisa's didn't follow Brahms's intention,
                         (premise)
Therefore, Lisa's performance wasn't good.
                         (conclusion)

Rebuild the argument the arguer meant to make, in its strongest version, and let them correct your reconstruction. Here, simply saying the hidden premise out loud is enough. Few people believe performers mustn't interpret, and we rarely know what a composer intended anyway.

“An opinion is an unsupported claim; an argument is a supported claim.”
Damer, ch. 2
03What Is a Good Argument?

Five criteria, applied with judgment

Any claim with one supporting claim is an argument. A good argument passes all five tests, and then you're obliged to accept its conclusion.

  • Acceptability, not truth. Damer swaps "true" for "acceptable". Absolute truth is rarely provable, a true premise does nothing if the audience can't check it, and what we usually mean by "true" is "accepted as true". But acceptable doesn't mean whatever people happen to believe. It means what a mature, rational person should accept.
  • Common knowledge isn't a head count. Most Americans believe God exists, but competent scholars dispute it, so it can be the conclusion of an argument but not an uncontested premise.
  • Sufficiency is the hardest test to apply. It depends on context: choosing a candidate needs different grounds from deciding whether to lease a car. Most disagreements come down to how much weight each piece of evidence deserves. Experience helps.
  • Rebuttal separates good arguments from merely clever ones. A jury that heard only the prosecutor would nearly always convict. Two opposing arguments can both look fine on the first four tests, but they can't both be right. The tie is usually broken by which one answers the other side better.
  • A serious objection is one a reasonable person would think needs an answer. Answering it in advance disarms the critic, because their big guns are spiked before they fire.

Which premises should you accept?

Accept a premise if it is
  • Undisputed common knowledge
  • Confirmed by your own experience or observation
  • Defended in the argument, or easily backed by an accessible source
  • Uncontested eyewitness testimony
  • An uncontested claim by a relevant authority
  • The conclusion of another good argument
  • A minor, reasonable assumption in context
Reject a premise if it
  • Contradicts credible evidence, an established claim or a legitimate authority
  • Clashes with your own experience
  • Is questionable and undefended, or can't be defended
  • Is self-contradictory or confusingly worded
  • Rests on an unstated, highly questionable assumption

A claim like "30% of divorces are caused by marrying too young" fails because no source could measure a cause that precisely. "Dan sings well because he's in a good choir" rests on a hidden assumption: what is true of the whole is true of each part.

When is a discussion over?

Principles 11 and 12 turn the five criteria into a stopping rule. Unless you can show the winning argument fails the tests, you're obliged to accept its conclusion. That's the uncomfortable part.

Making a weak argument stronger

  • Spell out implicit premises
  • Introduce a general claim so the conclusion follows by necessity
  • Restate it in the fewest words

  • Cut anything that doesn't support the conclusion
  • Cut irrelevant premises even if your audience likes them. Someone will expose them.

Some arguments can't be saved. If good arguments for a view aren't out there, the honest move is to drop the view, unless you're a defence lawyer.

04What Is a Fallacy?

A violation of a criterion, and two ways to attack it

The word comes from the Latin for "deceive". A fallacy looks like good reasoning, which is why it works on us.

  • Damer's theory ties every fallacy, named or unnamed, to a broken criterion. That makes the subject positive (it shows what good looks like) and connected, rather than a loose list.
  • Names are like medical diagnoses. Telling a patient "you're sick" doesn't help. Knowing the pattern makes you more confident about the flaw and better at treating it. But you can judge any argument with the five criteria alone, even if you forget every name.
  • Intent doesn't matter: a mistake is a mistake. And accepting a bad argument makes it your bad argument too, just as accepting a good one makes you a good thinker.
  • Damer mostly drops the Latin. Only post hoc and ad hominem survive, because people already know them. The other names are chosen to describe the error.
  • Method 1: let it self-destruct. Put the argument in standard form and say the hidden premise out loud. "What comes before an event causes it" or "whatever is now the practice should stay the practice" often collapses as soon as it's said.
  • Method 2: the absurd counterexample. Build an argument with the same form whose conclusion is plainly false. If the copy is flawed, so is the original. It needs no jargon, so it works on people who don't care what logicians call things.

The absurd counterexample method

Both premises in the original are true, so the problem must be the form. The kite version keeps the form, also has true premises, and reaches a ridiculous conclusion. If true premises can lead to a false conclusion through this pattern, the pattern is invalid, and "Dan is a Marxist" doesn't follow either.

Rules of the fallacy game

Don't be a fallacy monger. Students who learned the names started pouncing on friends and parents, and their relationships suffered. Only raise a flaw that actually undermines the conclusion. When you're caught in a fallacy yourself, admit it and adjust; don't be a sore loser. If you can, avoid the word "fallacy" altogether. Point at the reasoning, not the label.

05Structure

Arguments that don't work as arguments

If a premise just repeats the conclusion, two claims contradict each other, or a deduction breaks a rule of logic, nothing follows, however good the content looks.

Begging the question

The conclusion is assumed somewhere in the premises, so no independent reason is offered. Look out for loaded language ("tacky", "murder") and loaded questions ("Have you stopped cheating on your taxes?").

  • Arguing in a circle
  • Question-begging language
  • Complex question
  • Question-begging definition

Inconsistency

Premises that contradict each other, or a conclusion that contradicts a premise. From A and not-A, you could "prove" anything at all, so the argument proves nothing.

  • Incompatible premises
  • Premise–conclusion contradiction

Deductive inference

Breaking a rule of conditional or syllogistic logic. These are the only fallacies that are about form alone.

  • Denying the antecedent
  • Affirming the consequent
  • False conversion
  • Undistributed middle
  • Illicit end term

Why the undistributed middle fails

"All Marxists are atheists" only describes the Marxists. It says nothing about every atheist. Since the middle term (atheists) is never covered in full, Dan can sit in the atheist circle without being a Marxist.

Conditionals: two moves that work, two that don't

// If it rains, the street is wet.
It's raining.      → The street is wet.   valid
Street isn't wet.  → It isn't raining.    valid

Street is wet.     → It's raining.        ✕ affirming the consequent
It isn't raining.  → Street isn't wet.    ✕ denying the antecedent

A street-cleaning truck makes both bad moves obvious. Damer's own counterexample for the second: if Newt is a dog, Newt is an animal. Newt isn't a dog, so Newt isn't an animal? Newt is a cat. Flipping the conditional ("if wet, then rain") is false conversion.

How to respond

For circles, put premise and conclusion next to each other. For loaded questions, refuse a yes-or-no answer and name the assumption. For a question-begging definition, ask what evidence could ever count against the claim. If nothing could, it's a definition dressed up as a fact. For contradictions, write them as A and not-A. For invalid deductions, the absurd counterexample works better than reciting rules.

06Relevance

Reasons that don't bear on the point

A premise can be true and still useless, because its truth gives no reason to believe the conclusion.

  • Two quick tests: would this premise being true make the conclusion more likely? And even if true, should it count? A film's box office record doesn't tell you whether it's good.
  • Irrelevant premises point at the wrong thing: where an idea came from (genetic fallacy), a respectable cover story (rationalization), a conclusion the evidence doesn't support, or reasons unrelated to a possibly good conclusion.
  • Irrelevant appeals use some outside pressure in place of evidence: the wrong authority, the crowd, a threat, tradition, self-interest or emotion.
  • Not every appeal to authority is bad. An authority counts when they are named, expert in this field, not biased, and their field generally agrees. A family friend testifying for the accused fails the last of these.
  • Tradition is fine when nothing else is at stake. It only becomes a fallacy when it overrides a more important principle, as when "we've always voted Democrat" decides a vote despite the evidence.

Irrelevant premise

The reasons given aren't the ones that matter. With rationalization, they aren't even the real reasons.

  • Genetic fallacy
  • Rationalization
  • Wrong conclusion
  • Wrong reasons

Irrelevant appeal

Pressure in place of evidence. All six can be very persuasive, which is exactly the danger.

  • Irrelevant authority
  • Common opinion
  • Force or threat
  • Tradition
  • Self-interest
  • Manipulation of emotions
How to respond

With the wrong reasons, agree where you can: "It may be a good idea, just not for those reasons." Then offer better ones. With a rationalizer, they're protecting their face, so say you suspect you haven't heard the real argument yet, and don't expect a confession. With emotional manipulation, the fallacy is partly yours if you let the feeling decide. Notice it, then ask for the evidence.

07Acceptability

Confusing words and hidden assumptions

You can't accept a premise you can't understand, or one that only works if you swallow an unstated assumption no rational person would accept.

Linguistic confusion

Meaning slips: a word changes sense partway through, a phrase can be read two ways, a stress or a hint does the arguing. "I didn't lie, I just stretched the truth" is a distinction without a difference.

  • Equivocation
  • Ambiguity
  • Misleading accent
  • Illicit contrast
  • Innuendo
  • Vague expression
  • Distinction without a difference

Unwarranted assumption

The premise only works with a hidden, questionable rule behind it: parts equal the whole, the middle is right, what is ought to be, wanting makes it so, only two options exist, or rules have no exceptions.

  • Continuum
  • Composition
  • Division
  • False alternatives
  • Is-ought
  • Wishful thinking
  • Misuse of a principle
  • Fallacy of the mean
  • Faulty analogy

The fallacy of the continuum: small steps still add up

Damer uses this exact counterexample against an argument that, since no single moment of pregnancy marks a sudden change, a fetus at conception is the same as at birth. Whatever you think of the conclusion, that premise doesn't hold: short and tall, an A and an F, are different even though small steps connect them. To expose it, ask for a cut-off ("how much money makes someone rich?"), take a little off, and keep going.

How to respond

With linguistic confusion, ask what the word means here, and don't be put off by "you're being picky". You can't judge a claim you don't understand. With innuendo, say the hinted claim out loud and ask them to defend it. With unwarranted assumptions, state the hidden rule and test it: a counter-analogy against a faulty analogy, a third option against false alternatives, and for the middle position, ask that it stand on its own merits.

08Sufficiency

Too little evidence, or the wrong causal story

The reasons may be relevant and acceptable, and still not be enough to carry the conclusion.

Missing evidence

Too few cases, a skewed sample, no evidence at all, a "what if" treated as fact, a proverb in place of data, a double standard, or the decisive fact left out.

  • Insufficient sample
  • Unrepresentative data
  • Arguing from ignorance
  • Contrary-to-fact
  • Popular wisdom
  • Special pleading
  • Omitted key evidence

Causal fallacies

Claiming a cause without enough evidence: confusing what's necessary with what's enough, timing with causation, effect with cause, or missing the factor behind both.

  • Necessary vs. sufficient
  • Oversimplification
  • Post hoc
  • Cause–effect confusion
  • Common cause
  • Domino
  • Gambler's fallacy

Four ways to misread a correlation

Post hoc
  • A, then B, so A caused B
  • Three months after the wedding he started smoking pot, so his wife started him on it.
Reversed
  • B actually causes A
  • Waiters who get fewer tips are irritable. More likely, irritable waiters get fewer tips.
Common cause
  • C causes both A and B
  • A big vocabulary and business success may both come from something else, like education.
Oversimplified
  • A is one factor among many
  • Going out without a hat doesn't give you a cold unless the virus is already there.

Absence of evidence isn't evidence

"It's true unless you can prove otherwise" turns the burden of proof upside down. By that logic, your great-grandfather died of something exotic unless someone can show he didn't. Claims too odd to bother refuting would win by default.

Damer's advice is to play the same game. Make an equally unsupported claim and ask them to disprove it. For proverbs, reply with an opposite one, such as "look before you leap" versus "he who hesitates is lost". Now both sides need evidence.

For special pleading, name the double standard. Everyone outside a logic class knows that's unfair.

How to respond

Single-case generalizations usually come from a vivid personal experience, so respect the experience and point out that it isn't yet an argument. For domino arguments, ask for evidence for each link, or answer with your own absurd chain (credit card → debt → repossession → ruin). For the gambler's fallacy, remember that a run of bad seasons tells you nothing about the next one.

09Rebuttal

Ducking the other side

The most neglected criterion. These fallacies avoid, deny, divert from or discredit criticism instead of answering it.

Counterevidence

Pretending inconvenient evidence isn't there, or waving it away without engaging.

  • Denying it
  • Ignoring it

Ad hominem

Going after the critic instead of the criticism: insults, suspect motives, or "you do it too".

  • Abusive ad hominem
  • Poisoning the well
  • Two wrongs

Diversion

Moving the fight somewhere easier: a distorted version, a minor point, a side issue, a laugh.

  • Straw man
  • Trivial objections
  • Red herring
  • Humor or ridicule

Answering a criticism vs. dodging it

A real rebuttal
  • Restates the objection fairly, ideally in its strongest form
  • Shows why it doesn't damage the argument, or concedes and adjusts
  • Says what evidence would change your mind
  • Keeps attention on the main issue
A dodge
  • "That study's nonsense" (denying the counterevidence)
  • "Look who's talking" (two wrongs)
  • "So you want no rules at all?" (straw man)
  • "But what about the faculty?" (red herring)

Ask someone denying counterevidence what evidence would weaken their claim. If they can't name anything, stop arguing that point. You won't get anywhere.

How to respond

When attacked personally, don't hit back. Point out the abuse and ask, "But what do you think of my argument?" If the well has been poisoned against you, say so plainly, then invite them to judge the argument anyway. If the "you do it too" charge is true, admit it, then insist that your inconsistency doesn't make the criticism wrong. With a straw man, summarize your own argument again or ask them to. Enjoy a genuinely funny joke, then put the point back on the table.

10Writing the Argumentative Essay

Five steps from research to resolution

The essay is the five criteria put to work: one section for the case, and a whole section for the other side.

The outline
  1. Research the issueLook at every side. The goal is to find the most defensible position, not to defend the one you started with. You may end up dropping it. Keep an "idea file" organized by the five parts below.
  2. State your positionSay why the issue matters, then state your position early, like a prosecutor's opening. Be precise, define key terms, qualify it with the exceptions you accept, and don't promise more than it can deliver.
  3. Argue for itOne paragraph per premise, the strongest evidence first, no colourful extras. Go deductive if you can. For a moral or aesthetic judgment, include the value premise.
  4. Rebut the objectionsThe most neglected part of any argument. If you can't answer the serious criticisms, you probably shouldn't hold the position. Also show where rival positions fall short.
  5. Resolve the issueShow how your position settles the question or solves the problem, and how it meets all five criteria better than the alternatives.

Write for an intelligent reader who isn't in your field, like a fellow student rather than the professor. Explain any term a high-school graduate wouldn't know.

The book ends with a short sample essay on whether a married woman should keep her own name. It's a deliberately everyday issue, and it's built with exactly this structure.

Almost anyone can find premises for a view. A good argument also has to answer the people who doubt it.

What to keep

The toolkit

Damer's goal is less about winning arguments than about a world where good reasoning is normal. These are the habits that get you there.

Before you argue

Admit you might be wrong, firstIt's the best way to start a discussion. Others tend to follow, and if they won't, you know the talk is futile.
Separate the issueDeal with one question at a time. Treat "it's just semantics" as a reason to clarify, not to stop.
Take the burdenIf you make the claim, be ready to support it. If you can't, use the agnostic option: "I have no reason to believe it."
Ask for an argument, not an opinion"Why do you think so?" turns a stated view into something you can evaluate together.

Testing an argument

Put it in standard formPremises, subpremises, [hidden premises], rebuttal, conclusion. Four or five premises is usually the core.
Be charitableRebuild the strongest version the arguer meant, and let them correct it before you criticize.
Run the five tests in orderStructure, relevance, acceptability, sufficiency, rebuttal. No need to check acceptability if the premise is irrelevant.
Find the hidden premiseMost fallacies live in an unstated assumption. Say it out loud.

Answering a bad one

Let it self-destructJust reconstruct it plainly. Often the arguer withdraws the premise on their own.
Use an absurd counterexampleSame form, ridiculous conclusion. No jargon needed.
Skip the word "fallacy"Point at the pattern, not the label. Only raise flaws that affect the conclusion.
Concede when caughtAdmit your own fallacies and adjust. Being a gracious loser keeps the search for truth going.
