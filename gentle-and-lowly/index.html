Gentle andLowly

The heart of Christ for sinners and sufferers

Not what Jesus did for you, but how he feels about you, especially on the days you have failed again. Ortlund's answer, built from the Bible and the Puritans: more tenderly than you dare to think.

23 short chapters1 verse at the centrePuritan guidesfor normal Christians
The big idea

The one time Jesus describes his own heart

Across eighty-nine chapters of the four Gospels, Ortlund points out, there is only one place where Jesus tells us what his heart is like. He doesn't say austere, demanding or even joyful. He says gentle and lowly, in the invitation to the weary in Matthew 11:28–30.

The book is written for people running on fumes: Christians who know God loves them in general but suspect he is tired of them in particular. Who fear they have disappointed him beyond repair, or who read their pain as proof that he is stingy.

Ortlund's claim is that our natural picture of God is too small and too cold. We project our own impatience onto him. The Bible keeps surprising us the other way. Christ's tenderness is not a mood that comes and goes, but who he is, what flows out of him most freely when he meets sinners and sufferers.

He leans on old guides: Thomas Goodwin, John Owen, Richard Sibbes, John Bunyan and Jonathan Edwards. The Puritans would take a single verse and wring it dry for hundreds of pages, and that's the method here: one text per chapter, slowly.

What we expect, and what he says

"Gentle" means meek, not harsh or easily exasperated. "Lowly" means accessible, the word for someone humble and low to the ground. In the Bible, Ortlund stresses, the heart is not the emotions alone but the motivational centre of a person: what gets you up in the morning.

Part one · Chapters 1–12

His heart then, his heart now

What Jesus claimed about himself, he proved on the road. And because he is still human in heaven, the Gospels are not a memory of how he once was. They are a window onto how he is toward you today.

1–3The Gospels

Compassion is his reflex

He doesn't have to work himself up to mercy. It tumbles out of him before people even finish asking.

  • When a leper says "if you will", Jesus touches him at once. The word for "will" means desire: the leper was asking what Jesus most wants, and the answer was to heal him.
  • When friends lower a paralysed man, Jesus speaks comfort before anyone asks for anything. When he sees the crowds harassed and helpless, pity ignites, and he teaches, heals and feeds them.
  • The Gospel word for compassion literally means guts or bowels: what rises from someone's innermost core. And both times Jesus weeps, it is for others, never for himself.
  • The surprise of chapter 3: mercy makes Christ happy. Following Goodwin, his joy grows when people come to him for fresh forgiveness. A doctor who travelled into the jungle to cure a disease is not annoyed when the sick finally come to him. It's the reason he came.
The image

The "lowly" Jesus isn't a pushover. He drives out money-changers and confronts hypocrites. The point is where his heart goes most naturally. Harshness is the exception that takes provocation; tenderness is what flows when nobody pushes.

4–9Hebrews, John, Hosea

A priest who feels with you, and still speaks for you

The Jesus in heaven is not more distant than the one in Galilee. He is your priest, your intercessor and your advocate, and all three are about his heart.

Three things Christ does for you now

SympathizesHebrews 4:15. He was tempted in every way, so he doesn't lecture from a distance. His heart is bound up with yours in your weakness.
IntercedesHebrews 7:25. Justification is what he did back then. Intercession is what he is doing now: always living to plead for you, so his saving outpaces your sinning.
Advocates1 John 2:1. An intercessor stands between two parties; an advocate steps over to your side. Don't defend yourself. Bring the sin to the one already defending you.
  • Gentle with the wayward (Hebrews 5:2). Old Testament priests were sinners themselves, some notoriously so. Jesus knows weakness from the inside but never turned inward in self-pity, so he can deal gently with the ignorant and the stubborn. Look inside yourself and you can only expect harshness; look at him and you can only expect gentleness.
  • "I will never cast out" (John 6:37). John Bunyan, poor and uneducated, wrote a whole book on this verse. Ortlund walks through it word by word: all, not most; the Father gives, not haggles; whoever comes will never be thrown out. His heart is not a rental where you can be evicted, but your permanent home.
  • What our sins evoke (Hosea 11:8). Ortlund doesn't soften God's wrath. But for those in Christ, sin draws out his tenderness rather than pushing it away. Where sin increased, grace abounded all the more.

Where your sin sends him

What we assume
  • Each failure uses up some patience
  • He loves me, but with raised eyebrows
  • I need to clean up before coming back
  • Heaven is cooler than earth was
What the texts say
  • He is drawn toward the weak and wayward
  • Mercy is what makes him glad
  • Come as you are; he never casts out
  • He is the same heart, now interceding
10–12Beauty, emotion, friendship

A heart worth loving, a friend worth having

His tenderness is not just doctrinally true but beautiful, fully human and personal.

  • Beauty. In 1740 Jonathan Edwards preached a short sermon to the children of his church, ages one to fourteen. His first reason for loving Jesus above everything: no love is as great as the love in Christ's heart. Edwards stresses that this heart is lovely, and humans are pulled toward beauty.
  • Emotion. Jesus didn't stop being human when he ascended; he still is and always will be. So his tears at Lazarus' tomb and his anger at death show who he is now. With B. B. Warfield, Ortlund says Jesus was the most truly human person who ever lived, and his anger is on your side, against everything that hurts you.
  • Friendship. His enemies called him a friend of tax collectors and sinners as an insult. For anyone who knows they are a sinner, it's the best news there is. Sinners enjoyed being around him, and he enjoyed being with them. Ortlund also notes how thin friendship has become, especially between men.
Part two · Chapters 13–18

Not a gentle Son and a stern Father

A common hidden belief: Jesus is kind, but the Father is the one you have to watch out for. Ortlund takes this apart, first through the Spirit and the Father, then through the Old Testament.

13–14Spirit and Father

The Spirit makes it felt; the Father shares it

Christ in the flesh shows what is in the heart of all three persons of God.

  • The Spirit's role, added to the familiar list. He regenerates, convicts, gifts, leads and intercedes. Ortlund adds: he makes us actually feel Christ's heart. Being told your father loves you is one thing. Being swept up in his arms is another. The Spirit moves us from the postcard to the beach, and he does it again and again whenever we drift.
  • The Father of mercies (2 Corinthians 1:3). At the cross, the Father's justice had to be satisfied. But that's a legal reality, not a difference of heart. Subjectively, the Father wanted our rescue as much as the Son did. He is the spring of mercy, as naturally as a father fathers.
  • Ortlund quotes Tozer: what comes into our minds when we think about God is the most important thing about us. This book is an attempt to correct that picture.
15–18The Old Testament

Jesus adds sharpness, not new content

The gentle and lowly Christ is where the Old Testament was heading all along. He is the flesh-and-blood version of what God had been saying for centuries.

Exodus 34: the shape of God's character

When Moses asks to see God's glory, God doesn't show size or thunder. He proclaims his name: merciful, gracious, slow to anger, abounding in steadfast love. The prophets quote this "credo" again and again.

His natural work and his strange work

Natural
  • Mercy, compassion, pardon
  • Flows from his heart
  • What he delights in
Strange
  • Judgment, affliction
  • Real and just
  • "Not from his heart" (Lam 3:33)

Lamentations has five carefully built poems; the middle chapter is three times as long, and its literary centre is 3:33: God does not afflict from his heart. Goodwin and Isaiah 28 call judgment God's "strange work".

  • Isaiah 55. "My ways are not your ways" is usually quoted with a shrug about mysterious providence. In context it means something else: God pardons abundantly, far beyond how we would forgive. The surprise is his compassion.
  • Jeremiah 31:20. After twenty-nine chapters of indictment, God calls Israel his dear son, his darling child, and says his heart yearns for him. The old translation says his "bowels are troubled". Even when he speaks against his people, he remembers them.
Part three · Chapters 19–23

Loved then, loved now, loved forever

The last chapters deal with the fear that God forgave the old sins but is quietly running out of patience with the new ones.

19–23Ephesians, Galatians, Romans, John

Live from his heart, not for it

The battle of the Christian life is to replace an orphan mindset with the settled fact of adoption, every morning.

  • Rich in mercy. Ephesians 2: we were dead, God made us alive, and in between stands the reason: God is rich in mercy. The Christian life is a lifelong shedding of tepid thoughts about his goodness.
  • Law-ish hearts. A twelve-year-old in a loving family starts forging birth certificates and scrubbing the kitchen to secure his place. His father would say: calm down, you're already ours. That is how we often relate to God.
  • He loved us then, he'll love us now (Romans 5). If he loved us when we were his enemies, he won't love us less now that we're his children. Many believers secretly picture a flustered, disappointed love. That's projection again.
  • To the end (John 13:1). We love until we're betrayed. Jesus knew the betrayal was coming and walked over the cliff of the cross anyway, without holding anything back.
  • Forever (Ephesians 2:7). In the coming ages God will show the riches of his kindness. His heart for us is not a passing phase but our eternal home.

Epilogue · What now?

Don't apply it. Bask in it.

Ortlund says asking "how do I apply this?" almost misses the point, like someone from a cold country winning a holiday in the sun and asking how to apply the beach. There's only one thing to do: come to him. Here are ways to keep doing that.

When you fail

Go straight to himDon't wait until you feel cleaned up. Your failure is the occasion, not the obstacle.
Picture the advocateHe's already on your side of the courtroom. Raise no defence; bring the sin to him.
Watch for projectionAsk: is this God's impatience, or mine painted on him?

When you suffer

He feels it with youHebrews 4:15: no pep talk from heaven, but a friend who can't keep his distance.
Read the tearsJohn 11 at Lazarus' tomb: he weeps with you and is angry at what hurts you.
Ask the SpiritPray to feel, not just know, his heart for you.
