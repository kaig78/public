Gentle andLowly

The heart of Christ for sinners and sufferers

Not what Jesus did for you, but who he is toward you, especially on the days you have failed again. Ortlund's answer, built from the Bible and the Puritans: more tenderly than you dare to think.

23 short chapters1 verse at the centrePuritan guidesfor normal Christians
The big idea

The one time Jesus describes his own heart

Ortlund's father pointed out to him something Spurgeon had noticed long before: across eighty-nine chapters of the four Gospels, there is only one place where Jesus tells us what his heart is like. He doesn't say austere and demanding, or exalted and dignified, or even joyful and generous. He says gentle and lowly, in the invitation to the weary in Matthew 11:28–30.

The book is written for the discouraged and the cynical, for people running on fumes whose Christian life feels like running up a down escalator. For those who know God loves them in general but suspect he is quietly tired of them in particular. In other words, Ortlund says, for normal Christians.

It isn't about what Christ has done; plenty of good books cover the cross. It's about who he is. A wife can list her husband's height, job and hobbies, but that isn't the same as describing the look he gives her across the table after years of marriage. This book is about that look.

The method is Puritan. Thomas Goodwin, John Owen, Richard Sibbes and John Bunyan would take a single verse and wring it dry for hundreds of pages. Ortlund calls them channels, not sources: the Bible is the source. Each chapter takes one text or one old teacher and turns the "diamond" of Christ's heart a little further.

What we expect, and what he says

Ortlund's recurring diagnosis: our natural intuition can only give us a God like us. We assume the rich look down on the poor and the beautiful avoid the ugly, so the holy must hold his nose around sinners. That's why we need a Bible.

Part one · Chapters 1–3

What he says, what he does

Jesus claims a gentle heart in Matthew 11, proves it on every road in the Gospels, and, Ortlund adds, is made happy by acting on it.

1His Very Heart

Gentle, lowly, and kind

In the Bible, the heart isn't just feelings. It's the motivation headquarters, what gets you out of bed. At Jesus' centre we find gentleness.

  • "Gentle". The same Greek word appears for the meek who inherit the earth, the king who comes humble on a donkey, and the "gentle and quiet spirit" in 1 Peter. Jesus is not harsh, reactive or easily exasperated. His natural posture is open arms, not a pointed finger.
  • "Lowly". In the New Testament this word usually means low in circumstance, the socially unimpressive guests a host cringes to see. Applied to Jesus it means accessible. No prerequisites, no hoops.
  • Your burden qualifies you. "All who labour and are heavy laden": whether you're straining to force your life into shape or weighed down by things outside your control, you don't unburden yourself first. Rest is a gift, not a transaction.
  • A kind yoke. The word for "easy" also means kind, as in "be kind to one another". A yoke of kindness is a non-yoke. Refusing it is like a drowning man refusing the "burden" of a life jacket.
  • Not mushy. Just before these verses Jesus pronounces woes on unrepentant towns. Gentleness is who he is toward those who come to him. For them he can no more un-gentle himself than you can change your eye colour.
  • We picture Jesus touching sinners like a small boy touching a slug: face screwed up, arm stretched, quick yelp, hand withdrawn. Goodwin says we imagine him "severe and sour"; Jesus says gentleness is his nature.
2His Heart in Action

Compassion is his reflex

What he is, he does. When Jesus meets the fallenness of the world, his most natural instinct is to move toward it, not away.

  • A leper says "if you will"; the word means desire, and Jesus answers "I will" while touching him. With the paralysed man he speaks comfort before anyone asks. Seeing crowds "harassed and helpless", pity ignites, and he teaches, heals and feeds them.
  • The Gospel word for compassion literally means guts: what rises from someone's core. The two times Jesus weeps, it's for others: Jerusalem and Lazarus.
  • Clean and unclean. Under the old law, touching something unclean made the clean person unclean. Jesus reverses it: when the Clean One touches a leper, the leper becomes clean.
  • Jürgen Moltmann: miracles don't interrupt the natural order, they restore it; sickness and death are the real interruption. Goodwin: Christ is "love covered over in flesh". Pull back the skin of the Terminator and you find a machine; pull it back on Christ and you find love.
  • Hebrews 13:8: he is the same today, now through his Spirit, closer to us than he was to the people he touched in Galilee.

What about his harsher side?

1 · Not a seesawWrath and mercy rise and fall together. The more you feel his anger at evil, the more you feel his mercy.
2 · A different questionHis heart isn't one attribute among others. It asks what flows out of him most naturally.
3 · Biblical over balancedIf the Bible's portrait leans toward tenderness, lean with it. Better biblical than artificially "balanced".

Ortlund quotes J. I. Packer: a half-truth posing as the whole truth becomes an untruth. He also notes that our background (rules-heavy, chaotic or abusive) shapes which side of Jesus we are drawn to.

3The Happiness of Christ

Mercy makes him glad

Goodwin: Christ's own joy and comfort are increased by pardoning and comforting his people.

  • A wealthy doctor flies his equipment into the jungle to cure a contagious disease. The sick refuse, wanting to heal on their own terms, until a few step forward. What does he feel? Joy. It's why he came. How much more if they were his own family.
  • Goodwin goes further: Christ gets more comfort than we do when we come, like a husband more relieved by his wife's healing than by his own.
  • Hebrews 12:2: "for the joy set before him he endured the cross". The joy was seeing his people forgiven. When you come for mercy, you go with the flow of his deepest wishes, not against them.
  • Should we draw on his mercy in a measured, reasonable way? Would a father want his suffocating child to use the oxygen tank sparingly?
  • We are his body, and a head nourishes and cherishes its own flesh. That's why the risen Christ asks Paul, "Why are you persecuting me?"
Part two · Chapters 4–12

His heart in heaven now

Goodwin's whole book argued one thing: the risen Christ in glory is no less approachable than the man in Galilee. Ortlund follows him through Hebrews, John and 1 John.

4Able to Sympathize

A doctor who has had the same disease

Goodwin chose Hebrews 4:15 because it lets us lay our hands on Christ's chest and feel his heart beat for us, like a stethoscope.

  • Our intuition says Jesus is with us when life goes well. The verse says the opposite: he co-suffers with us in our weaknesses.
  • He was tested "in every respect as we are". Not Zeus, not Superman: he woke with bed head, had spots at thirteen, would never have made a fitness magazine cover. He knew hunger, rejection, betrayal and loneliness. Every follower would have unfriended him at thirty-three; he will never unfriend us.
  • Push equally on "in every respect" and "without sin". Our sin is like a blue tint on everything we do; nothing he did had any blue in it.
  • Can a sinless man understand our sin? Two answers. C. S. Lewis: a man walking against the wind who lies down never learns how strong it gets; Jesus never lay down, so he knows temptation's full strength. And only someone sinless can climb into the hole and pull us out.
  • When a relationship sours, a friend lets you down, the impressive people laugh at you: there he sits close. He never lobs down a pep talk from heaven.
5He Can Deal Gently

With all who come, whatever they did

Hebrews 4:15 tells us what he feels; 5:2 tells us how he handles us: gently, with restrained passion.

  • In Israel the king represented God to the people; the priest represented the people to God. The old priests were sinners, some notoriously so. Jesus is the priest they all pointed to.
  • The Greek metriopathein, used only here, means moderated passion. He doesn't throw up his hands.
  • "The ignorant and wayward" isn't a category of mild sinners. Numbers 15 distinguishes accidental sins from sins "with a high hand"; the two words cover everyone. What draws his tenderness isn't how small your sin is, but whether you come.
  • He doesn't scold the way many of our parents did, and not because he takes sin lightly. He sees far more of it than the tip of the iceberg we see.
  • John Owen spent almost twenty years writing seven volumes on Hebrews. His image: a nursing father doesn't cast away a crying baby.
  • Look at your sin, and you can't see how you could be safe. Look at this high priest, and you can't see how you could be in danger.
6I Will Never Cast Out

"But I…" "I will never cast out."

John Bunyan, poor and uneducated, wrote a whole book on John 6:37: Come and Welcome to Jesus Christ (1678).

  • Word by word: All, not most. The Father gives; it isn't a kind Son calming down an angry Father. Gives, not haggles. Will come: his purpose is never thwarted. Whoever: not robots; grace turns our desires. To me: to a person, not a set of doctrines or a church.
  • The Greek piles up two negatives: "I will not, not cast out." Bunyan says it was worded that strongly because we are so prone to fear rejection.

Bunyan's list of objections, and the single answer

"But I…"Christ
am a great sinner→I will never cast out
am an old, hard-hearted sinner→I will never cast out
have backslidden→I will never cast out
sinned against light and mercy→I will never cast out
have nothing good to bring→I will never cast out

Ortlund imagines the modern version: "I've really messed up." I know. "It's not just my past, it's my present." I understand. "My offences are against you." Then I'm the one most suited to forgive them.

  • Every human friend has a limit. With Christ, our sins are the very résumé items that qualify us.
  • It's not "whoever comes with enough contrition" or "with redoubled effort". Ortlund's two-year-old son grips his hand walking into a pool, but soon it's the father holding the boy.
  • More than the "perseverance of the saints": the perseverance of Christ's heart. Not only decree, but desire. His heart isn't a rental where you're a tenant; you're a child. It isn't a ticking time bomb.
7What Our Sins Evoke

Sin draws out his pity

For those outside Christ, sin draws wrath. For those in Christ, it draws holy longing and tenderness.

  • Ortlund doesn't soften hell. Martyn Lloyd-Jones: we can't feel our own sin because sin builds a defence mechanism; only a glimpse of God shows it to us.
  • There is no "thing" called grace, stored up and dispensed. Grace is Christ himself coming to us.
  • The purer a heart, the more it is horrified by evil, and the more it is moved to help. A corrupt heart sits still and indifferent.
  • Goodwin: a father hates the loathsome disease, not the child, and the disease makes him pity the child more. Discipline exists, but it's physical therapy, not punishment.
  • Hosea 11. Israel is "bent on turning away", yet God says his heart recoils within him: how can I give you up? "I am God and not a man… and I will not come in wrath." We expect "therefore I will come in wrath". His holiness is the very reason he won't.
  • It isn't our loveliness that wins his love. It's our unloveliness.
8To the Uttermost

Jesus is praying for you right now

Justification is what Christ did. Intercession is what he is doing. Ortlund calls it one of the most neglected doctrines in the church.

  • Intercession doesn't mean the cross was unfinished. It applies what the cross achieved, "the constant hitting refresh of our justification" in heaven's court.
  • Nor does it mean the Father is reluctant. The Son intercedes because his heart is full, and the Father delights to say yes. Picture an older brother screaming encouragement at a race even though his little brother is already winning.
  • "To the uttermost" (panteles) appears elsewhere only for a woman bent double for eighteen years. To-the-uttermost sinners need a to-the-uttermost Saviour. We tend to read it as "save for the most part" and quietly add our own contribution.
  • We all have one dark pocket we doubt forgiveness reaches. Those crevices are where he loves us most.
  • Like a plane towing a glider, he never releases us to glide the rest of the way. Louis Berkhof: he prays for us even when our own prayer life is poor. What if you heard him praying for you in the next room?
9An Advocate

Raise no defence

An intercessor stands between two parties. An advocate crosses over and stands on your side.

Three things Christ does for you now

SympathizesHebrews 4:15. He feels your weakness with you, from the inside.
IntercedesHebrews 7:25. Always, for our sinfulness in general. Bunyan: as priest he goes before.
Advocates1 John 2:1. As occasion requires, for specific sins. Bunyan: as advocate he comes after, a reserve who stands up when we've fallen.
  • 1 John says "so that you may not sin". Grace isn't morally indifferent. But a command alone would crush us. We need Christ as friend, not only king; beside us, not only over us.
  • Parakletos is used five times, four of them for the Holy Spirit. Helper, Counsellor, Comforter: "advocate" comes closest.
  • He is for "anyone"; "we have" him now; and he is "righteous", which we are not. Even our repentance needs forgiving.
  • Ortlund names the dark pockets: the drinking, the lost temper, the shady finances, the people-pleasing that's really fear, the resentment, the pornography. Who is Jesus in the middle of it, not after? Bunyan: Satan had the first word, but Christ the last.
  • We are natural self-advocates; children make excuses without being taught. What if you never had to defend yourself again? Bunyan: lay your hand on your mouth and be silent.
10The Beauty of His Heart

Lovely, not only true

In August 1740, Jonathan Edwards preached to the children of his church, ages one to fourteen. His first reason to love Jesus above all: no love is as great as the love in his heart.

  • Edwards: parents' kindness is nothing like Christ's; it's a candle next to the sun.
  • What wins hearts isn't God's greatness, which may overwhelm but leaves our resistance intact. It's one glimpse of his beauty, which bows the will.
  • Sound doctrine exists to preserve God's beauty, the way a good lens exists to capture what you photograph.
  • "Romance the heart of Jesus": make unhurried quiet to ponder who he is. Older saints may have got where they are by being won over, feeling his love and not just knowing it.
  • For parents: the goal is that children leave home at eighteen unable to believe their sins and sufferings repel Christ. Ortlund says his own father didn't force this on him; he drew him in.
11The Emotional Life of Christ

Perfect compassion, perfect anger

Jesus is still human and always will be. So the feelings we see in the Gospels show who he is toward us today.

  • The Heidelberg Catechism: in Christ "we have our own flesh in heaven". The Council of Chalcedon (451) called him truly God and truly man, not a blend. Emotions aren't a product of the fall.
  • B. B. Warfield's 1912 essay on Christ's emotions: compassion is the one most often attributed to him.
  • In Bangalore, Ortlund saw a leper sitting in a cardboard box, fingers eaten away, and felt only tepid compassion. Fallen emotions underreact as well as overreact. What would unfiltered compassion feel like?
  • Anger. A morally perfect being can't be indifferent to evil. The father who loves his daughter most is the angriest when she's mistreated. Jesus' woes on the Pharisees came from love for the people they burdened. He wove the whip for the temple himself; the money-changers were the ones really overturning things.
  • At Lazarus' tomb, Warfield reads the Greek as rage, at death and at the one who holds its power.
  • Are you angry about a wrong done to you? He is angry with you, more than you, and without sin. Let him be angry on your behalf, release your debtor, and breathe again.
12A Tender Friend

A friend with no limit

His enemies called him "a friend of tax collectors and sinners" as an insult. It's only an insult if you don't count yourself among them.

  • Friendship, especially between men, has thinned out; Ortlund cites research showing richer male friendship in colonial America.
  • Picture your relationships as rings around a bullseye. Some of us have no one at the centre, no one safe enough to tell everything. Even in good marriages people aren't "naked in soul".
  • Revelation 3:20 is addressed to Christians called wretched, poor, blind and naked: I stand at the door and knock, and will eat with them. With a good friend, you can just sit together without filling the silence.
  • John 15:15: friends are those he tells everything. Sibbes lists the marks of friendship as mutual consent, sympathy, freedom to share secrets, comfort and honour. "As he is our friend, so he is our king."
  • Human friendship still matters; everyone gets lonely, introverts too. His friendship doesn't remove the ache, but it takes out the sting. He won't even welcome us coolly.
Part three · Chapters 13–14

Not a gentle Son and a stern Father

Goodwin: the Son, manifest in the flesh, expresses what was in the heart of all three.

13Why the Spirit?

From postcard to beach

To the familiar list of what the Spirit does, Ortlund adds one: he makes us actually feel Christ's heart.

  • The Spirit regenerates, convicts, gifts, leads, bears fruit, kills sin, prays for us and transforms us. He also moves Christ's heart from heard to seen, to felt, to enjoyed. A child told he's loved, versus a child swept up in a hug.
  • John 16: "it is to your advantage that I go away". The disciples' hearts were full of sorrow; the Spirit is how Jesus leaves bodily but leaves his heart behind. Goodwin: the Spirit will tell you nothing but stories of my love.
  • He is a person who can be grieved. He never exaggerates Christ's love; like binoculars at a ballgame, he just brings it closer to its true size.
  • He does this once at conversion, and ten thousand times afterward, whenever sin, folly or boredom make us drift.
14Father of Mercies

Whoever has seen me has seen the Father

Tozer: what comes into our minds when we think about God is the most important thing about us.

  • Legally, the Father's wrath had to be satisfied. But in desire, the Father was as eager for our rescue as the Son. Theologians call their eternal agreement the covenant of redemption. The Father needed no more persuading than the Son.
  • 2 Corinthians 1:3: he "begets" mercies the way fathers beget children; there's a family resemblance. James 5:11 calls him literally "much-compassionate".
  • Goodwin pictures God's mercies as a shop full of boxes, the promises: for a hard heart, tender mercies; for a dead heart, reviving mercy; for a sick soul, healing.
  • Your earthly father, good or bad, is either a faint pointer to God or a photo negative of him.
  • John Flavel: God is far more tender toward you than you are toward yourself.
Part four · Chapters 15–18

Jesus adds sharpness, not new content

The gentle and lowly Christ is where the Old Testament was heading all along. Calvin: the Old Testament is the shadow, true but dim; the New is the substance.

15Natural and Strange Work

He does not afflict from his heart

God is sovereign over everything painful, and yet he sends affliction with a kind of divine reluctance.

The centre of Lamentations

Verse counts in the five poems of Lamentations, written after Babylon destroyed Jerusalem in 587 BC. Ortlund reads the middle verse of the middle chapter as the whole book in a nutshell.

  • The premise: God is sovereign over the stubbed toe, the poison ivy, the backstabbing friend, the chronic neck pain, the child vomiting at 2 a.m., the depression. The statement: he doesn't do it from his heart.
  • Goodwin: in punishing there is "a kind of violence done to himself"; mercy he shows with his whole heart and soul (Jeremiah 32:41). Isaiah 28 calls judgment his "strange" work.
  • Catch Ortlund off guard and grumpiness leaps out. Catch God off guard and what leaps out is blessing.
  • These men weren't soft. Edwards preached "Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God". Goodwin spoke 357 times at the Westminster Assembly, which wrote hell into its confession. They saw both, and their influence comes from having felt God's heart. Left to ourselves we flip it: mercy strange, judgment natural.
16The Lord, the Lord

His glory is his goodness

Moses asks to see God's glory. God answers: I will make all my goodness pass before you.

Exodus 34: how far each reaches

The prophets quote this passage again and again; the scholar Walter Brueggemann calls it Israel's credo. Owen notes that severity is mentioned only at the very end.

What needs provoking

God
  • "Provoked to anger": dozens of times
  • "Provoked to love": never
  • Slow to anger = literally "long of nostrils"
Us
  • Anger needs no provoking
  • Love does: "provoke one another to love" (Hebrews 10:24)
  • Short-nosed, like a snorting bull
  • The first two words God uses for himself are "merciful and gracious", just as the only two Jesus uses are "gentle and lowly". Not "exacting and precise", nor "tolerant and overlooking", nor "disappointed and frustrated".
  • Hesed, steadfast love, is covenant commitment that never throws up its hands. "Thousands" means a thousand generations: no expiry date.
  • God is not a softie; we reap what we sow. But his mercy far outruns the consequences of sin.
  • It takes many sermons and much suffering to believe this. Perhaps Satan's greatest victory in your life isn't a sin, but the dark thoughts about God's heart that lead you there.
  • When Jesus walks on the water, Mark says he "meant to pass by" the disciples, the same word used four times for God passing by Moses. Exodus 34 in 2D becomes Jesus in 3D.
17His Ways Are Not Our Ways

The surprise is his compassion

Usually quoted with a shrug about mysterious providence. In Isaiah 55 it means: he pardons far beyond how you would.

  • Seek him, return, and he will "abundantly pardon". Then verses 8–9 explain why: his thoughts aren't ours. Calvin: nothing troubles the conscience more than thinking God is like us.
  • A seven-year-old gets a birthday present and runs for his piggy bank to pay his father back. We are far more tit-for-tat than we realise.
  • A grandson shown a $100 bill decides grandpa must be rich, without knowing about the billions.
  • "As high as the heavens are above the earth" appears elsewhere only in Psalm 103:11, about steadfast love.
  • He can turn the worst parts of your past into the brightest parts of your future, but you have to bring him the mess. Isaiah 57:15: the High and Holy One lives with the lowly.
18Yearning Bowels

"Is he my darling child?"

After twenty-nine chapters of indictment, Jeremiah's "Book of Consolation" (chapters 30–33) opens God's heart.

  • Jeremiah 31:20: "Is Ephraim my dear son, my darling child?" A declaration in the form of a gentle question. "Remember" here is the opposite of forsaking, not of forgetting.
  • The word for heart here means the innards; old translations said "bowels". The verb means restless, roaring, turbulent. And "I will surely have mercy" doubles the verb for emphasis.
  • He yearns not for the curated version of you that you show online, but for the real you underneath.
  • Being stern with ourselves feels morally serious. But a "decaffeinated" view of God's heart isn't biblical.
  • Goodwin's logic: love feels pity in proportion to the beloved's misery. Sin is our greatest misery, so his love is most intense toward us in our sin, not only in our suffering.
  • At the cross, God truly "spoke against" his dear Son, so that he could "remember" us. Ortlund: repent of your small thoughts of God's heart, and let him love you.
Part five · Chapters 19–23

Loved then, loved now, loved forever

The last chapters take on the fear that God forgave the old sins but is quietly running out of patience with the new ones.

19Rich in Mercy

A billionaire in mercy

Ephesians 2: verses 1–3 are the problem (dead), verses 5–6 the solution (made alive), and verse 4 the reason: God is rich in mercy.

  • Nowhere else is God called rich in anything. "Being" rich, not becoming. Our withdrawals make his fortune grow, because mercy is who he is.
  • Goodwin: if anyone suggests God might cast off his people, he rejects it with indignation. Accusation only moves him to bless more. His love is "invincible".
  • Christ didn't come to mend the wounded, wake the sleepy or inspire the bored, but to raise the dead. Paul, a blameless Pharisee, counts himself among the dead too: you can be an immoral dead person or a moral one.
  • Titus 2:11: "grace appeared". Sibbes: Christ is pure grace clothed in our nature. You are seated with him in heaven; to undo you, he'd have to be put back in the tomb.
  • If you've been mistreated, the evidence of his mercy isn't your life but his: betrayed and abandoned in your place. If you've squandered his mercy, he pours out more.
  • Your places of deepest shame are not hotels mercy passes through, but homes it lives in. What makes you cringe most makes him hug hardest.
20Law-ish Hearts, Lavish Heart

Live from his heart, not for it

Every morning, swap the orphan mindset for the settled fact of adoption.

The parable · the twelve-year-old

A boy in a loving family starts trying to secure his place: one week he forges a new birth certificate, the next he scrubs the kitchen, the next he imitates his dad. His father says: calm down. You didn't do anything to get into this family, and you can't do anything to get out. You are our son. Period.

  • Galatians: grace isn't only the gateway into the Christian life but the pathway. John Newton, 1767: most of our complaints come from unbelief and "a legal spirit".
  • A closed heating vent on a winter day: the warmth is in the ducts, but your room stays cold. Galatians opens the vent.
  • Paul says those "of works" are under a curse. You can preach against earning God's favour from an "of works" heart. It shows up as score-keeping, fear-stuffing and control, and its root isn't childhood or personality type but a gospel deficit.
  • Clouds don't stop the sun shining. For those in Christ, the final judgment has already happened, at the cross.

Two ways to live the Christian life

For his heartFrom his heart
earning the smile of God→starting from it
orphan, trying to stay→child, already home
vent closed→vent open
"how could I, again?"→"he is glad I came back"
21He Loved Us Then

Not a flustered love

Many believers picture God loving them with slightly raised eyebrows: after all I've done, still?

  • Romans 5 says it three times: Christ died for us while we were weak, while we were sinners, while we were enemies. He didn't meet us halfway.
  • "God shows his love": he puts it beyond question. Edwards: an ocean without shores or bottom.
  • Paul's main point is "much more": if he saved us as enemies, how much more will he keep us as children? Conversion "invincibilizes" your future. Flavel: he didn't choose you because you were high; he won't forsake you because you're low.
  • Your pain over sinning is itself proof of adoption; a cold heart wouldn't care. Repent thoroughly, but reject the whisper that his heart has cooled. His deepest disappointment is with your tepid thoughts of him.
  • We will be less sinful in heaven, but not more secure. Spurgeon: you've often left him; has he ever left you?
22To the End

He came to the cliff and walked over

We love until we're betrayed, up to a limit. "Having loved his own… he loved them to the end."

  • Romans 5 says forsaking us would breach God's justice; John 13 says it would breach Christ's own heart.
  • What lay ahead, compared with his ministry so far, was like drowning compared with drizzle. A three-year-old can't grasp a spouse's betrayal; we can't grasp "the iniquity of us all" falling on one man.
  • Ortlund suspects, and flags it as speculation, that what broke him wasn't the physical torture but losing the felt love of the Father. Warfield: he died not of the cross but of a broken heart. A footnote clarifies that the Son lost the experience of the Father's love, not the love itself.
  • Richard Bauckham notes Jesus cried Psalm 22 in Aramaic, his own language: every human cry of forsakenness funnelled into his.
  • Bunyan: the King of glory sets his heart on people Scripture calls worms, shadows and vapours. His love needs no beauty in us. It isn't an arrow that falls to the ground, but an avalanche that gathers speed.
  • For his own: no exit strategy, no prenup. Bunyan: to the end of their lives, their sins, their temptations, their fears.
23Buried in His Heart

This life is the worst it will get

Edwards: God created the world so that his Son could have a bride to pour his heart out on.

  • We exist to glorify God, the way a car runs on petrol, not orange juice. One way he is glorified forever: his grace shown to sinners. Ephesians 2:7, "in the coming ages… the immeasurable riches of his grace in kindness toward us".
  • At the funeral of the missionary David Brainerd, who died in Edwards' home in 1747, Edwards said the saints will see Christ's majesty and his mildness together, and "swim in the ocean of love".
  • We will walk through the wardrobe into Narnia and never once be told, "Enjoy this, but remember you don't deserve it." Our fallenness becomes part of the glory. Even the squeaky-clean will discover the pride deep inside them.
  • "Kindness" is the same word as the "easy" yoke of Matthew 11. Not measurable, middle-class grace.
  • C. S. Lewis: heaven works backwards and turns even agony into glory. Owen: he loves life into us.
  • For those outside Christ, this life is the best it will ever be. For those in him, it's the worst.
Epilogue · What now?

Don't apply it. Bask in it.

Ortlund's answer to "how do I apply this?" is: you don't. Someone from the Arctic who wins a beach holiday doesn't ask how to apply the sun; he basks. There's one thing to do: come to him. Goodwin: he is more glad of us than we can be of him. If you knew his heart, you would.

When you fail

Go straight to himDon't clean up first. Your burden is your qualification.
Raise no defenceYour advocate is already on your side of the courtroom.
Check for projectionIs this God's impatience, or mine painted onto him?

When you suffer

He co-suffersNo pep talk from heaven, but a friend who can't keep his distance.
Let him be angryFor wrongs done to you, let his sinless anger replace yours, and release your debtor.
Ask the SpiritPray to feel his heart, not just know about it.
