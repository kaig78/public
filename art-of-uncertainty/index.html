Introduction

Uncertainty is yours, so own it

On 29 January 1918 Cecil Spiegelhalter, a gas officer near Passchendaele, was blown up on his way back from the front line. It looked like bad luck, but it kept him away from the front just before his old battalion met the German spring offensive. His grandson, the author, exists thanks to a chain of small accidents like that.

The book's big idea is that uncertainty is a relationship between someone and the world, so two well-informed people can sensibly hold different probabilities. Judgement is unavoidable, but not anything goes: probabilities can be scored against what happens.

From there he moves outwards, from chance and luck through Bayesian learning, science and blame to forecasting, disasters and decisions. Throughout he asks for slow thinking about not-knowing, and enough humility to turn Rumsfeld's "unknown unknowns" into acknowledged ones.

Part I · Chapters 1–2 · the book has no formal parts; this grouping is mine

Owning it

Uncertainty always belongs to someone. Once you accept that, you can express it as a number, and a number can be checked.

01Uncertainty is personal

Your probability, not the probability

Two people can rightly hold different probabilities for the same event, because each knows different things.

  • The covered coin. Before a flip, your uncertainty is about a future nobody can know. Once the coin is caught and hidden it is about a settled fact you happen not to know. Spiegelhalter, having peeked, holds 0 or 1 while you still say a half.
  • Its anatomy. Uncertainty is "conscious awareness of ignorance", with a subject, a checkable object, a source, an expression and often a feeling.
  • Edge-sorting. In 2012 Phil Ivey won over £7.7 million at Crockfords by having key cards turned so he could read them from their backs. The cards stayed just as random; only his knowledge changed. The Supreme Court called it cheating.
  • Not everyone wants to know. In a German survey of about 1,000 adults, 88% did not want to know when they would die. Intolerance of uncertainty is linked to anxiety and depression.
  • Denial has costs. Ministers insisted beef was safe during BSE; over 170 people later died of vCJD.
02Into numbers

"A fair chance" is not a number

Words like likely mean very different things to different people. Numbers can be checked and scored.

  • Bay of Pigs. The Joint Chiefs gave the 1961 invasion about a 30% chance. The briefing called this "a fair chance", meaning not too good, and Kennedy apparently read it as reasonable odds.
  • Words drift. Leaflets call a side effect "common" at 1–10%. Statin patients guessed "common" constipation meant 34%; the real rate is about 4%. People's median reading of "likely" is 60%, while the IPCC's "likely" means 66–100%.
  • Abbottabad. Advisers put bin Laden's presence at anywhere from 30% to 90%. Obama called it 50–50. Spiegelhalter thinks the spread itself was useful information.
  • Score the forecasters. A proper scoring rule penalises confident mistakes heavily, so stating your honest probability gives you the best expected score. Good probabilities are calibrated (70% claims come true about 70% of the time) and discriminating. Nate Silver's 28.6% for Trump in 2016 was not "wrong".
  • Foxes beat hedgehogs. In Philip Tetlock's experiments, cautious, adaptable foxes outperformed confident one-theory hedgehogs. The winning habits: aggregation, meta-cognition and humility.

Why the quiz punishes bluffing

The curves are for someone who is honestly 70% sure. The quiz rule pays 25 minus a penalty that grows with the square of the probability put on the wrong answer. Claiming exactly 7/10 then earns the most on average (+4), and bluffing 10/10 drops to −5. A rule that scores by plain distance from the answer rewards bluffing instead: +10.

Part II · Chapters 3–6

Chance

The mathematics of chance arrived late and works very well. It still can't say what probability actually is, and our intuitions about randomness are poor.

03Probability

Gamblers wrote the rules. Nobody agrees what they mean.

The whole theory rests on a few simple rules, but no instrument can measure a probability. In Spiegelhalter's view it is always a judgement.

  • Late arrival. People gambled on knucklebones for at least 5,000 years, yet nobody analysed chance until Gerolamo Cardano counted the 36 outcomes of two dice around 1550.
  • The problem of points. If a first-to-three coin game is stopped at tails-heads-tails, the leader wins three of the four possible endings. Fermat and Pascal split the stake by expected winnings, here 60 to 20 ducats. Probability trees give the rules: multiply along a branch, add across branches.
  • Counting gets huge. A deck has about 8 × 1067 orders, so a good shuffle is almost certainly new. Casanova's sums made his 1758 French lottery reliably profitable.
  • Swamped, not corrected. Bernoulli's law of large numbers pulls proportions towards the true rate. Early imbalances aren't evened out. They just get diluted. Believing an absent number is "due" is the gambler's fallacy.
  • What is it? Every "objective" definition quietly depends on judgement. Ramsey and Bruno de Finetti built probability on personal belief. De Finetti's book opens: "Probability does not exist." His exchangeability theorem still lets us act as if chances exist.
04Coincidences

What's the chance of that? Higher than you think.

Rare events are common when there are many chances for them to happen. Count the opportunities before being amazed.

  • Count the opportunities. His Cambridge collection gathered about 5,000 coincidence stories. The method: probability of the specific event times the number of chances for something similar gives an expected count. With about 13 million cohabiting couples, around 36,000 should share a birthday.
  • The e−m shortcut. If you expect m rare events, the chance of none is about e−m. 23 people form 253 pairs, so m = 0.693 and a shared birthday is slightly more likely than not. In the 2023 Women's World Cup, 16 of the 32 squads of 23 were expected to have a match. 17 did.
  • Clusters and courts. Three plane crashes in eight days in July 2014 had a 1 in 1,000 chance for that window, but about 0.59 for some such cluster within ten years. Lucia de Berk's "1 in 342 million" shrank to as little as 1 in 25.
  • Check the assumptions. Six double-yolked eggs in a box were called a 1-in-1018 event. But double yolks can be spotted and packed together; he bought a box labelled "double-yolked".

One rule for many coincidences: expect m, and P(at least one) ≈ 1 − e−m

The only input is the expected count. Hand a team's locker keys back at random and the expected number of matches is 1 whatever the team size, so the chance of at least one stays around 63%.

05Luck

Luck is chance with your name on it

The luck that matters most is the hand you were dealt at birth. After that it is chance, plus what you make of it.

  • Kinds of luck. Resultant (how things turn out), circumstantial (right or wrong place) and constitutive (who you were born as). Spiegelhalter rates the last as the biggest of all.
  • Luck you didn't know you had. NASA later estimated early Shuttle flights had about a 10% chance of disaster, and only a 6% chance of reaching the 25th launch, Challenger, without a loss.
  • Waiting times. Derren Brown's televised ten heads in a row took about 1,600 attempts at 1 in 1,024 each: a bit unlucky. With a constant chance per try, the most likely moment for success is the very first one, which is why random events cluster.
  • Luck in league tables. He gives a 67% probability that 2022–3 champions Manchester City really were the best team.
  • Making your own. "Lucky" people spot opportunities and bounce back, and serendipity like penicillin came from prepared research. For parents of children having heart surgery, "unforeseeable factors" proved the kindest name for chance.

06Randomness

Random is clumpy

Whether the world is deterministic hardly matters in practice. What matters is effective randomness, which is clumpier than we expect and very useful.

  • Chance or complexity? Software put his chance of carrying the BRCA2 gene at 33%; he didn't. Is Mendel's 50:50 true randomness or complicated mechanics? He sidesteps it: your uncertainty is the same either way.
  • Monte Carlo. Stanisław Ulam simulated Solitaire games he couldn't calculate, then applied the trick to chain reactions in the Manhattan Project. Most modern random number generators are fully deterministic, just chaotic enough to pass.
  • Lotto. After 50 UK draws ball 39 had appeared once and was "due". It caught up through dilution, not correction. On draw nine, 133 people shared a jackpot whose numbers made a neat pattern on the ticket.
  • Uses. Randomness gives fairness, balanced clinical trials and unpredictability, from penalty kicks to minefields, provided it is real: the 1969 Vietnam draft lottery took 26 of 31 December birthdays and 14 from January.

Spot the fake: real coins produce long runs

In twenty real flips there is a 78% chance of a run of four or more. Made-up sequences rarely have one, and a longest run of only two has a 2% chance. Real sequences switch about 9.5 times; people invent far more. Both strips are illustrative; the probabilities are the book's.

Part III · Chapters 7–10

Evidence

Bayes turns data into revised belief. Science adds models, which are always wrong somewhere. And blame depends on asking the right causal question.

07Being Bayesian

Update, but leave room to be fooled

Bayes' theorem turns evidence into revised belief. Keeping a small probability on "I may be wrong" lets it notice when something surprising is going on.

  • Vaccinated deaths. In June 2021 most UK Covid deaths were among the vaccinated. With rough figures (95% vaccinated, 2% risk unvaccinated, 90% protection) 19 of every 29 deaths would be. Most people killed in cars wear seatbelts too.
  • Odds form. Posterior odds = likelihood ratio × prior odds. At Bletchley Park Alan Turing added up logs of likelihood ratios by hand, a method later rediscovered as the "naive Bayes" spam filter.
  • Cromwell's rule. Following Dennis Lindley, never give anything probability 1 or 0 unless it is logically certain. It is named after Cromwell's 1650 plea to the Church of Scotland: "think it possible that you may be mistaken."
  • The Bayesian brain. Perception may work the same way, predicting from an internal model and adjusting when the senses disagree.

Needles and hay: a 1-in-1,000 error rate, yet most alerts are wrong

The Metropolitan Police's claimed rates for the 2023 coronation, applied to a crowd of 10,000 with 10 people of interest. A likelihood ratio of 700 can't overcome a prior of 10 in 10,000. Forgetting that is base-rate neglect.

08Science

Every interval assumes the model is right

Margins of error are calculated as if every assumption in the model holds. None is ever fully true, so the margins are usually too narrow.

  • Measurement admits judgement. Metrology adds judged "Type B" uncertainty to statistical "Type A". Michelson's 1879 data gave ±15.5 km/s for the speed of light; he reported ±51, yet was still 152 km/s too high. Official values of constants keep moving outside their error bars.
  • Dexamethasone. In the RECOVERY trial 29.3% of ventilated patients given the steroid died within 28 days, against 41.4% (relative risk 0.71, interval 0.58–0.86). It saved an estimated 1 million lives in nine months.
  • Read P-values gently. A P-value measures how compatible the data are with "no effect". It is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true. Sander Greenland suggests calling confidence intervals "compatibility intervals". Spiegelhalter prefers Bayesian posteriors, which here give 99.985% probability that the drug lowers mortality.
  • Many models. On 14 October 2020 twelve UK models estimated Covid's R, and many of their intervals didn't overlap. Simpler models give narrower intervals, not truer ones. Even physics' 5-sigma discoveries have been withdrawn.
  • Five remedies. State caveats prominently, test sensitivity to the assumptions, combine independent teams, add judged Type B uncertainty, and treat P-values as rough indicators.
"All models are wrong, but some are useful."
George Box, quoted in ch. 8
09Confidence

Two kinds of 50%

A probability needs a second label: how far you trust the evidence and analysis behind it.

  • Same number, different footing. "50%" for a tested coin and "50%" for which of two coins is heavier differ hugely in backing. Intelligence agencies grade their analytic confidence low, moderate or high.
  • Infected blood. For the UK inquiry his group simulated hepatitis C infections from transfusions 10,000 times: a median 26,800 infected in 1970–91 (21,300–38,800). They declared only moderate confidence, and for hepatitis B declined to give numbers at all.
  • Many scales. The IPCC, medicine's GRADE and SAGE all rate confidence; SAGE thought closing schools would cut R by 0.2–0.5, with low confidence. Whether a rating adds to a probability or replaces it is rarely clear.
Direct uncertainty
  • About the claim itself
  • A probability, interval or distribution
  • From statistical models or expert judgement
Indirect uncertainty
  • About the basis for the claim
  • Quality of data, expert agreement, gaps in information
  • A confidence or quality rating
10Blame

Effects of causes, causes of effects

"Does A raise the risk of B?" and "Did A cause this B?" are different questions. Science, courts and climate studies answer the second with very different tools.

  • HRT. Observational studies said HRT cut heart disease by about 35%; a 2002 trial found an 18% increase. They had studied different women, and timing was key.
  • Hazard is not risk. IARC put processed meat in Group 1 with smoking because both can cause cancer, not because they are equally risky. After aspartame was rated "possibly carcinogenic" in 2023, WHO advice still allowed fourteen cans of diet drink a day.
  • Attribution. Norwegian women who smoked had 14 times the lung-cancer risk of non-smokers, so about 93% of their cases are attributable to smoking. In civil courts, a relative risk above 2 implies a probability of causation above 50%. That logic won John Cookson compensation for bladder cancer after working with dyes.
  • Forensic odds. DNA evidence should be reported as a likelihood ratio. The prosecutor's fallacy flips the probability of a match given innocence into the probability of innocence given a match. In Sally Clark's case the "1 in 73 million" figure ignored that two sudden infant deaths can share a cause, and that double murder is also extremely rare. Kathryn Folbigg spent twenty years in prison before being freed in 2023.

Part IV · Chapters 11–16

The future

Prediction gets harder with distance and with human behaviour in the loop. At the far end: leave room for "something else", build resilience, communicate without spin.

11Prediction

The further out, the less the model knows

Simple models predict well in stable settings. Over longer horizons, assumptions about structure and human behaviour dominate.

  • Football. Simple Poisson goal models for the last 2008–9 Premier League weekend got nine of ten results right. A Brier penalty of 0.34 against 0.59 for a no-skill forecast is a 43% skill score. The next year he did badly.
  • Weather. In 1987 Michael Fish said no hurricane was coming. Re-run as a 50-member ensemble, over 30% of members showed hurricane-force winds.
  • Fan charts. The Bank of England shows 30%, 60% and 90% bands and leaves 10% outside them. The 25% annualised fall in early 2020 was far off the scale of its May 2018 chart, but it belonged to the 10% the Bank had left open.
  • Lifetimes. Halley's 1693 life table priced annuities by age. Annual death risk roughly doubles every eight years; girls born in 2020 are projected to live to 90.3 on average.
  • Climate. The IPCC downgrades its multi-model 90% ranges to 66%. Modeller David Stainforth prefers robust, flexible responses to ever-bigger models.
12Risk

It's the tails that sink ships

Disasters live in the extremes of a distribution. Models built for typical cases badly underestimate them.

  • MV Derbyshire. The 90,000-ton carrier vanished in a typhoon in 1980 and 44 people died. Fat-tailed extreme value models showed a hatch-crushing wave was very likely once it was damaged.
  • Thin tails, false comfort. In 2007 a Goldman Sachs executive reported 25-standard-deviation moves several days running. Under a normal curve each is about a 1 in 10135 event, so the model was wrong. Wealth and terrorist casualties follow fat-tailed power laws.
  • F-N curves. Rasmussen's 1975 nuclear study plotted accident frequency against deaths, putting a 1,000-death accident across 100 plants at about 1 in 1,000,000 a year. Three Mile Island followed in 1979.
  • Risk registers. The UK plans for "reasonable worst cases". Swine flu's 65,000 planning figure was reported as a forecast; 460 died. The 2017 register gave emerging diseases up to 100 deaths; Covid-19 was on over 230,000 UK death certificates by 2023's end.
  • Fair criticisms. Risk analysis can be spuriously precise (a cable car risk of 1 in 15,397,000 years) and narrow. He still thinks it vital, limits acknowledged.
13Deep uncertainty

Leave a slot for "something else"

When we can't list what might happen, the answer is humility, diverse imagination and resilience, plus some probability set aside for the unlisted.

  • Two kinds of surprise. A perfect storm is a familiar event from far out in the tail, like Fukushima's 15-metre tsunami against a 5.5-metre wall. A black swan is something nobody had considered.
  • Meta-ignorance. Worst is not knowing that you don't know; next is assuming your model captured everything. Our concepts themselves are a map, not the territory.
  • Against Knight and Keynes. He rejects the split between measurable "risk" and unmeasurable "uncertainty": well-defined outcomes can still get judged probabilities.
  • Scenarios. Use red teams, reasoning backwards from a feared end state, even commissioned science fiction, and watch for people's reactions to new situations.

14Communication

Aim to be trustworthy, not trusted

Honest communication shows the uncertainty. The evidence suggests this doesn't cost trust, and balanced messages win over sceptics.

  • Over- and understating. Caveats on Iraq's weapons vanished from public reports before the 2003 war; tobacco's "merchants of doubt" manufactured uncertainty instead.
  • The Krebs list. In a crisis, say what we know, what we don't know, what we're doing to find out, what you can do meanwhile, and that the advice will change. Politicians find the second and fifth points hardest.
  • Absolute and relative risk. Heavy TV viewing came with 2.5 times the risk of a fatal embolism: one extra death per 19,000 years of five-hour nights. Yet at L'Aquila in 2009 a low absolute earthquake risk was at least a hundred times normal, and a quake killed 300.
  • Formats. Keep denominators fixed, give both framings, avoid "1 in 100-year" return periods, and prefer gradient or fan-style bands to hard-edged error bars.
  • It works. Inform rather than persuade, be balanced, admit uncertainty and weak evidence, pre-empt misunderstandings. In experiments with over 1,000 people, sceptics found balanced messages on vaccines and nuclear power clearly more trustworthy.

15Decisions

Quantify what you can, then build in slack

Formal decision theory only works in "small worlds". In the large world, use numbers as far as you reasonably can, then aim for robustness.

  • Humans aren't Homo economicus. Identical game-show outcomes get opposite choices when framed as gains or losses; we buy both lottery tickets and insurance; and we prefer a bag with known odds (ambiguity aversion).
  • Four strategies. Full analysis, semi-quantified analysis, rules of thumb, or stories. He favours the second and distrusts emotionally driven narratives.
  • Tolerable risk. The Health and Safety Executive treats a 1 in 1,000,000 annual risk of death at work as broadly acceptable. It treats 1 in 1,000 as intolerable for workers and 1 in 10,000 for the public. In between, risks must be reduced as far as is reasonably practicable.
  • Burnt toast. Heavy eaters of burnt food take in about 1/160 of an acrylamide dose shown low-risk in mice, but two safety factors of 100 set the limit at a thumbnail of toast a day. Ridicule sank the 2017 campaign.
  • Precaution has costs. No Fukushima workers died in the accident, but the evacuation was followed by nearly 1,800 "disaster-related" deaths. For deep uncertainty he lists redundancy, robustness, resilience, reversibility, adaptivity, openness and balance.

16The future

Own it, number it, stay humble

The book ends with existential odds, overconfident AI, and a manifesto that pulls its lessons together.

  • Our own existence. Asking how probable our existence was makes little sense: nobody could have assessed it beforehand.
  • Doom numbers. Published odds of catastrophe range from Martin Rees's 50–50 for civilisation this century to 1 in 2 trillion for an asteroid. He reads them mostly as expressions of concern.
  • AI. He worked on uncertainty in AI in the 1980s. Today's large language models deliver everything with the same confidence, true or false. He used them while writing, and checked everything they produced. He wants AI whose stated uncertainty is calibrated.
  • The manifesto. Own your uncertainty. Use numbers that are calibrated and discriminating, and remember they are judgements. Update like a Bayesian but keep Cromwell's doubt. Treat model outputs as rough guides. Leave room for "something else". Take risks without being reckless.
Takeaways

The toolkit

Not a self-help book, it insists, but its habits travel well.

Owning it

Say "my probability"Name the subject and the object. Someone with different information can rightly disagree.
Swap words for numbers"Likely" can mean 25% or 90%. If you must use words, define them and remind people of the definitions.
Score yourselfYour 70% calls should come true about 70% of the time.
Be a foxCombine sources, change your mind, give probabilities rather than verdicts.

Weighing evidence

Count the opportunitiesBefore saying "that can't be chance", multiply the rare probability by the number of chances it had to happen.
Draw the frequency treeStart from a crowd of 10,000. Base rates decide whether an "accurate" alert is probably wrong.
Never 0, never 1Keep a small probability on "I may be mistaken" so evidence can still change your mind.
Distrust narrow intervalsThey assume the model is true. Ask for sensitivity checks, several models and a confidence rating.
Separate hazard from risk"Can cause" isn't "will, at real doses". Ask for absolute numbers.

Deciding & telling

Watch the tailsThin-tailed models make disasters look impossible.
Keep a "something else"Whatever your list of outcomes, give some probability to none of the above.
Use the Krebs fiveWhat we know, what we don't, what we're doing about it, what you can do, and that the advice will change.
Inform, don't persuadeBalanced messages earn trust from the sceptics you most need to reach.
Robust beats optimalBuild in slack, prefer moves you can reverse, and count the harms of excessive precaution too.
