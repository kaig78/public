God Is My CEO

Following God's principles in a bottom-line world

A management consultant interviews 20 leaders who put faith above the quarterly numbers, and finds that most of them ended up with better numbers too.

The big idea

The bottom line makes a poor master

Larry Julian was a sales executive whose god, in his own words, was the bottom line. He was fired, found faith, and became a consultant who runs strategy retreats. In that work he kept meeting talented people stuck on a treadmill that only speeds up. One sales team asked him to help them run it faster. He says they wanted to go down the wrong path more efficiently.

His diagnosis: most people live in two separate worlds. On Sunday there's a private faith. From Monday to Friday there are unwritten business rules. The two clash, and people are caught in the middle, asking whether they can be both ethical and profitable.

Julian's answer is yes, if you trust God's principles, have the courage to live by them and the patience to wait on His timing. Faith and results aren't opposites. The book works through the ten dilemmas he hears most often, each with two leaders who faced it, a short reflection and a discussion guide.

A ship that turns by one degree ends up hundreds of miles off its old course. Small shifts in what you trust, he argues, add up the same way.

Where he started

A president walks into a mission-statement workshop at noon, glances at the flipcharts full of "world-class service" and sums it up: the purpose is to grow revenue 15% in 11 months and 10 days. Julian uses moments like this to show what happens when profit becomes the purpose rather than the result.

You become what you believe and do

Belief→Act→Habit→Character→Destiny

Julian borrows Charles Reade's line, "sow an act and you reap a habit…", and adds a step in front: every belief generates behaviour. So the first question of the book is a diagnostic one. Does your faith define who you are at work, or do the business rules?

Chapters 1–3 · Why you work

Calling

Purpose, success and courage: what work is for, how you keep score, and what you do when the right thing costs you.

01Purpose"In all things God works for the good of those who love Him…"Romans 8:28

From earning a living to serving a purpose

Purpose drives everything else. In the companies that impress Julian most, profit is the result of the mission, not the mission itself.

IssueHow do I find meaning and purpose in my work?
SolutionFind God's calling and you find your purpose.

Bill GeorgeCEO, Medtronic

George felt called as a teenager to run a major corporation, but the calling got tangled with ego. Medtronic asked him to be CEO in 1978, 1986 and 1988; he said no each time, because a $750 million company seemed too small next to the $2 billion business he ran at Honeywell.

Driving around a Minneapolis lake in the autumn of 1988, he realised Honeywell was changing him more than he was changing it. He and his wife prayed, and he asked a friend to tell Medtronic he was available. "The moment I stepped through the doors at Medtronic, I knew I had come home."

Medtronic's mission is to restore people to full life and health. Every new employee receives a medallion that stands for it, and every holiday party, patients tell staff what the devices meant to them. At George's first party he met T.J., who had cerebral palsy and relied on a drug pump made by a division losing about $5 million a year. Instead of closing it, the team restructured it into one of the most profitable divisions. In the interview, George never mentioned Medtronic's financials. He talked about T.J.

C. William PollardChairman and CEO, ServiceMaster

ServiceMaster's lobby has a 90-foot wall carved with four objectives: honour God in all we do, help people develop, pursue excellence, grow profitably. When a shareholder complained that God didn't belong in an annual report, Pollard's reply was that God and business do mix, and the link between them is people.

As CEO he still took part in the company's "We Serve Day" and mopped hospital corridors. A relative of his wife spotted him and quietly asked whether everything was all right at home.

Meaning comes from the cause, not the task. Shirley, a hospital housekeeper for 15 years, still hums at work because she sees cleaning as keeping the doctors and nurses in business. On a $24.4 million school contract with 14,000 broken windows, ServiceMaster started by serving the existing staff. Within a year the schools were transformed and the district saved over $3 million, with the same people as before.

  • Find a home. Look for the environment that unleashes your talents, whether that means leaving or blooming where you're planted.
  • Align work with passion. Every job has dull tasks; don't let them put out the fire. Pollard turned down a better-paid law partnership to follow a mission.
  • Trust you're called to work for a purpose. Jesus never said money was wrong, only that you can't serve two masters. Priority is the point.
Ask yourself
  1. What would you do if you knew you couldn't fail?
  2. For organisations: other than making money, why do you exist?
02Success"…plans to give you hope and a future."Jeremiah 29:11

From success to significance

Success is about outcome: money, fame, possessions. Significance is about the process: what difference you make to people while you're here.

IssueHow do I define success?
SolutionExpand it from making money to making a difference.

Julian opens with a family business handover. The 70-year-old founder throws the business plan on the floor and calls his son an idiot in front of the consultant. He worked 35 years to give his son a better life and ended up a financial success and a personal failure: money, but no legacy.

Bob BufordFounder, Leadership Network; author of Halftime

In January 1987, Buford's only son Ross, 24, drowned trying to swim the Rio Grande. Months later the family found a will Ross had written the year before, ending "Adios, Ross." It gave Buford a sense of two worlds: the business world, which passes like a cloud, and the eternal one where Ross is now.

He handed over day-to-day control of his cable company and built organisations that help church leaders and people moving "from success to significance." His advice for the second half of life: don't coast mentally, don't retire into leisure, find a parallel career that combines what you're good at with what you care about. "The most important thing is to embark!"

Jerry ColangeloOwner, Phoenix Suns and Arizona Diamondbacks

A high school star with 66 scholarship offers, Colangelo ended college with injuries, no NBA draft pick and then a failed tuxedo rental business. At 26 he was unemployed with three young children. Through his wife's church he learned that "it was all me, myself and I."

Cleaning out his wallet, he found a two-year-old business card from his father-in-law and made the call. It led to helping start the Chicago Bulls, then running the new Phoenix franchise for $22,500 a year. He began with what he calls a "character loan" and the conviction that the city owed the team nothing. After a 1986 drug scandal dragged his name through the press, faith carried him; by 1999 the local paper named him Arizona's most influential sports figure of the century.

Buford's three-foot life

"Let's say our life is 3 feet long." Reason gets you through almost all of it. The last inch, the Holocaust, a son lost in a river, makes no sense, and only faith bridges that gap.

  • A sense of urgency. Life is "but a breath," so significance is a priority for today, at any age.
  • A sense of the whole of life. Seeing the bigger picture lets you live through a loss without being defined by it.
  • A sense of significance. Driven by calling rather than ego. You may not change the world, but you can affect one person; Mark's father could have started with his son.
Ask yourself
  1. If you had one more year to live, what would you do with it?
  2. What small thing could you do today that would make a difference to someone?
03Courage"Be strong and courageous…"Joshua 1:9

From the easier wrong decision to the tougher right one

Moral courage is private, between you and God. It often means quieting your own ego long enough to hear what you already know.

IssueHow do I do the right thing when I'm pressured to do otherwise?
SolutionWalk with God in courage.

Julian's own confession: his boss told him to "get some dirt" on Mike, one of his best salespeople, rumoured to use cocaine, and fire him. Julian wanted to talk to Mike and help. He took the easier wrong path, kept his job, lost the team's trust, and "lost a little piece of my soul."

Al QuieFormer Governor of Minnesota

In January 1982, with the state in a budget crisis, Quie kept telling reporters he'd run for re-election and felt a sharp pain each time he said it. A blizzard let him postpone an interview and retreat to his farm. Alone in the quiet, he saw there was no dilemma: he knew he shouldn't run. Fear of looking like a quitter and ego had stopped him.

Once he announced it, he was free, and his credibility rose. He brought the party leaders together in private, offered to take the blame for unpopular decisions, and the budget deadlock that had lasted months was resolved in three days.

Marilyn Carlson NelsonChairman and CEO, Carlson Companies

In October 1985 her 19-year-old daughter Juliet was killed in a car accident. Nelson was angry at God, then doubted He existed. She chose "better, not bitter" and kept searching. The parable of the talents became, for her, a parable about time.

Her test for every day: would you sign it? "Live each day as our signature day." She rebuilt one decision at a time, noticed small messages of hope (a sister who waited on the couch for hours after a fight), and later led the family company with a focus on people rather than on her father's fear of scarcity.

Two questions before any decision, big or small

Question 1
What is the right thing to do?

Not the easier thing, the thing that saves your job, or the thing that looks brave.

Question 2
Will I trust that God is with me in it, whatever the consequences?

Both leaders faced fear and discouragement, and both reached a point where trust outweighed the situation in front of them.

Most of life is shaped by small daily decisions. Each one moves you toward who you want to be or who God wants you to be. Power and authority, Julian concludes, come from character and from acting on your principles, not from money or position.

Ask yourself
  1. What's the easier wrong decision in front of you, and what would it cost?
  2. What's the right thing, and what would that cost?
Chapters 4–7 · How you hold up

Under pressure

Short-term demands, a work culture that keeps faith private, things you can't control, and decisions where every option hurts.

04Patience"Let us run with perseverance the race marked out for us."Hebrews 12:1

From sprinting under pressure to running with purpose

Business trains you to run the 100-yard dash. Patience is what lets you run the long race you were actually called to.

IssueHow do I avoid becoming a slave to urgent, short-term pressure?
SolutionDevelop patience to run a long race in a 100-yard-dash world.

Julian's sales team dropped a carefully built long-term plan to hit a fourth-quarter target in 45 days. They won the quarter, ignored their existing customers, and lost two of their biggest accounts to a competitor.

Tony DungyHead Coach, Tampa Bay Buccaneers

Pressure from the outside. As a rookie with mononucleosis, Dungy was told by a teammate that God wanted to see whether football was above or below Him. He put his career in God's hands. Later he was passed over for seven head-coach jobs in one year, partly because he told owners winning should be a result of doing what's right.

Hired by the Bucs in 1996, he started 0–5. He didn't blame anyone or change the plan; a player later admitted the team was waiting for him to "lose it." He didn't. By 1999 the Bucs were division champions, and the players had become role models in the community. Dungy's definition of success: doing the very best you are capable of doing.

Archie DunhamChairman and CEO, Conoco

Pressure from the inside. At 36 Dunham was an impatient middle manager. Leading a church land deal, an elderly deacon kept asking whether he was seeking God's help or trying to solve it himself. Sitting on his backyard swing, he finally handed over his career: he'd stay in hot, humid Houston for 30 years if that was the plan.

Six months later he was posted to California. He turned down a big headquarters project after prayer, and later stepped back from a Fortune 100 CEO search. Six weeks after that he was named CEO of Conoco, then steered its separation from DuPont, the largest IPO in US history at the time, keeping the company and its jobs intact.

Two kinds of race

A friend told Julian he had blinders on, like a greyhound chasing the rabbit. Dungy and Dunham relied on prayer to hold or let go of their plans, perspective to see past the immediate problem, and preparation: each round of perseverance built endurance for the next challenge. Who sets the pace, you or the quarter?

Ask yourself
  1. What's the greatest external pressure on you? The greatest internal one?
  2. What would the right pace for your work and your life look like?
05Leadership by Example"Let your light shine before men…"Matthew 5:16

From what you do to who you are

Integrating faith and work isn't about preaching at the office. It's about bringing who you are to work and letting it show in how you treat people.

IssueHow do I show my faith in a diverse, politically correct workplace?
SolutionLet who you are speak for what you believe.

Julian grew up Jewish. Walking home as a boy, a teenager asked if he was Jewish, he said yes, and got spat in the face. He learned to keep his beliefs to himself, a habit he carried into his business career after becoming a Christian.

Jeffrey H. CoorsFormer President, Adolph Coors; CEO, Graphic Packaging

After a deepened faith in 1974, Coors was zealous to do things for God, and forced things, even "forcing the peace." By 1989 he was running a dozen businesses, sitting on a dozen boards and neglecting his family. On a sabbatical he'd been told by pastors that business was a lesser calling and he'd eventually leave it.

He concluded the opposite: business was a legitimate calling. He moved "from doing for God to being with God." At Graphic Packaging that became a values statement built on three R's: respect for people, responsibility for actions and results, relationships with each other.

John D. BeckettPresident, R. W. Beckett Corporation; author of Loving Monday

Beckett took over the family burner business after his father's death and a fire in 1965. A union drive terrified him; his first labour lawyer died suddenly, and a verse about seeking counsel sent him to find another. Employees voted to stay non-union, and Beckett realised the company had neglected communication and benefits. He decided he couldn't keep living in two worlds.

His model: compassion teamed with accountability ("compassion without accountability produces sentimentalism"), investment in each person's growth, and family before work, with up to 26 weeks of supported parental leave, an adoption benefit and limits on weekend travel. The company calls itself Christ-centred and is explicit that belief has no bearing on hiring or promotion. Revenue grew from $4 million to about $100 million with roughly 75% of the North American market.

Principle 1Leadership by exampleBe salt and light. Emerson: "Who you are speaks so loudly I can hardly hear what you are saying."
Principle 2RespectDignity for every person, honouring diversity. The golden rule even helped a Minnesota company bridge cultures in Southeast Asia.
Principle 3CompassionNo policy can stop you from caring for a colleague or praying for someone. Only you can stop yourself being who you are.
Ask yourself
  1. What keeps you from expressing your faith at work?
  2. Which boundaries feel right to you when bringing faith and work together?
06Yielding Control"…whoever loses his life for me will find it."Matthew 16:25

From "surrender means defeat" to "surrender means victory"

Surrender here doesn't mean quitting. It means yielding the need to control what was never yours to control, and getting on with what is.

IssueHow do I deal with circumstances beyond my control?
SolutionDo my part, and let God do His.

In a strategy session for a fast-changing healthcare company, the operations VP, Jan, fought every new idea. The greater the uncertainty, the harder she resisted, and a very capable person became the team's biggest obstacle.

Tad PiperChairman and CEO, Piper Jaffray

The third-generation leader of a 100-year-old firm woke to a Wall Street Journal report calling the company bankrupt after a portfolio manager's leveraged derivative bets collapsed. For four months Piper tried to fix everything himself: the press, the lawyers, every unhappy client, every branch.

A colleague mailed him a sermon tape on Job. Weeks later, sleepless and exhausted, he prayed, "Lord, I just can't do it, so you have to," and remembered he wasn't alone. He'd learned the same lesson two years earlier when he admitted he was an alcoholic. He worked as hard, but calmly; he and his wife spoke openly about the strain to managers' spouses. The firm survived, grew, and was sold in 1998.

Jim SecordPresident and CEO, Lakewood Publications

His company was sold three times in 16 months and still had two of its most profitable years. A badly handled layoff in 1979 had put Secord in hospital, so this time he leaned on four things: a vision built with all 100 employees, open communication (six all-company meetings, even the sale documents shared), personal integrity (he split his $75,000 stay bonus with every full-time employee) and honest preparation for painful cuts.

At the end of the interview Julian asked about the flowers on his desk. They were from his wife, D'Arcy, recently diagnosed with Alzheimer's. After 37 years, Secord was leaving the company to be with her, calling it "a potentially magnificent journey."

My part and God's part

Leaders are accountable for what's in their power, not for what isn't. Julian suggests treating God as a partner rather than a consultant you delegate a little to, and borrows Dale Carnegie's "day-tight compartments": no control over yesterday or tomorrow, only today. Piper closed a speech with the Serenity Prayer.

Ask yourself
  1. What's out of your control but still causing you worry?
  2. What is within your control that you can do today?
07Tough Decisions"Blessed is the man who finds wisdom…"Proverbs 3:13

From giving in to discouragement to living with hope

Courage is doing the right thing; wisdom is knowing what it is. When every option is bad, there's usually no way around the dilemma, only through it.

IssueWhat do I do when I must choose between a bad solution and a worse one?
SolutionSeek God's wisdom to turn a bad problem into a good solution.

Linda Rios BrookFormer President and GM, KLGT-TV, Minneapolis

Her highest-rated, most profitable show was The Jerry Springer Show, which she hated and regularly sent back for content. Dropping it would fail her shareholders; keeping it meant airing sleaze. Springer himself flew in to tell her it was a morality play. She renewed, but wasn't at peace.

In a prayer meeting she asked who watches Springer late at night: people who need to see a life worse than theirs. She called a friend who ran a crisis counselling centre and proposed a crawl along the bottom of the screen: "NEED A FRIEND?" with a phone number. The lines jammed the first night, and over three years the show produced thousands of calls, many from hurting teenagers.

Brenda J. ScottCEO, Mobile Convention and Visitors Corporation

Julian had left Mobile, Alabama, convinced its racially split board was beyond help; a reporter had burst into the end of his retreat. Two years later he met the bureau's new CEO, an African-American woman, at a convention, and she told him business was booming.

Scott described herself on her CV as "God-based, heart-led, and people-motivated." She confronted three disruptive executives with "tough love" and legal advice, rebuilt trust by listening one-on-one, refused to sign a deal under pressure from a local official, and gave the city a shared vision. Four and a half years after the failed retreat, Julian came back to speak at a full and united Tourism Day lunch.

Finding the third road

Under pressure, people tend to fight (solve it for God), take flight or freeze with fright. Brook and Scott did three other things: they sought God's wisdom first, took responsibility for their own role in the solution, and never gave up hope. As Julian puts it, God didn't solve the problem for Brook; He used her to create the solution.

Ask yourself
  1. When you pray for wisdom about your hardest situation, what does your heart tell you?
  2. Describe the direction that feels right, and start walking.
Chapters 8–10 · How you lead others

People first

Serving employees, refusing to treat people and profit as a trade-off, and keeping the important things important before burnout does it for you.

08Servant Leadership"…whoever wants to be first must be slave to all."Mark 10:44

From getting the most out of employees to bringing out the best in them

Using people to raise profits tells them you care more about the numbers than about them. Serving them so they can serve customers works better, and it's good business.

IssueHow do I attract, retain and motivate good employees?
SolutionServe employees so they can serve others.

"Corporate's on line one." The order was to cut payroll by 25%, at a time when customer service was the declared priority. Morale, service and profits all collapsed, and head office then fired the whole executive committee, Julian included.

Jim BergesonChairman and CEO, Colle & McVoy

Bergeson removed fear: people get the authority to decide and to make mistakes without losing their jobs. When an employee made a $374,000 error and came in to resign, Bergeson asked to hear how it happened, then sent him back to work. He became one of the most loyal people in the firm.

There are no departmental sales targets; teams serve the client's interest. That trust let a client follow the agency's advice to raise a $2 million pet-medicine budget to over $10 million, which produced more than $50 million in first-year sales. The firm is employee-owned through an ESOP, and its stock rose 494% over ten years.

Horst SchulzeCEO, The Ritz-Carlton Hotel Company

"We are ladies and gentlemen serving ladies and gentlemen." Schulze demands full respect for every employee: a dishwasher matters as much as a vice president. His decision test asks whether something is good for everyone concerned. He's demanding about standards too; servant leadership isn't soft.

The system: careful selection for both skill and values fit (it alone halved turnover), leaders who work alongside staff (Schulze helps open every new hotel and trains the maids himself), over 100 hours of training per employee in the "Gold Standards," and a daily shift line-up. Turnover is 29% against more than 100% in the industry, and in 1992 Ritz-Carlton was the first hotel company to win the Baldrige Award.

Step 3Practise the four R'sResources, respect, recognition, rewards. Reinforce the behaviour, not just the result.

RespectA unique person, not a body filling a role.

Ask yourself
  1. How would you define your own leadership role?
  2. What one action will you take to improve employee satisfaction?
09Integration"Teach them… and show them the way to live."Exodus 18:20

From balancing people and profits to integrating them

A leader isn't a judge weighing people against profit. The job is to bring the two together, and not to do it alone.

IssueHow do I balance employee needs with profit obligations?
SolutionIntegrate people and profits into win-win solutions.

Ron, a senior VP, had compassionately handled hundreds of layoffs in a restructuring. Then he had to fire Fran, a young leader who'd just lost her husband and couldn't meet the company's demands. It broke him, and he resigned, worn out from deciding other people's fates alone.

Andrea RitchiePresident, Northwestern Travel Management

After losing a major account, the business case said lay off 30 people. Ritchie prayed, talked to her husband, friends and key salespeople, and asked one question: is the risk worth taking to do the right thing? She decided to keep everyone, then built the financial argument (the cost of training replacements later) and a sales plan to win back the revenue.

The owner told the staff they'd lost the account and in the same breath that everyone kept their job. She asked for a 10% productivity gain and got 24%. Turnover fell to a record low.

Ken MelroseChairman and CEO, The Toro Company

In the 1970s Melrose felt alone in caring about employees when management theory cared about profit. As CEO he rewrote Toro's philosophy from "profits first" to "people first," adopted servant leadership modelled on Jesus washing the disciples' feet, and tied a large share of senior managers' bonuses to how well they practised it.

When a seasonal slump threatened layoffs at the Shakopee plant while a product had problems in the field, Toro sent the plant's workers out to help distributors fix it. Twenty years on, turnover was less than half the national average.

Melrose's chain

Melrose's own sequence: take care of employees and remove their obstacles, and you're better placed to satisfy customers; satisfied customers bring market leadership; market leadership helps make a healthy, profitable company.

The Four Ps of a high-performing partnership

PurposeWhat common mission and shared vision are we both working toward?
PositionsWhat exactly do we expect of each other? Clarify it most in times of change.
ProcessHow will we work together to get there?
PerformanceHow will we measure success, in relationships as well as results?

Jethro's advice to an exhausted Moses is the chapter's model: you can't carry it alone. Represent the people before God, teach them the way, hand decisions to trustworthy leaders, and share responsibility for the mission.

Ask yourself
  1. Is the fate of your employees in your hands alone, or is accountability shared?
  2. How do you reward relationships as well as results?
10Priorities"Be joyful always; pray continuously; give thanks in all circumstances…"1 Thessalonians 5:16–18

From burning out to rekindling your spirit

The people who seemed least burned out weren't balancing anything. They had decided what was important and kept it that way.

IssueHow do I deal with burnout in the workplace?
SolutionKeep the important things important.

Mary works from 6 a.m. to 7:30 p.m. on weekdays, most of Saturday and half of Sunday. In tears she tells Julian it will calm down in a couple of months, and then she'll talk to her boss. Julian has heard that promise many times.

S. Truett CathyFounder, Chick-fil-A

After the first week of their 24-hour diner in 1946, Cathy and his brother had covered six 24-hour shifts between them. They decided to close on Sunday, and Chick-fil-A never opened on Sundays after that, giving up a day that brings in roughly 14% of restaurant sales. When a giant mall pressed him in 1982 and offered a church donation, he declined politely: some principles aren't negotiable.

Julian expected a busy executive. He was told Cathy was running late because of two important visitors: 13-year-old boys touring the operation. Cathy taught Sunday school to 13-year-old boys for 45 years and has 110 foster children. The company's purpose statement starts with glorifying God by being a faithful steward. To Cathy, work–family balance wasn't an issue; he was living the life he believed he was meant to live.

Robert O. Naegele Jr.Rollerblade; later owner of the Minnesota Twins

"My life has been a series of wrecks and rescues." Fear once drove Naegele: of failing, of dying. Rollerblade struggled for years because nobody knew the product. One anxious night he prayed over Philippians 4:6; two weeks later a Bears coach mocked Minneapolis's "Rollerdome" on Monday Night Football, and the marketing team jumped on it.

When he sold his share in 1995, he and his wife sent all 280 employees cheques before Christmas, over $4 million in total, based on years of service. He used the word "joy" 15 times in the interview: joy at writing the cheques, at the letters that came back, and at the national coverage. Joy, he says, is from God; exhilaration is just the thrill of the deal.

The chapter ends with Jake, a 90-year-old shoeshine man in Minneapolis, who turned around Julian's terrible day with one word when asked the secret of his life: appreciation. Julian had just come from surgeons who had everything and were complaining about time.

Ask yourself

Conclusion

A message of hope

All 20 leaders reached a defining moment where they trusted God more than their own reading of the situation, and took a leap of faith without knowing the outcome. Julian thinks most people will face the same test: business pressure, logic and fear ganging up until the easier decision makes more sense than trust.

He closes on his own story. He and his wife lost two pregnancies, and his business suffered alongside. His Bible teacher kept telling him, "Don't miss out on the privilege of your problems." The leaders in the book, he says, don't necessarily have happier lives, but better ones.

The book itself took seven years of unpaid work. By any return-on-investment test it made no sense, and he writes that he was blessed beyond measure anyway.

Workbook added to this edition

Your personal business plan

The book ends with a 15-part workbook that borrows the format of a business plan for your life. Julian calls it a framework, not a formula, and says the journey matters more than the outcome.

Three principles and a promise

Roots first: seek God's wisdom before every decision. Then growth: most plans fail, Julian thinks, from immaturity rather than lack of effort. Fruit comes in its season, which can be slow; the book's own seven years are his example. Adversity is part of the plan, not a sign it's failing.

Make today your signature day

Borrowed from Marilyn Carlson Nelson: would you put your signature on this day? Four attitudes help.

AppreciationThankfully accept all that's in the day, good or bad.
AvailabilityAvailable to God, and so to others.
AwarenessGod speaks through people and circumstances.
AlignmentEvery decision reflects His nature.

Seven reminders

  • Perspective. A bad day doesn't mean the plan is failing.
  • Purpose. It's His purpose, so stay flexible.
  • Priority. Keep the important things important.
  • Process. The process, not the outcome, honours God; let the plan evolve.
  • Patience and perseverance. His timing, not yours.
  • Peace. You're called to meaningful work, not maximum work. Rest daily.
  • Prosperity. Every day is significant even when it isn't visibly successful.

The book lists the components in this order but doesn't group them; the four headings are added here. Each component in the book has thought-provoking questions, an action plan and a worked example from Julian's own plan.

From the chapters and discussion guides

The toolkit

Each chapter ends in reflection questions rather than rules. These are the practical moves that come up again and again.

Before deciding

Ask the two courage questionsWhat's the right thing to do? Will I trust God is with me, whatever the consequences?
Ask Andrea Ritchie's question"Is the risk worth taking to do the right thing?" Then build the business case.
Retreat to listenLike Quie in the blizzard: get away from the noise long enough to hear what you already know.
Don't decide alonePray, then talk to the people who love you and the people affected.

Under pressure

Sign your dayWould you put your name on today if it stood for all of time?
Live in day-tight compartmentsDo what's yours today. Hand yesterday, tomorrow and the outcome to God.
Look for the third roadIn a lose-lose choice, ask what in the situation can be redeemed.
Count your blessings, literallyWhen stressed, list what you're thankful for. Jake's one word: appreciation.

Leading people

Make the mission bigger than moneyThen show people how their work serves it, like Medtronic's medallion and patient stories.
Remove fear of mistakesHear the whole story first. Honesty and accountability, not firing on the spot.
Use the four R's and the Four PsResources, respect, recognition, rewards; purpose, positions, process, performance.
Protect restTreat a weekly day off as a principle, not a perk, for yourself and for your people.
