The Ragamuffin's Path to God

A former Franciscan priest and recovering alcoholic argues that what fear needs is not more insight or clarity, but trust: the one thing, he says, that God most wants back from us.

The big idea

You don't need more insight. You need to trust what you already have

The book starts with a remark from Brennan Manning's spiritual director: Manning had enough insights into the faith to last him centuries, and what he lacked was trust in what he had been given. Manning spent the next two years taking his life and ministry apart and asking whether he actually trusted God. What he learned, he says, is that trusting is a ruthless act.

He explains the word in the last chapter. Ruthless means "without pity", and he uses it to mean without self-pity, because, as he puts it, "self-pity is the arch-enemy of trust." Trust here is not a feeling of certainty. It is walking into an unclear future relying on God's presence and promise, while doubts and worries keep coming.

His central claim is that a human heart that trusts it is loved pleases God more than any cathedral, symphony or painting. Moving from mistrust to trust is what he calls a second conversion, and it has to be renewed every day. The sign it is happening is a slow shift from self-rejection to self-acceptance, rooted in being accepted by Jesus.

Manning writes as a ragamuffin: someone who knows they are broken and stops pretending otherwise. He tells his own failures openly, including a relapse into drinking, and the book reads more like a series of reflections and stories than an argument.

The story that frames the book

The ethicist John Kavanaugh went to work in Calcutta hoping to find out what to do with his life, and asked Mother Teresa to pray that he would have clarity. She refused. Clarity, she told him, was the thing he was clinging to and had to let go of. She said she would pray that he would trust God instead.

Reading it for fear

Fear wants clarity. Manning offers trust instead

Fear runs through the whole book, though no chapter is named after it. Manning's diagnosis is that much of our anxiety comes from a thin, steady layer of distrust, and that demanding certainty before we move only makes it worse.

The clarity loop, and the way out

The loop is my drawing of what Manning describes in chapters 1 and 8: we try to remove the risk of trusting by demanding clarity first, and when it doesn't come we take control. His point is that clarity, reassurance and proof can't create trust or keep it going.

After ch. 1 (Kavanaugh and Mother Teresa) and ch. 8 (the spies at Canaan)

“I have never had clarity; what I have always had is trust.”
Mother Teresa to John Kavanaugh, as Manning tells it (ch. 1)

Manning does not promise that trust makes fear go away. He says plainly that there is "no trust without worry". Doubts and anxieties follow us all day and turn up in dreams. They are not voluntary, so they are not failures. What matters is whether we consent to them. When they threaten to take over, he answers them with a short, deliberate act of trust: growing still for a moment and hearing Jesus say not to be afraid.

He also separates two kinds of fear. One is the dread of a touchy, punishing God, which he wants to get rid of. The other is the Bible's fear of the Lord: awe, reverence, amazement. That one he wants back, and he thinks its loss has done real damage (chapter 6).

From ch. 1, 3, 4, 7, 8, 9, 12 and the preface

The book never claims Manning has got trust sorted out. His pattern, he says, is falling down and getting up, over and over. Each time, what gets him up is trusting that he is forgiven, not anything he has done.

The windowsill story has a second half. Years later, in prayer, he pictured his mother as a six-year-old on an orphanage windowsill, begging God for parents. His anger at her drained away. He calls that moment the disarming of the greatest enemy of trust in his life.

The relapse comes in the chapter on suffering, alongside a stillbirth and natural disasters. He tells it without excuses: the bottle, the five-dollar bill, the second pint, crying out for Jesus. He had done everything that worked for others: meetings, the Twelve Steps, a sponsor, prayer. He couldn't see why it hadn't worked for him.

Chapters 1–2

The way

What trust is, why it matters more than insight, and the test that shows whether you really have it.

01The Way of Trust

Trust is the gift God wants back

Believing that God exists is a matter of the head. Saying "I trust you" is a matter of the heart, and only the second changes you.

  • Jesus didn't put trust at the edge of his teaching but at the centre. Asked how the Kingdom would come, his answer was trust in God and in him.
  • Trust brings no final clarity. It doesn't calm the chaos or dull the pain. It is what you can still say when nothing is clear, as Jesus did on the cross.
  • Mistrust keeps us chronically anxious: dread that salvation is only for the pious, fear we're bound to backslide, a suspicion that the good news is wishful thinking. Self-hatred, for Manning, is a sign of not trusting that Jesus' work is enough.
  • The way of trust leads into obscurity, not a clear plan. Taking risks needs discernment with family, friends and a mentor first. After that, only trust will act.
  • Legalists distrust people who trust God rather than rules, and their favourite weapon is guilt.

02The Way of Gratefulness

Gratitude is how you can tell

Film ten people who all say they trust God for a month. The one who lives with steady gratitude is the one who means it.

  • Gratitude means seeing your whole life as an unearned gift. Manning tells of the philosopher Jacques Maritain, at 77, skipping across a hilltop and shouting that he was alive.
  • Its opposite is grumbling. Benedict's Rule treats it as a serious offence. Manning loves the line about sending two stout monks to reason with a troublemaker.
  • In 1996 George Gallup Jr. found people who were poor and deeply committed to faith to be more grateful and forgiving than others.
  • Giving thanks for an unanswered prayer or in inner desolation is, in his phrase, a doxology in darkness.

Four marks of a grateful life

InclusiveThankful for all of life, failures too. An Irish A.A. member called himself a grateful alcoholic: without the program he'd never have found God.
AttentiveBusyness, addiction and distraction blind us to each day's gifts. Gratitude needs prayerful awareness.
ContagiousA woman losing all movement was glad she could still move her neck, then glad of visits. You can't be grateful and resentful at once.
TheocentricChesterton: the atheist's worst moment is feeling grateful with nobody to thank. Gratitude trusts there is Someone.

Chapters 3–6

The difficulty, and a bigger God

Manning faces the strongest objection to trust, then argues that the answer isn't an explanation but a much larger picture of God, one that is also close.

03The Enormous Difficulty

Suffering is the real objection

A Christian writer who skips pain and evil is, Manning says, naive, dishonest or out of touch.

  • He begins with his own grief. His friend Rich Mullins was killed in a car crash at 41. Manning turned down every invitation to speak at memorials, lost in doubt, fear and anger.
  • A woman named Anne Donovan delivered a stillborn baby, and friends told her it was God's will or that heaven had needed a new flower. Platitudes from people who haven't suffered, he says, are not just useless but galling.
  • The scale of evil (death camps, Kosovo, school shootings) leads Louis Dupré to reverse the old question: not how God can allow evil, but how God is still possible. Philosophy, Manning says, runs aground here.
  • Job and the psalms of lament don't defend God. In the end God says Job, angry as he was, spoke rightly, and the polished theologian did not.
  • A second obstacle is Christians who preach a cruel or score-keeping God, like the woman in the French Quarter who welcomed nuclear war as a sign of the Rapture.
  • Only someone who has been through the valley can credibly speak of God in it. Jesus, he says, will look us over for scars, not medals.
04Thinking Big

A God too big to manage

The Hebrew kabōd, God's glory, means weight. Feeling that weight even briefly breaks the illusion that we are in control.

  • The word grows through three meanings: physical weight; then wealth and status; finally a light so bright that God is hidden in it. Moses is allowed to see only God's back as he passes.
  • Manning points to a newly found planetary system around a star 264 trillion miles away and a universe 12 to 15 billion years old. The point is scale, not science.
  • All talk about God is analogy: like and radically unlike. Forget the unlike and we get chummy, predict Armageddon, and claim to know exactly what God wants.
  • He mocks his younger self: newly ordained at 29, he pitied an old friar who said the older he got, the less he understood about God. Aquinas, after his own experience, called his writing straw.
  • A brush with glory leads to adoration, exposes our pictures of a vengeful God as projections, and ends the illusion of control. We accept that we are creatures, and that acceptance is where trust can wake up.
05Artists, Mystics, and Clowns

Our pictures are flat wrong

How we see God, other people and ourselves is badly off, and we need people with other eyes to break it open.

  • Dostoevsky's drunk Marmeladov imagines God welcoming drunkards on the last day precisely because none of them thought they deserved it.
  • Catherine of Siena described God as mad with love. Francis of Assisi, called God's clown, is pictured upside down, seeing everything suspended from God's kindness.
  • After 37 years of pastoral work he concludes that many believers' picture of God is radically wrong. We flatten other people too, reducing them to a label.
  • A wrong view of God often starts with a crushed view of ourselves. A student of his, told by a supervisor she had no gift for teaching, came to him soon after and announced she no longer believed in God.
06Infinite and Intimate

Hold the distance and the nearness together

Lose God's otherness and he shrinks to a disciplinarian. Lose his nearness and he becomes a distant spectator.

  • The cost is awe. Prayer time becomes a checklist. Parker Palmer calls the church an enemy of solitude, keeping people busy until they burn out.
  • Without a sense of God's greatness, faith shrinks to moralising. A child learns that God shares the fussy concerns of every parent, teacher and pastor, and comes to link God with fear. A youth worker who admitted struggling with pornography was fired the same afternoon.
  • But history shows the opposite error too. Reacting against a heresy that denied Christ's divinity, the church stressed his divinity so far that he became remote, and worship turned clerical: communion rails, the priest's back to the people.

Chapters 7–8

Trusting Jesus

The book's core definition, and the ways real trust gets corrupted.

07Trusting Jesus

“Faith + hope = trust.”

Faith comes from a personal experience of Jesus. Hope is relying on his promise. Trust is the two together.

Manning's equation, with his examples

1The centurion says he isn't worthy to have Jesus under his roof, yet asks for one word to heal his servant. Jesus says he hasn't found such trust in Israel.
2The royal official is told his son will live and sets off home on the strength of the word alone.
3The father of the mute boy believes in Jesus but doubts the cure, and asks for help with his lack of trust. What's missing is hope.
4The Israelites at Canaan still believe in God but give up on his promise when they see the giants. Manning calls this tainted trust (ch. 8).

Cases from ch. 7–8; diagram drawn for this page

  • Biblical knowledge is felt, not just reasoned. We learn what God is like from Jesus, not Jesus from our ideas of God. So trust means letting go of both our frightening and our comforting images of God.
  • Jesus promised presence and a promise, not protection from suffering. Trust doesn't demand explanations. It knows how to wait.
  • A four-year-old fell off a dock into deep water. When his father finally found him clinging to a post under water, the boy said he had just been waiting for his dad. He had a history of being loved.
  • Trust can't be generated by willpower. What is in our power is paying attention to Jesus' faithfulness, so there is no point in beating ourselves up for lacking it.
“the courage to accept acceptance”

08Tainted Trust

Two ways trust goes bad, and a third

Control tries to do God's part. Presumption leaves our own part to God. Hiding keeps parts of us off-limits.

Trust sits between two failures

Control
  • Craves proof, then takes charge
  • Endless analysis instead of action
  • God reduced to a spectator
Trust
  • Does the hard work
  • Leaves the outcome to God
  • Acts without guarantees
Presumption
  • Expects God to intervene on demand
  • Skips the work: prayer instead of treatment
  • Assumes a good outcome is guaranteed

Arrangement mine, from ch. 8

  • Edward Farrell names three obstacles to trust: amnesia (forgetting God's past faithfulness), inertia (not acting on his promise) and mañana (putting off until tomorrow what Jesus asks today).
  • Manning never planned his life. He joined the Franciscans in 1956, became a priest, and would never have imagined marrying or wandering the world as an evangelist. Yet he still catches himself trying to manage his life, and says only his hour of silent prayer each morning stops him trying to run the universe.
  • Father Joe Martin of A.A.: you can't ask Jesus to make you a heart surgeon overnight, and you can't ask him to cure your drinking without meetings, a sponsor and the Twelve Steps.
  • Jesus himself wasn't rescued from the cross. Trust doesn't assume God will step in. Often it begins on the far side of despair, once control and manipulation are exhausted.
  • Keeping our fantasies and worries from Jesus amounts to saying we trust him, within limits.
Chapters 9–12

Living it

What trust looks like day to day: humility without self-contempt, no comparisons, attention to the present, and no self-pity.

09Humble Confidence

Humility isn't self-hatred

Humility here isn't thinking badly of yourself; it's barely having yourself in view at all.

  • His parable of two seminarians who insult each other daily to become humble ends with both fleeing into self-destruction. A poor self-image keeps all the attention on the self.
  • The North American church's weakness, he says, is refusing to admit its brokenness, and the face we're afraid of losing is only the impostor's mask.
  • An old Franciscan's advice runs down a ladder: be holy; if not, perfect; if not, good; if not, wise; if none of these, carry your weight of sin to God without bitterness. Then the ladder runs back up.
  • Like seed that grows while the farmer sleeps (Mark 4), trust ripens into confidence on its own. Work hard, but the outcome rests with God.

The crab without a shell

Before a five-day silent retreat in Grand Coteau, Louisiana, his director told him to come out of the shell of his reputation and come to God as just a human being. In those hours he found a second addiction alongside alcohol: ministry, with its recognition. Even his closeness to God had started to stand in for God himself.

10The Cracked Pot

Stop comparing

The cracked pot was only miserable because it compared itself with the perfect one. It never knew it had been watering flowers along the path.

  • He refuses to squeeze a moral out of the fable. Moralising turns good news into another burden.
  • He reads Martha and Mary differently: Jesus, tired and afraid on his way to Jerusalem, needs company, not dinner. Mary sees it.
  • He rented a New Orleans office to write and was blocked for three months. Meanwhile he started shopping twice a week for Johnny, an 80-year-old neighbour. In prayer he heard that the shopping was the point and the book was a bonus.
  • We are taught to compare ourselves from infancy. The tumbler monk, who had nothing to offer Mary but his circus routine, worshipped more truly than the high mass upstairs.
  • His theme: trust yourself as someone God has given everything needed to live fully. Daniel Krulish, born with multiple learning disabilities, said he wouldn't want to be anyone else. He died at 23 in a fire on 28 December 1997.
11The Geography of Nowhere

Now/here

Run "now" and "here" together and you get "nowhere". Staying there, fully present, is an act of radical trust, because it is the only place God can be met.

Where the mind goes instead

A Cajun runs home in a panic because his house is on fire, then remembers his name isn't Richard and he doesn't own a house. Manning, drawing on John Shea, says the terrors of the unknown future are made by the mind alone.

Drawn for this page, after ch. 11

  • The Irish hero Fionn's finest music is the music of what is happening. It can only be heard in the present.
  • Manning's own prayer gets hijacked by yesterday's phone call and tomorrow's lecture. On a three-mile walk on Thanksgiving Eve he never noticed he was walking, only tomorrow's dinner.
  • Thich Nhat Hanh: wash the dishes to wash the dishes, not to get to dessert. Attention to ordinary things is preparation for prayer, and it brings compassion for ourselves and others.
  • Children are natural contemplatives until we hurry them. Nor is perfect presence possible or desirable: calm planning is fine as long as it isn't an escape.
12Ruthless Trust

Trust without self-pity

Self-pity is the normal first response to loss. Ruthless trust doesn't suppress it. It puts a time limit on it.

  • The mystic John Tauler prayed for eight years for a teacher, then found a bleeding beggar on the church steps who said he'd never had a bad day, because he accepted everything from God's hand. Tauler later called trust plus gratitude the shortest way to God.
  • Morrie Schwartz, dying, allowed himself a few tears each morning and then turned to what was still good. Self-pity turns malignant when it drives us into isolation, drinking or drugs.
  • Our culture prizes ruthless competition; Jesus asks for ruthless compassion. Dorothy Day told Manning about Peter Claver refusing to leave a dying slave because the man was Christ.
  • Ruthless trust also faces real guilt. A culture where everyone pleads not guilty can only pretend to be forgiven. Manning keeps falling and getting up, and he says forgiving seventy times seven describes Jesus' own practice.
  • He writes warmly about believers tormented by doubt who refuse to fake it. They are not outsiders among ragamuffins, and they are often the most credible witnesses.
  • AH, for "at home": breathe it out through the day as a reminder that God lives in you. He says being at home is the best way he knows to strip fear of its power.
  • His closing thought is that trust is the highest form of love, so hearing "I trust you" may mean more to Jesus than hearing "I love you".
