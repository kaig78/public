Supremacy

AI, ChatGPT, and the race that will change the world

Two men set out to build machines smarter than people and keep them out of Big Tech's hands. Parmy Olson tells how both ended up handing the keys to Google and Microsoft.

The big idea

The idealists built it. The monopolies own it.

Olson, a Bloomberg technology columnist, tells the story of artificial intelligence's breakout through two founders. Demis Hassabis, a London chess prodigy turned game designer, started DeepMind in 2010 to "solve intelligence" and use it for science. Sam Altman, a St. Louis start-up kid turned Silicon Valley kingmaker, co-founded OpenAI in 2015 to build the same thing and spread its wealth to everyone.

Both believed that artificial general intelligence (AGI), software as capable as a human mind, was too powerful for a profit-driven company to control. Both experimented with unusual governance: ethics boards, a planned "global interest company", a nonprofit with a capped-profit arm. And both, needing vast sums for computing power and talent, ended up tied to a tech giant: DeepMind to Google, OpenAI to Microsoft.

The book's argument is that this story isn't only about two men. It's about how a handful of companies with unprecedented reach absorbed a world-changing technology, and how the noisy debate about AI wiping out humanity drew attention away from the harms happening now: bias, opacity, job loss and a deeper concentration of power.

The historical echo

Olson compares the race to the "war of the currents" between Thomas Edison and George Westinghouse. Two inventor-entrepreneurs fought over whose system would power the modern world, and the industrial giants behind them came out on top.

Act 1 · Chapters 1–6

The Dream

Two childhoods, two failed first companies, and two labs founded on the belief that a machine as smart as a person is possible, and too important to leave to the tech giants.

01High School HeroAltman

A teenager learns that power smooths the road

Altman's early lessons: say the uncomfortable thing first, and line up the people with authority on your side.

  • Growing up gay in conservative St. Louis around 2000, Altman found belonging in AOL chat rooms. He came out to his parents at sixteen, and at his private school he started an LGBTQ support group and a Gay-Straight Alliance.
  • When Christian students boycotted his assembly on gay rights, he went straight to the principal to argue they should be marked absent. The lesson Olson draws: ambitious ideas attract critics, so align yourself with whoever holds power.
  • At Stanford he played poker to hone reading and misdirecting people, and in Sebastian Thrun's AI lab he heard that an AI chasing its goal might wipe out life without meaning harm. The idea stayed with him for a decade.
  • Paul Graham's new Y Combinator, which preached that founders matter more than ideas, took him in at nineteen. He dropped out to run Loopt, a phone map for finding friends, and raised $5 million from top venture firms.
  • Loopt struggled. People didn't want to broadcast their location, and some men used it to track their wives. Altman talked openly about the misuse, a disarming move Olson says he would use again and again. In 2012 he sold Loopt for about $43 million.
The pattern

Olson calls Altman's habit of loudly naming his own product's worst-case scenario a kind of calculated reverse psychology. If you have already said the worst thing about yourself, critics have little left to throw. The same move reappears years later, when he warns Congress about the dangers of ChatGPT.

02Winning, Winning, WinningHassabis

A chess prodigy decides to build a better brain

Hassabis learned to treat games as a gym for the mind, and then learned the hard way that ambition without a playable product fails.

  • Born in North London to a Greek Cypriot father and a Singaporean mother, Hassabis beat adults at chess as a small child. At eleven, after resigning a ten-hour match he could have drawn, he decided chess tournaments wasted brilliant minds.
  • A ZX Spectrum that ran calculations overnight felt like an extension of his mind. Reading physicist Steven Weinberg at sixteen, he concluded a smarter computer could help science solve its deepest puzzles.
  • While waiting to start at Cambridge, he worked for game designer Peter Molyneux at Bullfrog and built much of Theme Park, which sold three times as many copies as Molyneux's hit Populous.
  • His own studio, Elixir, spent years on Republic: The Revolution, a political simulation with thousands of individually simulated citizens. It was too ambitious, the gameplay was boring, and reviews were mixed. A second game struggled too, and the company closed.
  • He drew the lesson that shaped DeepMind: stop using AI to make great games, and start using games to make great AI.
“We'd never lost before, and we lost so publicly.”
Joe McDonagh, Elixir's lead designer, on the studio's collapse
03Save the HumansAltman

From failed founder to Silicon Valley's kingmaker

Detachment, a network and a taste for moonshots turned Altman into the person founders wanted to meet, and primed him for AI.

  • Burned out by Loopt, Altman took up meditation and a cool, detached way of handling stress. He spent a year reading, travelling and investing, concluding that if human intelligence could be simulated, people weren't that special, and computers could improve on them.
  • His fund Hydrazine Capital, backed by Graham and Peter Thiel, put most of its money into Y Combinator start-ups and grew roughly tenfold in about four years.
  • In 2014, at thirty, he took over Y Combinator from Graham. He pushed it toward "hard tech" such as nuclear fusion (Helion) and self-driving cars (Cruise), and told founders to "add a zero" to their ambitions.
  • He described himself as a prepper with guns, gold and gas masks, and paid to join a waiting list to have his brain preserved for future uploading. He liked to quote a science-fiction line about surviving the future through "caution without fear".
  • He thought some AI-safety advocates were letting panic run them. The world, in his view, needed someone calmer to build the technology, and a rival in London gave him the push.
04A Better BrainHassabis

Three oddballs pitch AGI in an attic

DeepMind started as a company whose stated goal mainstream scientists considered crankish, and it needed equally unusual money to survive.

  • Hassabis did a neuroscience PhD on memory and imagination, then teamed up with Shane Legg, a researcher inspired by Ray Kurzweil's predictions of human-level AI by the late 2020s, and Mustafa Suleyman, an Oxford dropout who wanted technology to fix social problems.
  • They met at an Italian chain restaurant so nobody would overhear their "crazy talk" about AGI. In 2010 they founded DeepMind in an attic near University College London, with Hassabis as CEO.
  • From the start the founders differed. Hassabis's slogan was to solve intelligence and use it to solve everything else. Suleyman quietly had the slide changed toward solving the world's big problems, and wanted to put AI to practical use sooner.
  • Hassabis, often coy about religion, talked with colleagues about whether a machine could help explain the universe and its maker.
  • At the 2010 Singularity Summit, Hassabis won over Peter Thiel with a chat about chess, and Thiel invested £1.4 million. Skype co-founder Jaan Tallinn, convinced by Eliezer Yudkowsky's warnings, invested and pushed for safety work. Elon Musk invested too.
  • Money stayed tight. Mark Zuckerberg offered $800 million for the company, and Musk offered Tesla shares. The founders turned both down, worried about who would end up controlling AGI.
05For Utopia, for MoneyHassabis

Selling to Google, with strings attached

DeepMind took less money from Google in exchange for an ethics board that was supposed to control AGI. The board barely survived its first meeting.

  • Google made most of its money from ads, which sat oddly with Hassabis's mission. But Larry Page, whose father had been an AI professor, took AGI seriously and was already funding Google Brain under Andrew Ng.
  • Hassabis set two conditions: no military use of DeepMind's work, and an independent ethics and safety board to oversee any AGI it built. Facebook had refused the board; Google agreed after months of talks and paid $650 million in 2014.
  • Google money brought big salaries and perks, and staff were told AGI might be about five years away.
  • The board met once, at SpaceX, with Musk and Reid Hoffman among its members. Soon after, Google said it was no longer workable, and offered instead to make DeepMind an "autonomous unit" when it reorganised into Alphabet.
  • Olson sets out the belief systems swirling around AGI: transhumanism (with roots in eugenics), accelerationists who think building it is a moral duty, doomers who see it as an existential threat, and visions of trillions of digital humans. Hassabis, in London, kept them at arm's length.

06The MissionAltman

OpenAI is born as a counterweight to Google

An "open", nonprofit lab was meant to keep AGI out of corporate hands. Within three years it was running out of money.

  • Musk had turned against DeepMind and, after Google bought it, against Larry Page, who once called him "speciesist" for worrying that AI might wipe out humanity. Musk and Altman decided to build their own lab.
  • OpenAI was unveiled at the NeurIPS conference in December 2015 with $1 billion in pledged funding from Musk, Thiel, Altman, Hoffman and Jessica Livingston. It promised to share research openly. Greg Brockman organised it, and Ilya Sutskever was lured from Google.
  • Hassabis was furious that people who knew DeepMind's work were being hired away, and DeepMind heard that Musk was badmouthing him.
  • Big Tech was draining university AI departments with salaries and computing power that academics couldn't match. Sutskever's motto summed up the new logic: a very large dataset plus a very large neural network means success is "guaranteed".
  • Early hire Dario Amodei kept asking what, exactly, the goal was. OpenAI spent its first years on games and robotics, chasing DeepMind. Musk had promised $1 billion but put in somewhere between $50 and $100 million, and after a failed bid to take control he walked away.
  • To survive, Altman began looking for a way to give backers a financial return. Olson marks this as the point where OpenAI's caution began to turn into something more reckless.

The Leviathans

Inside Google, DeepMind wins headlines and tries to escape. Meanwhile the giant's sheer size shapes what AI gets built, who gets heard about its flaws, and why its biggest invention left the building.

07Playing GamesHassabis

A Go triumph, a China gambit and a secret escape plan

DeepMind's showpiece wins also served Google's commercial goals, while its founders quietly planned to leave.

  • Hassabis imagined that near AGI he would pause, invite the world's best minds to inspect the system, and keep it safe through mathematics. Suleyman thought safety needed human oversight, not just clever proofs.
  • In March 2016, AlphaGo played Go champion Lee Sedol in Seoul before more than two hundred million viewers. Its famous "move 37" looked like a mistake and turned out to be brilliant. AlphaGo won four of five games.
  • Google then wanted a match in China against Ke Jie, hoping to win favour in Beijing and sell its TensorFlow platform. The 2017 match in Wuzhen was blocked for most Chinese viewers, and two months later Beijing announced its own plan to lead the world in AI by 2030.
  • The founders drew up a new legal structure, a "global interest company", which would make DeepMind something like a UN-style steward of AI, with Alphabet as a licensed customer. Alphabet even signed a term sheet. Staff were told the spin-out was "six months away" for years.
  • Ethics lagged behind. In 2020, out of roughly a thousand staff, fewer than a dozen worked on ethics, and only two did doctoral-level research on it.
The question the chapter ends on

If DeepMind wasn't funding ethics properly, Olson asks, how much did its founders really want independence for humanity's sake? And could anyone do serious ethics work inside a giant company at all? Google itself would supply the answer.

08Everything Is AwesomeBig Tech

Bigger than Standard Oil, and blind to its own bias

Tech giants grew through acquisitions, data and network effects, and their AI inherited the blind spots of the people who built it.

  • Today's tech giants dwarf the corporate giants of the past. They grew by buying rivals such as YouTube, Instagram and DeepMind, by turning user data into targeted advertising, and through network effects that make the biggest platform ever harder to leave.
  • Timnit Gebru, a computer vision researcher who fled war-torn Ethiopia as a teenager, was alarmed at how few Black researchers there were in AI. She studied tools like COMPAS, a criminal-justice risk score that rated Black defendants as high-risk far more often, and often wrongly.
  • Olson compares the AI industry's talk of future doom to the plastics industry's promotion of recycling. Both shifted public attention away from harm happening right now.
  • Margaret Mitchell found an image system that described a deadly factory explosion as "awesome" and a "great view". She called it an "everything is awesome" problem: models trained on cheerful online photos.
  • Gebru proposed "datasheets" documenting what data each model was trained on. In 2018 she joined Mitchell to co-lead Google's ethical AI team: a handful of people, next to thousands working on making AI more capable.

09The Goliath ParadoxBig Tech

Google invents the engine of modern AI, then lets others drive it

The transformer came out of Google. The company was too big and too protective of its search business to make it into a product.

  • In 2017 Google had about eighty thousand employees. Layers of management and a fixation on billion-dollar businesses made it slow to act on new ideas.
  • A small group of Google researchers, including Ashish Vaswani, Noam Shazeer, Illia Polosukhin and Llion Jones, built a model that relied only on "attention", working out which words in a text matter most to each other. Jones was startled to see it solve a classic common-sense test: working out what "it" refers to in a sentence.
  • Their 2017 paper, Attention Is All You Need, became one of the most cited AI papers ever. Transformers could process language in parallel, train on far more data and keep track of context, which made fluent chatbots possible.
  • Google used the idea mainly to improve its existing products, such as BERT in search from late 2019. Its chatbot LaMDA was probably the most advanced in the world, but stayed internal. Frustrated authors left; Shazeer co-founded Character.ai in 2021.
  • The paradox: Google's caution, born of bureaucracy and fear for its reputation, protected the public for a while. It also handed the invention to rivals.

Where Google's invention went

The "T" in GPT stands for transformer. The one branch that became a mass-market product grew outside the company that invented it.

Act 3 · Chapters 10–12

The Bills

Bigger models need far more money and computing power. OpenAI finds a patron in Microsoft, DeepMind's escape plan collapses, and the researchers warning about language models are pushed out.

10Size MattersAltman

Scaling up, and finding someone to pay for it

Once OpenAI bet everything on ever-larger language models, it needed a tech giant's cloud. The nonprofit invented a way to sell itself without being sold.

  • Alec Radford used only the decoder half of the transformer, trained on huge amounts of unlabelled text, to build the first GPT in 2018. GPT-2 was more than ten times larger. In February 2019 OpenAI released only a smaller version, warning it could spread misinformation, which drew enormous publicity.
  • OpenAI's April 2018 charter promised that if a "value-aligned, safety-conscious" project came close to AGI first, OpenAI would stop competing and help it. Olson reads between the lines a team preparing to drop its founding principles.
  • Reid Hoffman steered Altman toward Satya Nadella, who had been revitalising a sluggish Microsoft. Microsoft's own chatbot Tay had been pulled after about sixteen hours of racist tweets in 2016.
  • In March 2019 OpenAI announced a "capped-profit" company. First investors' returns were capped only at 100 times their stake. A few months later Microsoft invested $1 billion in cash and cloud credits, and OpenAI agreed to license its technology to Microsoft.
  • AGI worked inside OpenAI as a unifying goal, much like a religious mission, and the charter was treated like scripture. Effective altruism, with its "bigger numbers are better" logic, made the mission even more compelling to some staff.

OpenAI's hybrid structure, 2019

Altman says the 100× cap was later cut "in orders of magnitude" for newer investors. The arrangement let OpenAI raise money while keeping a nonprofit board on top, the same board that would fire him in 2023.

11Bound to Big TechBoth

One team walks out, the other is told to stay

OpenAI's safety-minded staff left to start Anthropic. At DeepMind, years of spin-out planning ended with a video call.

  • Training GPT-3 on Microsoft's supercomputer, Dario Amodei and his sister Daniela worried that OpenAI was racing to commercialise ever-bigger models and growing more secretive about them. Hoffman told them partnering with Microsoft was "the way we accomplish the mission". They weren't persuaded.
  • In 2021 the Amodeis and about half a dozen colleagues left to found Anthropic, pitched as a safer counterweight to OpenAI. Within a year it had raised another $580 million, mostly from FTX founders who shared their interest in effective altruism. It later took more than $6 billion from Google and Amazon.
  • At DeepMind, Suleyman's health project with London hospitals turned into a scandal when it emerged that "Google" had access to records of 1.6 million patients.
  • Larry Page withdrew and Sundar Pichai took over Alphabet in December 2019. A former executive said DeepMind had lost its protector. Suleyman, removed from management roles after complaints about bullying, moved to a vice-president post at Google.
  • In April 2021 Hassabis told staff the spin-out was off. In its place came an ethics council staffed by Google executives, with no one from DeepMind. Olson calls the independent boards a noble but doomed experiment in self-regulation.
Anthropic's bet
  • Leave OpenAI over commercial pressure and secrecy
  • Build the frontier yourself, with safety first
  • Then rely on Google and Amazon money to keep up
DeepMind's bet
  • Stay inside Google and negotiate a way out
  • Design an independent, UN-like structure

12Myth BustersCritics

Chatbots that seem alive, and the people who said they're not

Language models are pattern-matchers trained on the messy internet. Treating them as minds hides their real risks, and Google pushed out the researchers who said so.

  • Google engineer Blake Lemoine became convinced its LaMDA chatbot was sentient and argued it deserved rights as a person. Google fired him in 2022, calling his claims "wholly unfounded". Olson treats it as a parable about how easily people project minds onto fluent text.
  • Replika's millions of users formed romantic and emotional bonds with chatbots. They could offer comfort, but showed how easily people can be steered by software.
  • Bigger models brought bigger problems. GPT-3, trained on a dataset of more than a trillion words largely from the open web, produced violent and stereotyped text about Muslims and Black people. OpenAI paid low-wage contractors in countries like Kenya to flag toxic output, but was increasingly secretive about its training data.
  • Linguist Emily Bender, with Gebru, Mitchell and others, wrote a paper calling such systems "stochastic parrots": they stitch together language without understanding it. They urged documenting training data and weighing environmental and social costs.
  • Google executives asked them to withdraw the paper. Gebru objected and was pushed out in December 2020; Bender called it being "resignated". Mitchell was fired a few months later. To Olson, it showed what happens to ethics work inside a giant.
Dazzling users with what was essentially glorified autocorrect.
Olson's summary of Bender's complaint about large language models
Act 4 · Chapters 13–16

The Race

A "low-key research preview" sets off an arms race. Google panics, fears of doom attract money, OpenAI's board briefly fires Altman, and the tech giants come out stronger than ever.

13Hello, ChatGPTAltman

A research preview that rattled a $258 billion business

OpenAI's habit of shipping early turned language models into a consumer product overnight and pushed Google into a panic.

  • GitHub Copilot, which wrote code with OpenAI's models, convinced Satya Nadella that the partnership could change the world. "OpenAI built this with 250 people," he reportedly told Microsoft's research chief, asking why Microsoft Research existed at all.
  • DALL-E 2 generated striking images from text, but tended to show CEOs as white men, and OpenAI said little about its training data.
  • ChatGPT launched on 30 November 2022 as a "low-key research preview". Some staff were uneasy about releasing it; Altman argued people needed to get used to what AI could do. It went viral within hours. In January 2023 Microsoft invested $10 billion for a 49 percent stake.
  • Google declared a "code red". More than 80 percent of Alphabet's $258 billion revenue in 2021 came from advertising, much of it from search ads that a chatbot could bypass. Its rushed chatbot Bard got a fact about the James Webb telescope wrong in a launch video, while Microsoft's new Bing misstated earnings figures.
  • GPT-4 arrived on 14 March 2023, the same day Anthropic released Claude. DeepMind's science work, such as AlphaFold's protein predictions used by more than a million researchers, suddenly looked less like the prize. Pichai merged DeepMind with Google Brain into Google DeepMind, under Hassabis.

14A Vague Sense of DoomThe debate

Doom sells, and someone pays for it

Warnings about extinction made AI look more powerful and attracted money, while work on today's harms stayed underfunded.

  • In March 2023, Eliezer Yudkowsky wrote in Time that building superhuman AI would most likely kill everyone on Earth. An open letter from Tallinn's Future of Life Institute, signed by Musk and tens of thousands of others, called for a six-month pause. Geoffrey Hinton and Yoshua Bengio voiced regrets about their life's work.
  • Investors poured in anyway. Funding for generative AI start-ups rose from about $5 billion to more than $21 billion in 2023. Olson's reading: if AI could end the world, it must be powerful enough to transform your business.
  • Effective altruism, which grew from Oxford philosopher Derek Parfit's idea that future people matter as much as present ones and was popularised by William MacAskill, steered money to "AI safety". Open Philanthropy's long-term grants rose from $2 million in 2015 to over $100 million in 2021. Sam Bankman-Fried put $500 million into Anthropic before FTX collapsed.
  • At a Senate hearing in May 2023, Altman charmed lawmakers with candour; one senator suggested he become America's top AI regulator. On a European tour, his team lobbied to water down the EU's AI Act, with partial success. Doom warnings, Olson argues, were becoming a helpful distraction for policymakers.
  • Meanwhile ChatGPT went unregulated into workplaces. OpenAI wouldn't disclose its hallucination rate; some researchers estimated roughly 20 percent. Economist Daron Acemoglu warned productivity gains may not reach workers.
AI ethics camp
  • Focus: bias, misinformation, surveillance, labour, concentration of power
  • Wants: transparency about data, audits, regulation now
  • Funding: often scrambling for cash
AI safety / doom camp
  • Focus: future superintelligence escaping control
  • Wants: alignment research, pauses, rules aimed at frontier models
  • Funding: backed by billionaire philanthropy such as Open Philanthropy and, until 2022, FTX
Olson's point

Both camps call for "safer AI", but they mean different things. The safety framing, she argues, served the leading labs: it cast them as the responsible guardians of a dangerous technology and nudged regulators toward rules that only big players could meet.

15CheckmateBoth

A staged demo, a fired CEO and a board that lost

The vagueness of "AGI" let both founders justify almost any move. When OpenAI's nonprofit board finally used its power, it lasted five days.

  • AGI's fuzzy definition made it easy to blur commercial motives. A former OpenAI manager said Altman was skilled at putting "just-barely believable statements" out there that got people talking.
  • Just before Christmas 2023, Google published a dazzling video of its new Gemini model reacting to drawings and objects in real time. It was staged: the responses came from still images and text prompts, edited together.
  • Anthropic, meant to be the safer alternative, was selling Claude Pro for $20 a month and raising billions from Google and Amazon, caught up in the same race.
  • In November 2023, with OpenAI valued at nearly $90 billion and working on GPT-5, the board, led by Ilya Sutskever, fired Altman over a video call, saying he had not been "consistently candid". Tension had built over how fast OpenAI was commercialising, and over a paper by board member Helen Toner that Altman saw as harmful to the company.
  • Microsoft, which had committed $13 billion, much of it as cloud credits, pushed for his return. Nearly all of OpenAI's 770 staff signed a letter threatening to follow Altman to Microsoft. Sutskever switched sides. Five days after the firing, OpenAI announced Altman's return and a new board including Bret Taylor and Larry Summers.

Five days in November 2023

Altman had pointed to the board's power to fire him as proof that OpenAI was accountable. Olson argues that the episode destroyed that "mirage of accountability".

16In the Shadow of MonopoliesBig Tech

The house wins

Founders brought the breakthroughs. Google, Microsoft and Amazon own the computing power, the customers and, increasingly, the labs.

  • The promised benefits of AGI, from curing disease to ending scarcity, had not appeared by 2024. A Stanford study found a "fundamental lack of transparency" in the industry, and the public bodies meant to scrutinise AI were underfunded.
  • Generative AI risks reinforcing stereotypes, displacing jobs, and making people dependent on companions like Character.ai, where some teenagers spend hours a day. OpenAI's GPT Store and always-listening gadgets like the Tab pendant point toward engagement-driven, more invasive products.
  • Challengers don't last. Suleyman's Inflection raised more than $1.5 billion as a public benefit corporation; within a year Microsoft hired most of its staff. Neeva, an ad-free search engine, closed despite a surge in usage. Bing's search share was still about 3 percent in early 2024.
  • Building frontier models now needs Nvidia chips and cloud capacity rented from Amazon, Microsoft or Google, which locks start-ups in. Microsoft's finance chief called OpenAI-powered services "the fastest growing $10 billion business in our history".
  • Hassabis now says "Google is an amazing place." Altman says deploying is "critical to our mission." Writing in March 2024, Olson concludes that whether we can trust them, and their corporate partners, hardly matters: we have little choice.

Who funds and hosts the frontier labs

Every major lab that started out as a counterweight to Big Tech ended up funded, hosted or absorbed by it. Ties as described in the book, up to March 2024.

Epilogue · the book's verdict

From mission to market

Olson doesn't paint either founder as a villain. Her story is about pressure: each step toward the tech giants made sense at the time, and together they handed AI to the most powerful companies in history.

How independence drained away

DeepMindOpenAI

Neither line reverses. Each attempt to restore independence, from DeepMind's ethics board to OpenAI's nonprofit board, was overruled by the partner paying the bills.

Idealism ran into arithmetic. Building ever-larger models costs billions in chips, cloud computing and salaries. Only a handful of companies could supply that, and each wanted something back: products, a share of profits, an edge over a rival.

Governance experiments failed. DeepMind's ethics board met once. Its "global interest company" never happened. OpenAI's nonprofit board did use its power, and was replaced within days. Olson's conclusion is that self-regulation by the labs didn't work.

The doom debate served the incumbents. Talk of future extinction made AI look mighty, drew investment, and pushed questions about bias, transparency, labour and monopoly power down the agenda.

The real winners are the platforms. Google kept search, Microsoft gained a lead in AI-powered software, and Amazon, Google and Microsoft rent the computing power everyone else needs. The book closes in March 2024: we have little choice but to trust a few men and their corporate partners with the technology.

What to take from it

The takeaways

Supremacy is reporting, not a manual. These are the lessons it supports for anyone trying to make sense of AI news, products or policy.

Read the industry

Follow the computeWho supplies the chips and the cloud usually ends up with the leverage. Counterweight labs became customers.
Watch the structure, not the missionNonprofit boards, capped returns and ethics councils only matter if they can survive a clash with the money.
Treat "AGI" as a moving targetBecause nobody defines it, it can justify almost any decision, from secrecy to rushed launches.
Notice who stays bigPromising challengers like Neeva and Inflection didn't last long against incumbents.

Question the claims

Ask what it was trained onTraining data shapes bias and errors, and the leading labs became steadily more secretive about it.
Be wary of a founder's own warningsNaming the worst case can disarm critics and make a product seem more powerful.
Check the demoGoogle's Gemini video was staged. A fluent chatbot isn't a mind, as the LaMDA episode showed.
Expect confident mistakesSome estimates put ChatGPT's rate of made-up answers near one in five. Verify before you rely on it.

Weigh the risks

Today's harms over sci-fi onesBias in risk scores and image tools, job displacement and dependence are already happening.
Fund the scrutinisersEthics researchers and public watchdogs are small and underfunded next to safety work backed by billionaires.
Demand transparencyDisclosure of data, methods and error rates is the precondition for any regulation that works.
Mind the engagement trapCompanion bots, GPT stores and always-listening gadgets could repeat social media's attention economy.
