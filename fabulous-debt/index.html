The epic story of how bonds built the modern world

Introduction

The market that can intimidate a president

The book opens in April 2025. A week after unveiling sweeping tariffs, President Trump paused most of them for ninety days. Stocks had been falling for weeks, but Wigglesworth reports that what alarmed Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick was the bond market: the yield on the Treasury bond due in 2035 had jumped from a low of 3.8% to 4.5% by the morning of 9 April. Trump himself admitted that the bond market’s jitters had prompted the pause.

A bond is a loan cut into tradable pieces, usually paying a fixed coupon. Because the coupon is fixed, a falling price means a rising yield, the return a new buyer actually gets. Treasury yields are the benchmark for borrowers everywhere.

Bonds have a dull reputation. Wigglesworth’s case is that they are now the most important part of the financial system, probably bigger than banking itself, and that this shift is the defining issue in finance today.

Clinton adviser James Carville wanted to “come back as the bond market”; the author thinks that is truer than ever.

Era I · Chapters 1–4

Invention

Venice invented the bond, the Dutch spread it through society, Britain built a superpower on it, and Hamilton used it to bind a new country.

01Venice, 1171

A forced loan that nobody could repay

The bond began as an emergency war levy. What turned it into a lasting institution was a fixed coupon that was reliably paid and a claim that could be sold to someone else.

  • The trigger. On 12 March 1171 the Byzantine emperor had more than 20,000 Venetians arrested and their goods seized. To pay for a revenge fleet, Doge Vitale II Michiel assessed citizens’ wealth and compelled them to lend at 5% a year.
  • The twist. The receipts, prestiti, were tradable. The expedition collapsed in plague, a mob killed the doge in 1172, and the loan was never repaid; interest kept flowing and the debt became permanent.
  • The first bond market. Rialto dealers traded the claims. In 1262 Venice merged its debts into one Monte (“mountain”) at 5%, with creditors paid before almost all other spending.
  • Creditors as constituents. The Great Council that managed the debt was full of the city’s largest lenders, so default was against their own interest.
  • Price as a verdict. The Monte traded at 80–100% of face until the 1370s, then fell to around 20% in the 1380s war with Genoa.

Why price and yield move in opposite directions

Book example: a ten-year bond with a $1,000 face value paying a fixed $40 a year. Worried investors bid the price down, so the fixed coupon gives a new buyer a higher return. The curve between the three marked points is calculated with standard bond maths.

02Netherlands

A wet country that borrowed its way to power

The Dutch took the bond out of the city walls. Ordinary savers in many towns lent to each other, and the whole country could borrow cheaply even while at war.

  • Water boards. Local boards financed the dikes with renten, annuity-style bonds. When drifting ice broke a dike on the River Lek on New Year’s Day 1624, the local board sold over fifty goatskin bonds and raised about 23,000 guilders for the repair.
  • Cheaper money in wartime. Holland’s borrowing cost was over 8% around 1550–60. By the late 1600s it was 3–4%, despite decades of war with Spain.
  • The contrast. Philip II of Spain defaulted four times and paid nearly 10% on short loans; after the 1575 default, unpaid Spanish troops sacked Antwerp.
  • The first corporate bond. The Dutch East India Company (VOC) raised 6,424,588 guilders from shareholders in 1602 and barely issued shares again. It grew on debt instead. The earliest known VOC bond, from 26 October 1622, paid 6.25%.
03England

Consols: parliamentary credit beats a bigger enemy

After 1688, England copied Dutch finance and put its debt in Parliament’s hands rather than the king’s. One deep, uniform pool of debt let a smaller country outspend France for more than a century.

  • Before and after. In the 1600s England paid 8–14% to borrow, the Dutch 3–5%. After 1688, debt rose from £1 million to almost £17 million in the Nine Years’ War, yet yields fell to about 3% by the 1730s.
  • Sampson Gideon. This stockjobber steadied the market in the 1745 Jacobite scare and helped Prime Minister Henry Pelham cut the rate on £58.7 million of 4% debt, in steps, to 3% and merge it into one perpetual bond: the Consol.
  • The Consol’s advantages. A debtor answerable to Parliament, strong property rights, open accounts, identical securities and confidence there would always be a buyer.
  • Waterloo. Debt reached about £900 million, more than twice GDP. French rentes sometimes sold for a tenth of face value, while Consols rarely fell below half. Their post-war recovery toward par, the author says, gave Britain a large wealth effect that helped finance industry.
  • The title. Macaulay, mocking the prophets of ruin, called it “a gigantic, a fabulous debt”.

04United States

Hamilton turns a ruined credit into a national asset

By taking over the states’ war debts and paying everyone in full, Hamilton made bondholders an interest group with a reason to keep the union together.

  • Starting point. In 1787 American government bonds traded below 15 cents on the dollar, a yield of 25–40%. Congress couldn’t tax, and the federal government owed $54 million while the states owed $25 million more.
  • The First Report (January 1790). Hamilton argued for paying whoever held the paper in full: buyers had bought fairly, and tracing original holders would be unjust and unworkable.
  • The deal. James Madison led the opposition, convinced “a public debt is a public curse”. At a dinner arranged by Jefferson, Madison accepted federal assumption of state debts in exchange for siting the capital on the Potomac.
  • Results. By early 1791 the new 6% bonds traded near 85–90 cents. When William Duer’s attempt to corner them collapsed in 1792, Hamilton’s sinking fund bought bonds to calm the panic, an early version of what is now called quantitative easing.
  • Louisiana. Napoleon sold it for $15 million. Barings and Hope & Co. bought the $11.25 million of 6% US bonds from France at a 13.3% discount and resold them in London and Amsterdam.
Era II · Chapters 5–8

Expansion

Bonds went international, industrial and public, and produced the first global bond bust, a railway crash and credit ratings.

05London, 1820s

Rothschild’s template, and a country that didn’t exist

Nathan Rothschild made foreign bonds easy for British savers to buy. Investors looking for higher yields then bought almost anything, including bonds from a country that didn’t exist.

  • The template. Rothschild’s 1818 £5 million loan to Prussia was a sterling bearer bond with interest payable in London, and Prussia had to accept checks on its borrowing. It set the pattern for a borderless market.
  • Poyais. In October 1822 the Scottish adventurer Gregor MacGregor sold a £200,000 bond for Poyais, an invented Central American country. Of 250 settlers on the first two ships, about 180 died.
  • The 1825 bust. When the Bank of England tightened credit as gold flowed out, Latin American bonds that had been used as loan collateral collapsed. Every Latin American borrower except Brazil eventually defaulted, 73 of 770 English banks failed, and British GDP fell 13% in 1826. The Bank only slowly accepted the role of lender of last resort.

Where a nineteenth-century bond’s face value went

The borrower owed the full face value but received only part of it. Mexico’s 1823 bond, underwritten at 50 with £421,360 deducted, raised about £1.18 million against a £3.2 million liability. Goldschmidt resold at 58; the book puts its take, with fees, near £300,000.

06Railroads

Jay Cooke saves the Union, then sinks Wall Street

Bonds can spread risk among many investors. They don’t help when the unsold bonds end up on a bank’s own books.

  • Selling the Civil War. With $50 bonds, 2,500 salesmen and heavy advertising, Cooke raised over a quarter of the Union’s $6.2 billion of wartime bonds, notes and currency.
  • Rails on bonds. US railway bonds outstanding rose from $416 million in 1867 to more than $5 billion by 1890. Cooke agreed to sell $100 million of Northern Pacific bonds at 7.3%. When buyers dried up after a Vienna crash in 1873, the unsold bonds and loans to the railroad stayed on his firm’s books.
  • 18 September 1873. Jay Cooke & Co. couldn’t raise $1 million against collateral that was already pledged. The NYSE closed for ten days, 71 railways defaulted in 1874 alone, and the Long Depression lasted more than five years.
07Ratings

From credit gossip to a legal standard

Ratings started as a way to fill an information gap. Regulation then made them mandatory, and when issuers began paying for them their incentives changed.

  • Three founders. Lewis Tappan’s Mercantile Agency (1841) reported on merchants’ credit; Henry Varnum Poor’s manual (1868) gathered railroad data; in 1909 John Moody graded nearly 1,300 railroad bonds from Aaa to E.
  • Built into regulation. In 1936 US banks were barred from buying “speculative” securities as defined by rating manuals. In 1975 the SEC formally designated Moody’s, S&P and Fitch, which today hold roughly 90% of the market between them.
  • The conflict. In the 1970s the agencies switched from charging investors to charging the issuers whose bonds they rated. After 2008 the official crisis inquiry called them essential to what went wrong.
08Liberty bonds

A nation of bondholders

America paid for the First World War by turning bond buying into a test of patriotism, and its government debt was never paid down again.

  • From 350,000 to 34 million. Bankers told Treasury Secretary William McAdoo that only about 350,000 Americans owned bonds. By the end of the war around 34 million had bought federal bonds. The first Liberty Loan, at 3.5%, drew about four million subscribers and was oversubscribed, and five loans raised $21.4 billion in total.
  • The campaign. Charlie Chaplin rallied crowds on Wall Street, preachers gave Liberty bond sermons, and people who refused to buy were sometimes shamed.
  • Prices fell anyway. Trading showed the bonds were priced too richly; they slipped below par, and a $1.7 billion buyback fund couldn’t lift them back.
  • The legacy. Researchers link the campaign to more stock and bond ownership afterwards. US government debt never shrank back, which realised Hamilton’s idea of “public credit immortal”.
Era III · Chapters 9–13

Reinvention

Capital controls and inflation nearly finished the bond market. It came back offshore, then grew into junk, mortgage securities and derivatives.

09Bretton Woods

The dollar replaces gold, and bonds lose money

Bretton Woods tied currencies to the dollar and the dollar to gold. The capital controls it needed broke up the international bond market, and the inflation that followed nearly finished bonds as an investment.

  • Keynes versus White. In July 1944 Keynes’s global clearing currency lost to Harry Dexter White’s plan: the dollar was fixed to gold at $35 an ounce.
  • Financial repression. From 1942 the Fed held long Treasury yields at 2.5% while inflation peaked near 20% in 1947. The 1951 Treasury Accord gave the Fed its independence to set rates.
  • The Triffin dilemma. To supply the world with dollars the US had to run deficits, so the dollars held abroad eventually far exceeded the gold behind them. By 1969 US gold reserves were $10.5 billion against $40 billion of dollars in foreign official reserves. Nixon suspended convertibility on 15 August 1971.
  • Inflation. The ten-year yield peaked at 15.84% in September 1981. Someone who bought a ten-year Treasury in 1970 had lost nearly half their money in real terms by 1980.
10Eurobonds

Siegmund Warburg brings international bonds back to London

Rules made to protect the dollar pushed a new market offshore, and it put London back at the centre of international finance.

  • Eurodollars. Dollars held abroad escaped US caps on deposit rates (Regulation Q); by the late 1960s European banks held $53 billion of them.
  • Autostrade, 1 July 1963. S. G. Warburg arranged a $15 million, 5.5% bearer bond for Italy’s motorway company. To avoid taxes it was formally issued at Schiphol airport and listed in Luxembourg, and many buyers were anonymous clients of Swiss private banks.
  • Washington helps. Kennedy’s Interest Equalization Tax, proposed that month, drove foreign borrowers out of New York: $40 million of foreign bonds sold there in 1964, against about $545 million of dollar Eurobonds.
  • New products. Floating-rate notes tied to LIBOR came out of this market. By 1982 Eurobond issuance exceeded the US domestic corporate market; today it is over €13 trillion.
11Junk

Michael Milken’s high-yield market

Milken was right that a diversified portfolio of risky bonds pays investors more than enough to cover defaults. Where he went wrong was assuming that held for the much riskier bonds his own market created.

  • Hickman’s study. W. Braddock Hickman found that from 1900 to 1943 low-rated bonds out-returned high-rated ones despite more defaults. At Drexel, Milken built a market on that: trading, a loyal buyer network, and from 1977 new junk issues.
  • Scale and power. Junk outstanding grew from $15 billion (1976) to $125 billion a decade later. Drexel held close to 70% of the market, and its “highly confident letter” let raiders bid for companies.
  • The fall. Drexel pleaded guilty to six felonies and paid $673 million. Milken pleaded guilty in April 1990, served twenty-two months, and was pardoned in 2020. Drexel went bankrupt in February 1990.
  • What lasted. A 1989 study found more than 16% of high-yield bonds issued in 1977–86 had already defaulted, because buyout debt was far riskier than the fallen angels in Hickman’s data. Still, the book reports that since mid-1986 junk bonds have returned over seventeen times investors’ money, against about ten times for investment grade, and the market is now about $5 trillion.
12Securitization

Turning mortgages into bonds

Lewis Ranieri at Salomon Brothers turned home loans into tradable bonds, and bank business began moving into the bond market.

  • Earlier versions. Prussia’s 1769 Pfandbriefe became Europe’s covered bonds, which the book says have never lost money. In the Depression about $8 billion of America’s $10 billion of 1920s mortgage bonds defaulted.
  • The pass-through. Ginnie Mae’s first modern mortgage-backed security (February 1970) pooled loans and passed the monthly payments on to investors with a government guarantee. Because homeowners can repay early, investors never knew when they would get their money back.
  • Ranieri’s solution. A former mailroom worker, Ranieri profited from a 1981 tax break for thrifts that sold mortgages, then split the cash flows into tranches. The first was a $1 billion, three-tranche collateralized mortgage obligation for Freddie Mac in 1983. He lobbied for a 1984 law that let most institutional investors buy them, and for 1986 tax rules that made far larger deals possible.
  • Growth. Mortgage securities went from under $100 billion in 1980 to nearly $1 trillion a decade later.
“Securitization is like fertilizer. You can grow tomatoes or blow up buildings.”
Simon Mikhailovich, hedge fund manager, quoted in ch. 12
13Derivatives

Futures, swaps and credit default swaps

After 1971, interest rates and exchange rates could be traded separately from the bonds themselves. That made risk easier to hedge and also easier to hide.

  • Futures on money. Leo Melamed paid Milton Friedman $5,000 for a feasibility study and opened currency futures at the Merc on 16 May 1972. Treasury and Eurodollar futures followed.
  • Swaps. In August 1981 Salomon arranged for the World Bank to service IBM’s Swiss franc and Deutschmark debts in return for IBM servicing new World Bank dollar bonds. Each side got cheaper funding. Interest-rate swaps reached about $5 billion by the end of 1982. Within a decade, swaps of all kinds referenced $4.6 trillion.
  • Credit default swaps. In 1994 JPMorgan’s Blythe Masters paid the EBRD to bear the default risk of a $4.8 billion Exxon credit line. The 1997 BISTRO deal, below, became the model for synthetic CDOs.

Tranching: who takes the first losses

The loans stayed on JPMorgan’s books, but the bond carried their risk, so regulators granted capital relief. The Ba2 (junk) slice takes the first $237 million of losses; triple-A is hit only after.

Era IV · Chapters 14–18

The machine

Power moved from bankers to funds, leverage came through repo and money funds, and each breakdown needed a bigger central bank rescue.

14PIMCO

Bill Gross trades bonds actively

Most investors used to buy bonds and hold them to maturity. Gross showed that trading them actively could produce large returns, and bond fund managers gained more power than the banks that sold to them.

  • From the vault to trading. In 1971 Gross was clipping coupons in Pacific Mutual’s vault. He talked the firm into letting him trade a $5 million account, which became PIMCO. A former blackjack card-counter, he called his method “structural alpha”: many small, systematic edges.
  • The edges. These included short-dated junk bonds paired with futures, selling options on bond volatility (“selling the noise”), insisting on large allocations of new issues, which tended to be priced slightly cheap, and a mispriced Ginnie Mae futures contract that made $70 million at once.
  • Peak and exit. PIMCO grew past $2 trillion; Gross left in September 2014, a day before he expected to be fired.
15LTCM, 1998

Repo and leverage

A repo is a short-term loan secured on bonds. Long-Term Capital Management used it to turn tiny price gaps into large profits, until the gaps widened and lenders demanded more collateral.

  • How repo works. Sell a bond and agree to buy it back tomorrow at a slightly higher price; the difference is the interest. The safer the bond, the more you can borrow.
  • The trades. John Meriwether’s former Salomon team, with Myron Scholes and Robert Merton as partners, started in 1994 with $1.25 billion. One early trade: a new thirty-year Treasury yielded 7.24% and a six-month-older one 7.36%. LTCM bought $1 billion of the older bond, shorted the new one, levered about 25 times, and profited when the gap closed.
  • The crash. By spring 1998 it held $134 billion of assets on about $4.5 billion of capital. After Russia’s moratorium on 17 August its spreads widened instead of narrowing, it lost 44% that month, and the New York Fed organised a rescue by Wall Street banks.

Leverage turned 2.45% into 42.8% (1995)

The margin spiral

The loop recurs: LTCM in 1998, Bear Stearns’s funds in 2007, the Treasury basis trade in 2020. LTCM’s Victor Haghani later concluded its trades were mostly sound but too big for its capital.

162008

Money market funds: the keystone species

Wigglesworth argues that the global financial crisis was as much a bond-market crisis as a banking crisis, and that at its centre was a run on short-term funding that nobody had properly mapped.

  • The keystone. Money market funds are the Pisaster, a small starfish whose removal upsets a whole ecosystem: they supply the repo and commercial paper much of finance runs on.
  • Leverage fails. Bear Stearns’s two mortgage funds turned about $1.9 billion of investors’ money into more than $20 billion of mostly CDO holdings, financed by repo. In June 2007 they collapsed; the bank itself fell to a repo run in March 2008.
  • Breaking the buck. The oldest money fund, Reserve Primary, had dropped its ban on commercial paper in 2006; holding $785 million of Lehman paper, it fell below $1 a share. In one week $349 billion fled prime funds, until the Treasury guaranteed the industry.
  • The fix. Prime funds now have floating values and have shrunk to about $1.2 trillion, while government-only funds manage more than $6 trillion. The author’s verdict is that the risks moved outside the banks rather than going away.
17QE

When the central bank buys bonds

Now that so much lending goes through the bond market, cutting overnight interest rates isn’t always enough, so central banks also buy long-term bonds to push down long-term rates.

  • Origins. Japan tried it hesitantly in 2001. The Fed began on 25 November 2008 with $600 billion of agency mortgage bonds; by 2016 its balance sheet was $4.5 trillion.
  • Through the looking glass. With QE and negative policy rates in Europe and Japan, bonds with negative yields peaked above $18 trillion. A Danish bank offered negative-rate mortgages, and even some junk-rated European companies’ bonds traded at negative yields. Post-COVID inflation ended it.
  • Does it work? A 2021 ECB review found every central-bank study positive, but only about half of independent ones.
How QE creates money (the book’s example)
Fed buys $1bn Treasury from a pension fund → pays with new central bank reserves
pension fund deposits it at its bank → bank holds $1bn reserves, owes $1bn deposit
net: the Fed has created short-term debt (reserves) to buy long-term debt; every balance sheet still balances
18March 2020

The safest market breaks

In the “dash for cash”, investors sold Treasuries because those were the only bonds they could sell, and the market that was meant to be the safe haven stopped working properly.

  • Signs of trouble. The ten-year yield hit a record low of about 0.5% on 9 March, then rose to nearly 1.2% by 18 March in the middle of a stock crash. Older, off-the-run Treasuries effectively stopped trading.
  • Why. Post-2008 rules left banks less room to absorb selling. Hedge funds ran a basis trade, buying Treasuries and shorting futures with repo leverage of up to about a hundred times; their short futures passed $750 billion. Margin calls forced them to sell.
  • The Fed’s response. On 15 March the Fed cut rates to near zero and promised at least $700 billion of bond purchases. On 23 March it promised unlimited purchases and a facility to buy corporate bonds. Its balance sheet eventually grew by about $4.7 trillion, yet it bought under $14 billion of corporate bonds: the promise alone reopened the market.
  • The author’s view. Wigglesworth argues the Fed was right, but that it was still a bond-market bailout. The New York Fed’s Daleep Singh: “you can’t destroy risk, you can only move it”.

Chapter 19 · How Much Is Too Much?

A $340 trillion mountain and a 400-year-old bond

Wigglesworth ends between two positions. Warnings about ruinous debt have been wrong for three centuries, yet today’s debt levels still worry him, and he thinks a bond-market crisis may be what finally forces governments to act.

By late 2025 government debt worldwide had passed $100 trillion; with companies, banks and households, total debt topped $340 trillion, nearly four times global output, mostly in bonds.

Ferguson’s Law, Niall Ferguson’s 2025 proposal, says a great power is in trouble once interest costs exceed defence spending. The US crossed that line in 2024. Wigglesworth treats it as a rough rule of thumb: Britain probably broke it at the height of empire, and Japan’s debt is nearly 240% of GDP with no crisis.

All three big agencies have now cut America’s triple-A (S&P 2011, Fitch 2023, Moody’s 2025). Recent turmoil, he argues, came from policy mistakes unwinding leveraged trades, not solvency fears, but the kindling for a larger fire is there.

He ends in Utrecht in December 2024, at the 400th birthday of the 1624 dike bond from chapter 2. It still pays interest, now €13.61 a year, collected by its owner, the New York Stock Exchange.

Ferguson’s Law, United States 2024

Interest on federal debt$880bn · 3.1% of GDP

Interest is forecast to reach nearly 5% of GDP by 2049. Wigglesworth thinks a little spending discipline and somewhat higher taxes could ease it.

Takeaways

The toolkit

The book is a history, not an investment guide. These lessons recur across its 850 years.

Reading the market

Read the yield, not the couponThe trading price shows what lenders really demand.
Credibility cuts borrowing costsFrom Venice to Hamilton, accountable borrowers paid less.
Treasuries set the base rate for everyoneMortgages, company bonds and other countries’ debt are all priced off US yields.

Spotting danger

A bad loan is still bad when it’s in a bondPackaging doesn’t change the borrower: railways, Poyais, subprime.
Being able to sell isn’t the same as finding a buyerIn a crisis buyers disappear.
Ask what the leverage isA tiny spread levered many times is a big bet in disguise.

The bigger picture

A nation’s debt isn’t a household’sMacaulay’s point: much of it is owed to the country’s own citizens, and growth can outpace it.
But it is not unlimitedNo ratio marks the danger point, and markets can still force a change of course, as Liz Truss found in 2022.
Risk moves, it doesn’t disappearSafer banks pushed risk into funds and money markets.
Rescues have a costEach one teaches people to expect the next.
