The big idea

Bad communication is a symptom

Hannah and Timo come to therapy saying communication is their problem. Algermissen disagrees. At the start of their relationship they talked easily, so they know how. What has changed is everything that now gets in the way: hurts that were never repaired, a darker picture of each other, and emotions that take over in seconds.

Her answer has two parts that run through the whole book: regulate your emotions so they don't knock you off course, and build understanding for yourself and the other person. Better phrasing only helps once those two are in place. Words without feeling behind them are, in her phrase, empty shells.

The book follows a couples-therapy process. Part 1 diagnoses why talking gets hard. Part 2 builds the foundation: notice, classify, change. Part 3 shows how couples grow together and stay out of the old loop. Exercises come throughout, and she asks you to do them as you go.

The core model

Every hard conversation has three layers

We argue about the holiday flat, but that's only the tip. Underneath sit the big relationship themes, and in the moment, the emotions that make us react on autopilot.

The iceberg of a small argument

Algermissen draws on three models: Watzlawick's point that every message has a content and a relationship side, the sender–receiver model in which what is sent isn't always what arrives, and the iceberg. The part that steers the conversation is the part nobody says out loud.

What the holiday-flat fight was really about

the flat pressure being seen equal footing being patronised understanding responsibility being left alone lost closeness

Hannah wants to book; Timo wants to look first. Within eight lines Hannah says nothing would get done without her, Timo says it always goes her way, and the holiday is off. Timo's subtext: I feel pressured and you decide over my head. Hannah's: everything lands on me, and when you pull back it feels like a slap.

Signs a conversation is about more than its topic
  • Strong emotions appear out of nowhere
  • You feel misunderstood, and keep repeating yourself
  • Problems don't get solved, although both try

Part 1 · Chapters 1–5

Understand the problem

Why do people who give friends wise advice, stay calm with annoying colleagues and open up easily to strangers lose all those skills with the person closest to them?

01Communication Is the Problem

The words were never the problem

Couples knew how to talk at the start. What blocks them later are repeated hurts that have turned into trigger points.

  • "Triggered" means two things. In everyday speech it just means annoyed or frustrated. In clinical psychology it's a cue that brings a past feeling back into the present, as in a war veteran's flashback. The book deals with a milder but very common kind, the wounds left by childhood, school or earlier relationships.
  • Her examples: a child who learns that love comes only with achievement; a pupil who eats lunch alone every day and concludes they're strange; someone who was cheated on and now anticipates betrayal every time their partner goes out.
  • A typical sign is the fight over a trifle. In seconds a conversation moves from swapping information to winning, and if one of you has to win, you're no longer on the same team.
  • Common shapes: ping-pong blame, where every complaint is answered with the other's faults; passive-aggressive digs that feel like "many small knife pricks"; and no longer really talking at all. In each, talking produces frustration instead of closeness. Communication turns into a weapon instead of a bridge.
  • "Just be nicer to each other" doesn't work. Couples in these loops already regret their fights. They just end up in the same place next time, with one more hurt and a little more distance.
  • Why can the therapist find calm words when the couple can't? Two and a half reasons: she isn't emotionally involved, she sets out to understand each person, and (the half reason) she's trained. Regulating emotion and building understanding become the book's two threads.
The quiet version

Elli and Francesca never fight. Friends call them the model couple. But they only discuss the weather and their jobs. Francesca doesn't know Elli is often lonely; Elli doesn't know Francesca feels hemmed in by their routines. When Francesca comes home late once again, Elli, close to tears, says: "You do what you want anyway." Not every damaging pattern is loud.

02What Nobody Teaches Us

We operate on the heart without training

Imagine starting medical school and being sent straight into heart surgery. That's how we're sent into relationships.

  • It isn't your fault that communication is hard. Nobody teaches it, and many people even see it as a bad sign if a relationship takes work.
  • Algermissen quotes therapist Esther Perel: the quality of our relationships determines the quality of our lives. A meta-analysis she cites links working on relationships to better mental health.
  • In any one conversation we usually talk on three levels: the apparent subject, the big themes nobody names, and the emotions rising in the moment that make us reactive.
  • Watch for meaning that doesn't arrive. When Timo asks "Why are you putting on so much pressure?", he's sending I'm tense, I'd like a calmer talk. Hannah probably receives you're getting on my nerves.
  • Exercise: recall the last bad conversation with anyone (a parent, a friend). What was the spark? What did you want to get across that didn't land? What one heading would describe it?
  • Hurts are hard to spot in other people because they're hidden under anger or apparent indifference. For now, the point is only to sense that we're often discussing several important things at once.
“The quality of our relationships determines the quality of our lives.”
Esther Perel, quoted by Algermissen (my translation)
03Why Can't It Just Be Easy?

Unrepaired hurts change the picture we have of each other

Negative feelings signal unmet needs. They also fire off fast protective reactions, and those hurt the other person's needs in turn.

  • Driving home from therapy, Hannah wants to talk it through and Timo, overloaded, goes quiet. Both stew. When he nearly misses a cyclist, it all comes out: "You don't look out for anyone!" / "You've been talking at me the whole time!" Calmer, he could have said it's been a long day, give me a few minutes; she could have said that really scared me.
  • The ping-pong runs like this: Hannah criticises a trifle, which undercuts Timo's need to be valued. He withdraws and turns away. She feels punished, so her anger rises, which hurts him more. Each one's protection is the other's injury.
  • John Gottman calls the result negative sentiment override: unhappy couples see even neutral moments through a dark lens, so a harmless comment reads as an attack. Add the negativity bias, our tendency to weigh bad experiences more than good ones, and the hurts stay vivid while the good moments fade.
  • The turning point comes when you're more annoyed by your partner's reaction than by the issue. "The dirty dishes annoy me" becomes "you annoy me." From then on, the other person is the problem.
  • The picture shifts slowly: from "you're on my side" to "mostly reliable, sometimes selfish" to "I have to watch out for you." The benefit of the doubt shrinks. Warning signs: sentences that start with "always", the feeling of "I knew it", and no alternative explanation for what the other did.
  • Who started it and who did more only become questions when both people feel unsafe. Relaxed people don't need to prove the other is the problem.
  • Avoiding hot topics (Elli and Francesca) and pushing them at full speed both fail. You can't build good communication by ignoring problems or by forcing them.

How flow turns into friction

The eight stages Algermissen describes. There's rarely a clear turning point, which is why couples don't notice they need to change something.

Whatever comes to mind first shows how far the picture has shifted. Only if you pause and question it can you respond differently.

The river

Early on, communication flows like a river: whatever you send reaches the other bank. Over time, driftwood from unprocessed hurts piles up. Less and less gets through, until it feels like you no longer speak the same language.

04What Hardly Anyone Knows

Communication is only as strong as how we handle emotion

Too little feeling is cold; too much is escalation. The aim is calm, not cold, and open, not explosive.

Fire, ice and the middle way

Many people lean one way under stress, and some swing between both. Hannah is fire, Timo is ice. Each has what the other lacks: she's in touch with her feelings, he can stay calm. That's what they admired in each other at the start.

  • Ice often works well at the office. These people are efficient, cheerful and full of practical advice. Their partners say things like "he's there, but somehow absent." Everyday logistics get discussed; depth never comes.
  • Timo walks on eggshells. He keeps quiet about what bothers him to offer "as little surface as possible." The therapist turns it around: the further he withdraws, the harder Hannah pursues, because to her it feels like contact being withdrawn, even punishment.
  • Men in particular are still taught to stay cool and rational. A boxed note cites Australian research on men after suicide attempts who said that as boys they had no room to show vulnerability (a trigger warning comes with it).
  • Learned phrases like "I feel under pressure" help only if they carry real feeling. Words without emotion are just shells.
  • The key-and-lock principle: rational problems need rational answers, and emotional problems need emotional ones. Mixing them up is a classic: she wanted empathy and got a three-point plan. Say what you need first, for example "I just want to tell you how I feel; I don't need a solution yet."
  • Fire: Hannah says she sometimes only "wakes up" when it's all over, like coming out of a tunnel. There is a gap between trigger and outburst, and that's where change is possible. Her first signal is heat rising from her chest to her face.
  • Treat change like training for a muscle goal: short-term costs (getting off the couch, facing feelings) for long-term gains you can't see at first.
Signs you're running on ice
  • You go stiff in conversations
  • You fear how the other will react
  • You distance yourself inside
  • You want to escape emotional moments

  • Heat in the body, sweaty palms

05So What's the Solution?

Listen for the grinding before it turns into a fight

The moment someone feels attacked, hurt or unseen, you have an emotional problem first, whatever the topic.

  • Feelings matter less when you're calmly drawing up a plan. They take the lead when the goal is closeness, being understood or helping with something emotional. Many people misread the situation as a rational problem.
  • Algermissen's name for the tipping point is the Knirschgefühl, the grinding feeling: the moment a conversation suddenly feels wrong. Signs: repeating yourself, going in circles, irritation, frustration, the sense that what you say isn't landing, a conversation that loses its lightness.
  • The grinding means you've missed each other emotionally and are pulling in two directions. Catch it and you can avoid both the fight and new hurts. Ignore it and it builds to escalation.
  • She caught herself in the same trap days after writing the chapter: her partner talked about a stressful work week, she started handing out tips, and they went round in circles. She laughed, came back and said, "We missed the grinding. You don't want advice, do you?" The tension dropped immediately.
  • A better version of Hannah and Timo's talk isn't perfect. Timo still proposes a plan and gets a bit snippy, but Hannah states her goal ("I don't want a plan right now") and he asks "What can I do?" She answers: "Just listen." Imperfect communication can still change the outcome.
  • Practise on low-stakes topics first, like a hike, the weekly shop or choosing a film. If you start with your sorest themes, you're more likely to fail and lose heart.

Decoding the colleague conversation

  1. HannahMy colleague behaved badly again today. I can't stand it any more.emotional problem: pressure at work
  2. TimoRaise it with your boss. It's been going on forever.a fix for an apparently rational problem
  3. HannahI can't. They're friends, it would get around.grinding 1: suggestion rejected
  4. TimoYou're not pillorying anyone. You have to say something at work, as often as you complain to me.grinding 2: repetition
  5. HannahYou don't get it. Your job's easy.grinding 3: defence

He wanted to solve, she wanted to share. Neither noticed they had different goals, so the talk ground its way down to their oldest themes.

1 · Name your goal
  • "I'm asking because I want to solve this, not to attack you."
  • "I'm quiet because I'm looking for the right words, not to shut you out."
2 · Call out the grinding
  • "We're going in circles. Do you actually want a solution?"
  • "Feel that grinding? Something's off." Hannah, hungry: "I'm in a grinding mood."
3 · Name feelings
  • "I'm pretty tense. Can we take a short break?"
  • Good ones too: "It's lovely sitting here with you tonight."

End of Part 1 checklist. Understand: what are your themes, and how has your picture of the other changed? Feel: team fire, how do you notice emotions boiling up? Team ice, what keeps you from opening up? Communicate: watch for the grinding and say your intention up front.

06The Old Protective Strategy

Your brain treats losing connection like a sabre-toothed tiger

Fight, flight and freeze buy short-term safety and cost long-term closeness. The way out starts with noticing sooner.

  • The brain learns from danger: after a near-miss with a car, your pulse rises the next time you cross while looking at your phone. For a social species, being cut off from the group was as deadly as a predator. Attachment is a basic need.
  • Edward Tronick's Still Face experiment shows it: when a mother suddenly stops reacting, her baby first tries everything to get her back, then turns away. Even a blank face is enough.
  • Conflict with someone close can register as a threat to the bond, so the brain switches to fight, flight or freeze, fast and automatic. That's why you "forget" every good technique exactly when you need it. "Triggered" means high activation because an old wound was hit.
  • Hannah fights: you're pulling away, so I'll attack before you can hurt me. Timo flees or freezes: if I wall myself off, you can't reach me. Neither gets what they want, which is closeness and understanding.
  • Timo objects that he knows his partner isn't a sabre-toothed tiger. Hannah jokes that he runs from her as if she were one, and he grins: "Your attacks are my sabre-toothed tiger." Hers is withdrawal of love and being left alone. Everyone should know their own tiger.
  • The three steps out are learned like the piano: theory, then clumsy practice, then gradual fluency, with wrong notes on bad days. Step one is noticing. About 11 million impressions hit us each second and only around 40 become conscious, so the signals need training.
  • Not every danger mode is loud. Going cold and distant is protection too, and for Hannah it's a warning sign that Timo is fleeing inward.

Exit in the yellow zone

The pressure traffic light. Yellow is where you can still turn off. By red you only react. Hannah's yellow is "restless and annoyed". She used to stay quiet there so as not to be the one starting a fight, which only made the pressure climb.

A pause that works

  1. Spot your signal and say so"I'm in the yellow zone. I want to sort this out with you, but I can't listen right now. Give me a minute?"
  2. Agree a length and time itAnywhere from 30 seconds to three minutes. Some couples use a phone timer, and a code word helps.
  3. Breathe, don't just waitBreathe deep into the belly, hold a few seconds, breathe out through the mouth. Repeat.
  4. Ask yourselfWhat put me under pressure? What feeling came up? Which theme was hit? What is my partner trying to tell me? What's our shared goal?
  5. Restart with the answer"That last sentence touched our theme of not being wanted. Could you hold my hand for a moment?"
  6. Check the pressureStill high? Take a longer break or postpone. Never carry on while both are triggered.

Three ways to train noticing

Reflection questionsEach morning or at night (or while brushing your teeth): how high is my pressure, how heavy was today, did I hit a trigger? Plot it 1–10 if you like.
Pressure traffic lightPlace yourself in green, yellow or red, and tell people when you move into yellow. Some couples put coloured stickers on a shared calendar.
Body scanA few minutes a day, travel through your body: tingling, tension, pulling, pain? What happens if you consciously let go or breathe into it?
07Experiences That Hold Us Captive

Behind the anger is an older hurt

Each partner reacts not only to the moment, but to everything that happened between them, and long before them.

  • Timo's family never argued. As a small boy he was sent to his room when he was angry or sad, until he learned to go there himself. So a raised voice means a state of emergency: the bond is at stake, and maybe she's about to leave.
  • "Criticism doesn't have to be shouted to hit you." His father always found something wrong with his performance, and Timo just tried harder. With Hannah he tries to put things right with advice. When that never works, a counter-reaction grows: defiance, "nobody asks me," and finally "I don't care."
  • Elli's first girlfriend lost interest. Elli tried everything (gifts, caring for her parents, reading every wish from her eyes) and was still left for someone new. Now when Francesca makes plans without her, fear of loss surges and she clings. The sense of urgency, I have to do something NOW, is the mark of danger mode.
  • Francesca grew up with rigid rules and a family that wouldn't accept her sexuality. She built a wall to survive their accusations. When she feels hemmed in, the wall goes back up and she turns cold. The colder she gets, the more Elli clings.
  • Hannah is the eldest of four. Her father worked and her mother had her own problems, so Hannah ran the family. She swore never to be as dependent as her mother, and she hates asking for help. When she does ask and Timo forgets, she's angry at herself for depending on him, and the anger keeps him away.
  • Anger often lies over a more vulnerable feeling, like a wounded animal baring its teeth. Hannah's real feeling is being left to fight alone. While only the anger shows, Timo can never learn what these "little things" mean to her.

The pursue–withdraw loop

The full sequence: she gets louder; his tension rises and he goes quiet; she feels rejected and criticises; he feels he's failed again and withdraws further; she loses contact and gets louder; he freezes. Eventually he turns defiant and passive-aggressive, she attacks, and he flees.

08How the Past Shapes Us

When present and past pour into the same glass, it overflows

A trigger feels different from a normal feeling: more intense, rigid and fast. That's the sign something old has joined in.

  • Concepts like the inner child, attachment styles and triggers share one idea: a cue in the present links to an experience from the past and sets off strong feelings and behaviour. Therapists call that link an affect bridge.
  • Timo doesn't want to blame his parents. He doesn't have to: this is about understanding, not fault. Most parents do their best, and school, a first love or a past relationship can shape us just as much.
  • "Every time I think Hannah is overreacting, I've hit a trigger?" Not guaranteed, but it's a good starting point for a conversation, as long as nobody raises it from above.
  • Fixating on the current topic and blanking out the past usually fails. Sometimes you need a wide-angle lens on your life to see what you're looking at.
  • The trigger plan puts the parts of one pattern on paper. Pick a situation where you reacted strongly, picture it vividly, then go through the five steps below and fill in sentences like "I feel my trigger first in…" and "The intensity comes from a time when I…".
  • If it doesn't fit yet, choose another situation, write only the parts that feel right, repeat it another day, or do it with someone you trust. "When I can't think straight, I'm triggered" is a fine start.

09Finding a New Strategy

Don't fight the trigger. Tend the wound.

You can't delete experiences or the feelings they set off. You can change what you do when they arrive.

  • A common mistake at this stage is putting pressure on yourself never to fall back, and condemning your patterns. Trying to feel "nothing" takes enormous energy, and the pressure comes out later as an explosion or as love slowly going cold. She compares it to a car crusher.
  • Patterns once had a purpose. A child with a hot-tempered father learns to read every mood and stay invisible. Thirty years later the same adult is hypersensitive to bad atmosphere and can't voice a need. Therapist Terrence Real calls this part the adaptive child. Sort behaviour into "helpful" and "no longer helpful," not good and bad.
  • The same goes for the "what do others think of me" voice: it grew up as protection against criticism from a demanding parent or coach. Criticising yourself for being self-critical only doubles the pressure.
  • You'd never leave a burnt hand on the stove. You'd cool it, put ointment on it and bandage it. Emotional wounds deserve the same: stop when you notice an emergency, calm the body, understand what happened, then talk.
  • Tell your partner what you're doing before you do it. Getting up and leaving without a word can itself hurt the other person.
  • Timo goes onto the balcony, breathes, watches people below, reads his pattern on his phone, and tells himself on the way back in, "We're a team." Hannah makes tea, writes down what she wants to say, reads her notebook and pictures the overwhelmed little girl in her. Timo learns his tone matters too: "You're getting loud again" said from above lands as "you're the problem."

First aid: four ways to bring activation down

1 · Move

Shake yourself out, walk round the block, squats, push-ups, stairs, a short HIIT round. Also loosen a body that has frozen mid-talk.

"I need to move, my tension's getting too high."
2 · Shift attention

Count breaths, solve a sudoku, name everything starting with L, recite lyrics, say the alphabet backwards. Not social media, which floods the brain.

"Want to count to 100 with me?"
3 · Make distance

Balcony, garden, the next room, a walk, or a calming safe place you visit in your mind with all your senses.

"Back in ten minutes. I want to sort this out today."
4 · Use the senses

Ice cubes in your hand, cold water on your wrists, frozen peas on your chest, something sour or spicy, a scented oil, loud music.

"This oil always helps me. Want to try?"
First aid: lower the intensity
  • Spot the warning signal and pause
  • Use one or two of the four strategies
  • Check: calmer, but is the feeling still in your body?
Aftercare: understand and soothe
  • Read your trigger plan (keep it where you go to calm down)
  • Make contact with the hurt part: a child, a wounded animal, a colour
  • Picture a wise, loving figure going to it, or write it all down and reread it as if a friend wrote it
  • Return with: what triggered me, which wound was hit, what would help me now?
What coming back can sound like

"I just ran straight into my pattern, sorry. Those last sentences hit me so hard I couldn't think. I've felt alone a lot lately and that went right into the wound. I'm calmer now, we can keep talking. Just be a bit careful with me there. Could we hug for a moment?"

10The Understanding Formula

Where understanding ends, the fight begins

Even calm couples go round in circles if both still want to win. First both people need to feel understood.

  • Most couples skip this step and try to settle disputes without having understood each other, like doing a jigsaw blindfolded. Think of a sports team: you listen to a teammate's criticism because you're still playing for the same goal.
  • Three early signs that understanding has broken: repetition (you wouldn't need to say it again if you felt understood), tempo (faster speech, interrupting, firing arguments) and body language (crossed arms, narrowed eyes, loud sighs).
  • In session, the day after Timo phoned from the street to say he was off to see his friend Andreas, both are asked whether they feel understood. "No!" they say in unison. So that comes first.
  • Hannah, asked to dig deeper, finds the common denominator: being left alone, "like always." Timo takes her hand.
  • Understanding works short term (it defuses a tense moment) and long term: you start to see the wound behind the coping behaviour and take less personally. Where "he just doesn't love me" used to be the explanation, there's now a better one.
  • You don't have to share the other person's opinion to understand them.

Grow together

Handling hot moments is only half of it. The other half is keeping fights from building up, repairing them when they happen, and keeping a good loop going.

11Repairing What Broke

What broke has to be repaired

Raise things while pressure is low. After a fight, hold a repair talk instead of just carrying on.

  • A conversation has limited resources. If 90% goes on calming down, little is left for solving. So talk about what bothers you early, before it ferments.
  • Two ways to find your themes: a journal at least weekly (when did I say nothing although something bothered me, and how does it tie to my triggers?) and meditation, where after the itchy legs and to-do lists the bigger themes surface.
  • Timo, on a quiet Sunday, writes a "weekly reflection": she planned the weekend without asking him, which fits his theme of not being seen. Conflict fear sends him to the balcony to calm down. He texts her to suggest a speak-and-mirror talk that evening. Midway he catches himself: "Wait, I only said the criticism. What I'd wish instead is that we plan it together." Hannah: "Phew, I nearly started justifying myself."
  • Without repair, gaps between fights get shorter: each hurt makes you quicker to feel attacked, quicker to strike, and more suspicious.
  • In a repair talk, content and blame don't matter. Look at the problem together instead of making each other the problem. Anger and boundaries are allowed, but staying stuck in blame fixes nothing.
  • Start with understanding and drop the "but". "I see your side. But you have to consider…" cancels itself. People stop listening after the second sentence if they don't appear in it.

The repair conversation in six steps

  1. Regulate and reflect aloneWhat hurt me, which theme was hit, what should I say? Test: could I really let their view into my heart? If not, you're not ready.
  2. Set a concrete timeSoon. Reconnecting has priority.
  3. Agree what happenedFind a shared story, e.g. "We didn't notice it slipping, a misunderstanding caught us, and we both fell into our old patterns."
  4. Find the tipping pointWhere did a normal talk become a fight? Which warning signals did each of you miss?
  5. Understand the other first"Maybe you were as hurt as I was. I know how hard it is for you when I go cold."
  6. Say your view and wishesBe concrete: what hurt, which trigger, what should change in future.

12The Positive Cycle

From vicious circle to a cycle of appreciation

Every couple has resources, things that give strength and connect them. Reactivate them on purpose and keep talking about the hard stuff.

  • Hannah and Timo now sit close together on the couch. Timo feels his opinion counts when they plan; Hannah sees him pitch in and listen. What's missing is a fixed time for deeper talks.
  • Early on, their best conversations happened walking his parents' dog. Evenings are too tiring, so they settle on Saturday-morning walks.
  • Ask yourselves: what keeps us stuck, and which behaviour of A sets off which feeling in B? What helps us move towards each other? What routines keep us in the good loop?

13Typical Problems and Fixes

Ten common snags

A reference chapter to come back to when something stalls.

1 · Too stressed for the relationshipRefill your own reserves at least once a week, and put relationship talks in the calendar.
2 · Conflict fear stops me startingWrite your thoughts down, turn them into a letter or message, then prepare for a spoken talk.
3 · I can't control my emotionsLearn your warning signals better, use regulation preventively, and run "What if" together.
4 · My partner can't take criticismCriticism may be part of their trigger. Ask what it means to them and what would help.
5 · I struggle to name my needsAsk yourself daily what you want. Find where the belief "my needs burden others" came from.
6 · What I say doesn't landIn danger mode nothing new goes in. Make sure you're both calm, then repeat it. Try speak and mirror.
7 · No time for all thisStart with one small ritual: a conscious hug, a kind text, a call on the way to work.
8 · Setbacks"We got out once, we can do it again." Remember how, reread the book, make a plan together.
9 · We can't find solutionsCheck your VOR first. Then look for what works for both and what neither wants.
10 · We miss the exit pointPlan important talks. Set a one-hour timer and a repeating five-minute one to check activation.
14In Closing

Not perfection, but the next attempt

Combine emotion regulation with understanding for the other person's inner world, and you're on the right path.

  • In their last session Hannah describes the armour of anger she wore and how she had to learn to stay with herself and show her soft core. Timo describes his wall and how he became more self-assured by speaking up before things escalate.
  • What helped most: for Timo, time to reflect, and when he skips it the old problems return within weeks. For Hannah, deep talks where both actively listen.
  • What they'll tell themselves next time. Hannah: give him a chance, regulate, and remember that "it's all his fault" only drives you apart. Timo: she's as sad and hurt as I am; we work as a team.
  • Her closing message: be patient, getting tangled up is normal, and those moments are the chance to get closer. It doesn't matter who is right, only that you come together as a team.
