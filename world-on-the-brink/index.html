Brink

How America can beat China in the race for the twenty-first century

A cybersecurity founder who learned to hunt Chinese and Russian hackers argues that the United States is already in a second cold war, that the flash point is Taiwan, and that America can win if it buys time and stops spreading itself thin.

Prologue & introduction

Every morning, Xi should decide not today

The book opens with an invented future. On 13 November 2028, days after a US election, with Washington run by caretakers, Xi Jinping issues Taiwan an ultimatum. When Taipei refuses, helicopters fly from amphibious ships toward the Port of Taipei and the airports, hovercraft race up the Tamsui River toward the government district, and the island's internet goes dark. The scenario ends with an American president being woken to decide whether to fight.

Alperovitch came to this subject through hackers. In 2009, at McAfee, he led the investigation into Operation Aurora, the Chinese break-in at Google, and later exposed Night Dragon and Shady RAT. He concluded that the real question was who was attacking and why, not which malware. That idea shaped CrowdStrike, which he co-founded, and this book: America, he argues, has a China problem, not a cyber problem.

His thesis has two halves. China and Russia are weaker than they look, and the United States is stronger. But the next stretch, perhaps as short as thirty years before China's demographic decline sets in, is the most dangerous since the start of the first Cold War. The goal is to deter an invasion of Taiwan day by day until the window closes, and to keep America's lead while doing it. He sums this up in a Latin motto, Sinae deterrendae sunt: China must be deterred.

Why the author calls it a brink
60%

For over a century no US rival reached 60% of US GDP. China passed that mark in 2014 (Rush Doshi's figure).

4×

Times President Biden publicly said the US would defend Taiwan. There is no treaty that obliges it.

~30 yrs

How long China's window as a superpower may last before its demographic and economic problems catch up.

The cyber lesson he carries over

Spam, he notes, went from about 2% of email to 90% in roughly a year because a thinking opponent adapted to every defence. Geopolitics works the same way: you study the adversary's motives before you study its tools.

Part I · Chapters 1–5

Cold War II, a new era

The first half explains why the US was slow to notice the contest, why Taiwan sits at its centre, and why, on a sober count, America's position is stronger than headlines suggest.

01The road to the brink

One salami slice at a time

China rose inside an American-built order while taking territory in steps too small to fight over.

  • May 2014 as a starting gun. In one month the Justice Department indicted five officers of PLA Unit 61398 for economic hacking, a Chinese oil rig moved into waters Vietnam claims, and dredgers were caught building land on Johnson Reef. Alperovitch suggests historians may date Cold War II from here.
  • The relationship that never settles. George Marshall brokered a ceasefire between Mao and Chiang in 1946, and it collapsed. The author traces US policy through four presidents: FDR treated China as a great power, Truman "lost" it, Nixon reopened it, and Carter recognised Beijing, ended the defence treaty with Taiwan and left behind the deliberately vague Taiwan Relations Act. A fifth, Clinton, changed China's view of America by letting it into the WTO.
  • Lessons China drew. Watching the Gulf War, and then two US carrier groups sent near Taiwan in 1996, Beijing realised it could not match America at sea, so it started a crash programme of missiles and ships designed to keep the US Navy away. The accidental 1999 bombing of its Belgrade embassy was widely read in China as deliberate.
  • Prosperity under US protection. In a generation China went from 90% of people below the $2-a-day poverty line to 3%, on trade routes the US Navy kept open.
  • The reef template. At Mischief Reef, deep inside the Philippines' economic zone, China followed a sequence it later repeated elsewhere: markers, then concrete, then temporary shelters, then a base. A 2016 tribunal in The Hague rejected China's claims. Beijing ignored the ruling.

Salami tactics: each slice too thin to fight over

Dates and features are from the book; step heights are illustrative. Each new slice (dark gold) stays smaller than a grab that would be fought (dashed), yet the base keeps growing. Thomas Schelling compared salami tactics to a child edging into water after being told to stay out. Each step looks too small to resist, but together they add up to a new reality, which is what happened in the South China Sea.

02Distracted and disoriented

America keeps missing the start of cold wars

The other side declared the rivalry long ago; Washington still talks about "strategic competition".

  • Incoherence over Ukraine. In late 2021 the author put it to Jake Sullivan that the US would not send troops to defend Ukraine yet still backed its eventual NATO membership, which would oblige it to do exactly that. To him this showed a country that no longer knew what it wanted.
  • A cheap success. For under $100 billion, roughly seven weeks of Pentagon spending, the West helped Ukraine wreck Russia's offensive army without US casualties. But "as long as it takes" still has no defined end.
  • Cold War I began in 1920, not 1947. Wilson sent about 13,000 troops into Russia; about 400 died for nothing lasting. The Bolsheviks then treated the West as an enemy for two decades, stealing its industrial secrets while Western firms built their factories. The author sees the parallel with China.
  • China's turn. Tiananmen, the Gulf War and the Soviet collapse convinced Beijing that America was its main threat. It hid its strength, bought cheap "assassin's mace" weapons such as diesel submarines with anti-ship missiles, and secured WTO entry. Its economy went from 10% of America's to 70% in two decades.
  • A new kind of cold war. Xi now speaks of a "great struggle". Alperovitch prefers the label Cold War II over "Cold War 2.0": a distinct contest, not a sequel.
Cold War I (US vs USSR)
  • Little trade between the blocs
  • Moscow exported revolution and ideology
  • Each side expected the other's system to collapse
  • Rigid blocs: West, East, non-aligned
  • America had a clear plan: containment
Cold War II (US vs China)
  • Deeply intertwined economies
  • Beijing seeks influence and silence, not regime change
  • Both assume the other is here to stay
  • Fluid coalitions; even allies hedge
  • "Strategic competition" with no stated end goal
"Russia is a hurricane. China is climate change."
Rob Joyce, NSA, quoted by the author on why Ukraine is a distraction
03The Taiwan dilemma

Why Xi may repeat Putin's gamble

An ageing, isolated leader, a modernised army and a prize slipping away: the conditions that preceded the invasion of Ukraine.

  • Reading Putin. Alperovitch, who grew up in Moscow, sees Putin as the bully from a Soviet apartment courtyard and a gambler whose risks (Chechnya, Georgia, Crimea, Syria) had always paid off. By 2021 he was nearly seventy, isolated by Covid and surrounded by yes-men, and watched Ukraine move west. That is why the author predicted the invasion in public in December 2021.
  • The same checklist for Xi. Xi has also turned seventy, scrapped term limits in 2018, holds the state, party and military top jobs, and has said Taiwan cannot be left to the next generation. Meanwhile Taiwan is moving away: in a 2023 poll only about 1% wanted unification as soon as possible. A Pentagon official told the author China's takeaway from Ukraine is that America won't fight a nuclear power.
  • Five reasons Taiwan matters to Xi: history (lost during the "century of humiliation"), destiny (unfinished civil war), security (fear of US encirclement), geography (the gateway to the Pacific) and ego (achieving what Mao couldn't). Deng's advice to hide strength and bide time has, in the author's paraphrase, become "show off your strength, waste no time".
  • History is on Taiwan's side. The author argues that the Qing loosely held only the western third for two centuries and faced 159 sizeable revolts. Mao himself told Edgar Snow in 1936 that Formosa could have independence. No government has ever ruled the mainland and Taiwan at the same time.
  • A Munich war game. When Taiwan "fell" in a 2023 simulation he ran, players representing US allies said they would now treat China as Asia's leading power. US credibility rests on Taiwan.

Why the island is a cork in China's bottle

Depths are from the book; the profile is schematic. Holding Taiwan would give China's submarines and navy open access to the Pacific and ease its "Malacca dilemma": about 70% of its oil imports pass through a strait that narrows to 1.7 miles and is patrolled by the US Navy. MacArthur called the island an unsinkable aircraft carrier.

04They are weaker

Two rivals running out of road

Russia has crippled itself, and the four things that powered China's rise are all running down.

  • Not ten feet tall. In the 2016 DNC hack, CrowdStrike found two Russian services bumbling through the same network unaware of each other, and their leak sites flopped until WikiLeaks stepped in. Adversaries, the author says, usually get too much credit.
  • Russia as a gangster state. Alperovitch sees Putin as shaped less by the KGB than by 1990s "Bandit Petersburg" racketeering. The war has cost Russia roughly 30% of its military power and over 60% of its 3,500 operational tanks. At the 2023 Victory Day parade a single T-34 crossed Red Square.
  • Russia's demographic decline. Moscow ruled almost 300 million people in 1991 and now has 146 million. By 2100 the population may be 67 million. Stopping the decline would take over a million immigrants a year for eighty years. The author expects it to end up closer to a nuclear-armed North Korea than to a great power.
  • China's four engines are stalling: workers, capital, resources and willing trading partners. Fertility is about 1.3 children per woman, down from 3.6 when the one-child policy began. Real estate is about 30% of GDP (US housing never exceeded 13%), local government debt is around $9 trillion, and income per person is about a sixth of America's ($12,500 against about $70,000).
  • Few friends. China grows only 65% of its own food. The Belt and Road reached 147 countries but left cracked dams and half-built railways; heads of state at its forum fell from 37 in 2019 to 23 in 2023. "Wolf warrior" diplomacy has made China unpopular almost everywhere.

05We are stronger

The Five I's

America's advantages reinforce each other, and its rivals can't copy them.

  • A partnership closer than any alliance. On Christmas Eve 2003, fearing a nuclear attack on Washington, NSA chief Michael Hayden told Britain's GCHQ that if Washington went down, GCHQ would take over. The Five Eyes intelligence-sharing partnership is the author's example of an alliance network no rival has.
  • An immigrant's evidence. Alperovitch left Moscow in 1994 with three suitcases. By forty he had taken a company public. In America, 13% of billionaires are immigrants; Xi sent his only daughter to Harvard; about 300,000 Chinese students come to the US each year.
  • Rule of law vs rule by law. Khodorkovsky lost Yukos and Jack Ma lost Ant Group's record IPO after crossing the ruler. In the US you can raise money on an idea (CrowdStrike raised about $1.1 billion) and sell to one huge market.
  • The dollar isn't going anywhere. Dollar reserves look as if they fell, but the 2000 peak was an anomaly caused by the euro's arrival. The renminbi is not freely convertible and nobody trusts China's currency controls.
  • Influence and ideas. US defence spending equals the next ten militaries combined, mostly allies'. China's last war was against Vietnam in 1979. And a system built for argument adapts better than one ruler.

Part II · Chapters 6–9

A game plan for victory

Four steps: enable American innovation, defend it, make it easy for allies to side with the US, and refuse distractions. The common thread is leverage: increase China's dependence on the West and reduce the West's dependence on China.

06Enable innovation

Everything begins with the chips

Control the machines that make chips, secure the minerals, and attract the best people.

  • How fabrication left America. US fabs made 37% of the world's chips in 1990 and 13% by 2010; today the US share is 12%. Morris Chang's TSMC built a business making chips designed by others. Taiwan now produces nearly 40% of the world's new computing power each year.
  • Old chips still matter. The author proposes calling chips above 20 nm "foundational" rather than "mature" or "legacy". An iPhone has a few advanced chips and dozens of foundational ones, and he compares the latter to aluminium in an aircraft. China, blocked from the cutting edge, is subsidising foundational chips and selling them 20 to over 30% below competitors.
  • Where to squeeze. Only the US, the Netherlands and Japan make the tools for a modern fab. Export controls from 7 October 2022 and 17 October 2023 cut off advanced AI chips and toolmaking, and barred US citizens and green-card holders from working in China's advanced chip industry. He wants controls on all chipmaking equipment, but not on most chips: the aim is to keep China dependent on imports, not to cut it off.
  • Four steps against a Chinese "chip breakout": allied controls on equipment; moving production away from Taiwan (the CHIPS Act's $76 billion is a start); making clear that seizing Taiwan will never deliver TSMC's know-how (otherwise the "silicon shield" becomes a "silicon honeypot"); and threatening a total chip embargo if China invades.
  • Minerals. China mines about 65% of rare earths and processes about 85%, and 96% of exported lithium concentrate goes there for processing. After GM sold Magnequench, maker of smart-bomb magnets, to a Chinese-backed group in 1995, production moved to China. The author wants permitting reform, duties on subsidised imports, clean refineries, recycling and a club of mineral allies.
  • People. Immigrants founded 44 of 87 US unicorns in 2016, yet H-1B visas are capped at 85,000 a year. He would give vetted science graduates a green card, then invest in AI, biotech, space and green tech, and guard AI models against theft.

Six links in the chain, and the one China can't forge

The author's point is that you need all six: having five gets you no closer to a working phone or missile than having one. Software is easy to copy and chemicals can be learned, but extreme-ultraviolet machines rely on Western lasers, German mirrors and constant on-site servicing. He considers copying that whole chain harder than landing a person on Mars.

Source: ch. 6

Why a chip embargo would hurt China far more than Russia

China's chip imports, 2022$417bn
China's crude-oil imports, 2022$360bn
Russia's chip imports, 2021$0.5bn

Russia got around Western bans by buying black-market chips through China. Nobody can smuggle $417 billion of chips. From 2014 to 2020 China's domestic share of its own chip needs only moved from 15.1% to 15.9%.

07Defend innovation

Too expensive, and too few

The US has the world's largest defence budget but can't produce enough munitions for a long war, and it hasn't made China pay for theft.

  • The Javelin math. When Biden visited the Troy, Alabama, Javelin plant in 2022, the roughly 7,000 Javelins sent to Ukraine amounted to a quarter of US stocks and over three years of production. In its first year of war Ukraine fired about 7,700 shells a day, while US plants produced about 14,000 a month.
  • Hooked on exquisite weapons. The F-35 may cost nearly $2 trillion over its life, and planned F-22 purchases fell from 750 to 187. Meanwhile the drone both sides in Ukraine prefer is China's cheap DJI quadcopter.
  • "The Last Supper." At a 1993 Pentagon dinner, defence executives were told to consolidate. Fifty-one aerospace firms became five, and defence-industry jobs fell from 3 million in 1985 to about 1.1 million in 2021. Today two firms make missile rocket motors and one makes most cruise-missile engines.
  • Taiwan can't be resupplied like Ukraine. CSIS estimates a war would use 5,000 missiles in three weeks. Taiwan is waiting on a $19 billion backlog, including Javelins and Stingers ordered in December 2015. The author's priorities: munitions stockpiled on the island, more attack submarines, hardened bases in Japan and Guam, and forces trained to fight without GPS.
  • Make theft costly. In his account, Chinese intelligence stole parts designs for the C919 airliner from Western suppliers. His remedy is to sanction the beneficiaries, even entire industries, and to set clear cyber norms. In 2016 the US indicted the Russian team that leaked DNC emails but not the one that only spied, because the problem was releasing the material, not taking it.
  • Trade tools. In the author's view, admitting China to the WTO was the biggest mistake, because its rules assume market economies. He recommends US tariff powers (Sections 301, 201 and 232), an "IPEF+" bloc without China, and reciprocity: with US platforms banned in China, TikTok should not reach about 150 million Americans.

Every three weeks Ukraine used as much ammunition as the US normally uses in a year. Orders take 13 to 18 months to start arriving.

The navy plans to buy 64 hypersonic rounds. Enough cheap missiles can overwhelm any air defence, and the US already has over 4,000 Tomahawks.

08Say yes to our friends

Make it safe to stand with America

China punishes allies one at a time. The US should defend them together, and ask more of them in return.

  • Who pays for helping. After Canada arrested Huawei's CFO at US request, China detained "the two Michaels", Kovrig and Spavor, for 1,019 days. Earlier, the Garratts, a Canadian couple running a café in Dandong, were seized when Canada held a Chinese spy for the FBI. The US gave Canada little more than sympathy.
  • Economic bullying. Australia called for an inquiry into Covid's origins, and China hit about $13 billion of its exports: rock lobsters (94% of which had gone to China), barley, timber, and wine with tariffs up to 212%. Norway's salmon, Taiwan's pineapples and Lithuania's chip-containing products were also targeted. Washington did not organise any airlift of pineapples.
  • Tough love for Taiwan. Taiwan has 23 million people and terrain made for defence, but has spent on F-16s that need intact runways, M1 tanks and a $1.5 billion home-built submarine. Until 2024 conscription lasted four months, and in 2022 45% were not at all or only slightly worried about invasion. The author wants a "porcupine": anti-ship missiles, mines, drones, trained reservists, buried munitions, and ports ready to wreck, as Ukraine wrecked the Hostomel runways.
  • Strong medicine for Europe. Many European leaders privately accept that China will take Taiwan. He argues that they would have no choice but to cut ties with China the moment Americans and Japanese started dying in the Pacific, so deterrence is in their interest too.
  • Get the undecided off the fence. Court India as an equal, invest in Indonesia, deepen AUKUS and the Quad, and pitch predictable rules against Beijing's whims rather than democracy against autocracy.

TAME: an Article 5 for trade

Australia's coal shows step 3b can work: after China's ban, the coal went to buyers who had lost Russian and Indonesian supply, a reshuffle rather than a loss. The author sees Australia, Japan, Lithuania, Norway, Sweden, the Czech Republic, the Philippines and Taiwan as likely early members. As with NATO, the best sanctions are the ones never used, and the alliance only happens if the US commits first.

Source: ch. 8

09Say no to distractions

One test for every other crisis

Ask of each crisis: does this help contain China, or pull attention away from it?

  • The pivot that never was. Obama's 2011 "Pivot to Asia" was derailed by Syria, Crimea and ISIS; Biden's by Ukraine and the Hamas attack on Israel. Top-level attention is finite and must be rationed.
  • Russia: aim for neutrality, not friendship. Russian imperial ambitions will outlast Putin, Alperovitch argues, but becoming China's junior partner will gall Russian elites who see themselves as European. Keep arming Ukraine and its neighbours, but offer Moscow a roadmap: an end to aggression, reparations, then gradual sanctions relief. The goal is a Russia standing apart from both blocs, not a "reverse Kissinger" alliance against China.
  • North Korea: accept the bomb, lower the temperature. North Korea has an estimated fifty warheads and will not give them up after seeing what happened to Saddam and Gaddafi. Trade economic integration for verifiable limits on proliferation, testing and the DMZ.
  • Iran: delay and deter. Iran is one order away from a bomb. Slow it down by diplomacy, prepare to deter it, perhaps with a defence pact for Saudi Arabia, and above all stop the know-how spreading further.
  • Let China spend its own attention. When Beijing brokered a Saudi–Iranian rapprochement in 2023, the author welcomed it, since time it spends there is time it doesn't spend on Taiwan.
RussiaContain and waitUntil Moscow decides that being China's vassal state is a worse deal than standing on its own.
IranDelay the thresholdHold off the bomb, counter its regional aggression, and prevent a regional nuclear arms race.
North KoreaDeter and curbProtect South Korea and Japan, and stop arms trafficking, cyber-theft and proliferation.
Conclusion

Taiwan is the Berlin of Cold War II

In 1961 a US ambassador put the odds of war or a humiliating Western retreat over Berlin at close to 50–50. Then Khrushchev built a wall, and Kennedy understood that the Soviets had given up on taking the city.

The author argues that without Taiwan the US–China rivalry would be manageable: trade, cyber and human-rights disputes that are serious but unlikely to lead to war. So the job is the one Truman, Eisenhower and Kennedy did with Berlin: hold the outpost and avoid a general war until a stable arrangement emerges.

He names two worst cases. One is war between nuclear powers that reaches US territory. The other is China taking Taiwan without a fight, leaving Asia to accommodate Beijing and making later wars over other disputed territory more likely.

Victory, in his account, does not mean the collapse of the Communist Party. It means delay: buying Taiwan time to arm itself, hoping Xi's successor is more cautious, and arriving at a cold but stable relationship like the one Kennedy's successors had with Moscow. His version of "speak softly and carry a big stick" is to tone down the rhetoric and step up deterrence.

"A wall is a hell of a lot better than a war."
John F. Kennedy, August 1961, quoted in the conclusion

Unidirectional entanglement: decouple in one direction only

The author argues that full decoupling would be unrealistic and would make China freer to ignore Western interests. Instead he wants one-way entanglement: China more dependent on Western supply chains, the West less dependent on China. He expects Beijing to try the mirror-image strategy, and thinks whoever wins that race will shape the century.

Source: conclusion and ch. 6

Takeaways

The toolkit

Alperovitch's prescriptions in fifteen moves. They are his argument, not settled consensus.

Read the rival

Study the who before the howAsk about motive, fear and ego before looking at weapons or code. The problem is the adversary, not the malware.
Believe what leaders sayPutin published his case for invading Ukraine in advance. Xi talks openly of a "great struggle" and of rejuvenation.
Watch for closing windowsAgeing leaders, a prize slipping away and a rebuilt army together are a warning sign.
Count the slicesSmall encroachments add up. Respond before the reef has a runway.
Don't inflate the threatClumsy hackers and a struggling army are not superpowers. Weigh demography and debt alongside headlines.

Build leverage

Control the tools, not the productsBan chipmaking equipment while still selling most chips, so China stays dependent on the West.
Buy volume, not only exquisite weaponsMines, anti-ship missiles, shells and drones are what deter a crossing, and they must be pre-positioned.
Punish the beneficiarySanction the companies and industries that profit from stolen intellectual property, not just the hackers.
Recruit talentOffer a green card to every vetted foreign science graduate of a US university.
Insist on reciprocityOpenness should be matched. Access to the US market should depend on access to China's.

Hold the line

Back friends who take the hitTreat coercion of one ally as aimed at all, and compensate the industries China punishes.
Ask allies to do moreTaiwan must become a porcupine, and Europe must see that a war over Taiwan would hit it too.
Run every crisis through one filterDoes it help deter China? If not, share the burden or set it aside.
Use time as the weaponA delay of a month, a year or a leader's term is a win. Patience won Cold War I.
Speak softly, deter hardAvoid gestures that provoke without deterring, and do the quiet military and economic work instead.
