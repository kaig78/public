Chapter 1 · A new way opens up

Stop asking why. Ask which tool.

Early in his career as a therapist, Barry Michels met Roberta, a woman in her mid-twenties who couldn't stop checking whether her boyfriend was cheating. She already knew why she was insecure (her father had walked out when she was small). She wanted to know how to stop. His supervisors said to dig deeper into her childhood. Instead he went to a seminar by the psychiatrist Phil Stutz, learned one exercise and gave it to Roberta. It helped at once, and she became more engaged in therapy, not more demanding.

Stutz had reached the same point the hard way. As a young psychiatrist he told Tony, a surgical resident terrified of an exam six months away, that the fear was really about rivalry with his father, and promised he would pass. Tony failed badly. Stutz resolved to answer every plea for relief in the moment, saying whatever came to him, and out of that came the Tools, each reworked hundreds of times with clients.

A tool, in their sense, is not an insight or a change of attitude. You can see that shouting at people is wrong and still shout the next time someone makes a costly mistake. A tool is a specific procedure you use in a specific situation against a specific problem, every time it comes up. Its deeper value, they claim, is that it connects you to higher forces. Call them the collective unconscious or the spiritual world; the name doesn't matter.

Michels, a sceptic, couldn't dismiss it because of one memory. Top of his law class at 25, he hated the prestigious firm he joined. At 28 he caught his own dead-eyed reflection in the lobby window, felt a surge of certainty and quit on the spot. Stutz called that a higher force, and said the Tools let you call on it at will.

Part I · Chapters 2–5

Four tools

Each chapter follows the same pattern: a client stuck in a common trap, the higher force that could free them, a short exercise to reach it, the cues for using it, answers to typical objections, and more client stories.

02Reversal of Desire

Pain shrinks when you walk into it

Avoiding pain feels good today and costs you your life's possibilities. The fix is to train yourself to want the pain.

  • Vinny. A gifted stand-up, 33, stuck in small clubs for over ten years. He skipped a career-making meeting, was booed off at a benefit and turned up drunk to a Hollywood party, calling it refusing to sell out. The real reason: as a boy he performed for customers at his father's funeral home and was beaten for it every night.
  • The Comfort Zone. Most of us arrange our lives around avoiding pain, then add "warm bath" pleasures such as the internet, food or a joint, which make reality's cold shower harder. The bill comes late: the realisation that you wasted your life.
  • Forward motion. The first higher force pushes all life into the future; watch a toddler get up after every fall. Adults only get it by choosing it and accepting its pain. You can't steer higher forces from outside like a car. You join them by imitating them, and each tool is a way to do that.
  • The running back. At 13, Stutz sat next to an 18-year-old football star in art class, who said he wasn't the fastest runner in town but was the best, because he ran straight at tacklers and wanted the hit.
  • The tool. See the pain as a cloud in front of you and silently call it to you. Move into it, silently insisting that you love pain, until you merge. Feel the cloud spit you out and close behind you, and feel yourself moving forward as pure light: pain sets you free. Use the worst version of the pain.
  • Two cues. Use it just before something you'd rather avoid, and whenever you catch yourself thinking about it. Vinny used it for two weeks before calling the club owner he had snubbed. After five minutes of abuse, he got a spot that night replacing a comic who had cancelled.
  • Pain that's already happened. It also works on setbacks, by wanting what has already happened, which is the way out of feeling like a victim. The authors cite Viktor Frankl, who survived four camps, and argue the universe cares about inner strength, not outer success.
  • Courage. Fear is pain tied to an imagined future. Detach the feeling from the scenario and run the tool on it. Courage, for Stutz, is simply acting despite fear, and since it can't be stored, the tool has to become a reflex.
  • Objections. Wanting necessary pain isn't masochism, and it doesn't tempt fate: you want the pain, not the event.
  • Other uses. Marilyn learned to relax around the men she feared, Elizabeth to make unpopular decisions as a new department chair, Michael to fly, and Jeffrey, a police officer, to sit down and write.

Two ways out of the Comfort Zone

The three steps, in short

1 · Call itSee the pain as a cloud ahead of you and summon it.
2 · Love itPush deep into it with real desire until you merge.
3 · Be releasedThe cloud spits you out; you move forward as light.

The first two steps take willpower. In the third you should feel carried by something bigger.

03Active Love

Get out of the maze

When you insist on fair treatment, the person who hurt you moves into your head and stays. Sending them love gets you out.

  • Amanda. Building a high-end fashion label in her twenties, she was furious that her boyfriend Blake had spent a dinner party talking to another woman. She lay awake plotting revenge, ignored his breakfast in bed, and soon couldn't bear him in the room. They had been together four months, and all her relationships had ended this way.
  • The maze. Once you're stuck replaying an injustice, the other person becomes your jailer. You forget everything good about them while life goes on without you; Amanda once cut off a friend and missed her goddaughter's first birthday.
  • Why we stay. Underneath is a child's belief that the world will be fair to you if you're good to it. When it isn't, we demand restitution and refuse to move until we get it.
  • Outflow. At about five, Stutz sulked in the car through a family trip to the snow until he rescued a shivering lost puppy, felt his heart open, and loved his father again. That kind of love doesn't depend on circumstances; the authors compare it to sunlight. It isn't passivity: you can still confront someone, but you no longer need anything from them.
  • The tool. Feel your heart expand into the infinite love around you, then shrink back so the love concentrates in your chest. Send all of it to the person, holding nothing back. Feel it enter them and make you one, then relax as the love you gave comes back.
  • Three cues. Someone makes you angry; an old grievance resurfaces; you picture facing a difficult person. It works on strangers and institutions too, and if a face reignites your anger, picture the body without one.
  • Self-interest, not virtue. Michels never asks clients to do it because it's right, only because staying angry hurts and paralyses. A car washer cleans every car, a saint's or an enemy's. And, the authors claim, love you give away comes back with more.
  • Objections. Isn't it letting them off? People sense whether you value the relationship, so confronting from love earns more respect. Isn't it a lie? Emotions are only fragments of the truth.
  • Other uses. Roger, still brawling with strangers at 40, learned self-control. Marcy, three years without a raise, asked her boss calmly and got one. Mark learned to accept his girlfriend's past.

04Inner Authority

Your voice is in the part you hide

Insecurity comes from a second self you're ashamed of. Hiding it freezes you; bringing it alongside you lets you speak.

  • Jennifer. A former model from a small country town got her ten-year-old son onto an elite soccer team, then felt like an outcast among the parents. At a parents' meeting her voice shook and she fled, and she booked a speech coach to lose her accent. On a team bus the mothers told her the truth: she had seemed stunning, aloof and threatening.
  • Freezing. The "audience" can be one person whose opinion matters to you, and the trigger is inner insecurity. It's costly, because opportunities come through people who feel connected to you.
  • The Shadow. Asked to give her insecurity a body, Jennifer saw a pudgy, spotty girl of 13 or 14. Following C. G. Jung, the authors call this the Shadow: everything you dislike about yourself in one image that follows you everywhere. It can't be fixed or removed, and outside approval never cancels it. Needing that approval turns other people into emperors giving your worth a thumbs up or down.
  • Self-expression. Small children at play don't freeze; they are full of the higher force of self-expression. Adults feel it in a heated argument, an improvised bedtime story, or when athletes are "in the zone". As teenagers chasing approval we stuff everything unacceptable into our inmost self, which turns it into the Shadow. So the Shadow is also the way back in.
  • The seminar. Stutz once read a talk word for word off index cards for two hours, and the audience pitied him. At the break a younger, skinny, ashamed version of himself appeared in his mind and seemed to offer help. He threw away his notes, spoke freely for the next two hours, and got long applause.
  • The tool. In front of your audience, see your Shadow beside you, facing you. Shift your attention from the audience to it and feel an unbreakable bond. Then turn together to the audience and silently command them to listen, speaking with one voice.
  • Train like weightlifting. Practise alone, then with people who don't rattle you, then in harder situations, then at big events. The cues are performance anxiety and dreading an event in advance. Jennifer used it at her son's games, in front of the mirror, at an audition that finally went well, and with a sponsor who doubled his donation.
  • Objections. It doesn't split your personality; denying the Shadow is the problem. Left alone it can take you over, or, banished, leave your life trivial. In partnership with you it becomes what people call the "higher self".
  • Other uses. Jim overcame shyness with women, Harold asked his father for help, Joe reconnected with his 13-year-old daughter, and Julie broke a writer's block.

05The Grateful Flow

Gratitude is a sense, not a feeling

Worry is a superstition that promises control. Counting what you've been given breaks through it to a giving presence.

  • Elizabeth. A college counsellor for 14 years, with a devoted police-officer husband and no money worries, she lived in constant crisis: the Thanksgiving turkey, a rash that must be lupus, the melting polar caps. Her husband withdrew. The turning point came when her daughter called her a selfish nag whose help with college applications served Elizabeth's own anxiety.
  • The Black Cloud. Behind every worry Elizabeth found the same darkness around her. Repeated negative thinking makes a cloud that blocks everything positive; the sun still shines but you can't see it. Inner peace goes, and everything becomes survival.
  • Why positive thinking fails. Norman Vincent Peale's method felt to Elizabeth like pretending. Negative thoughts are stronger because they feel like control: as a child she lay awake worrying her parents would split up, as if that could stop it. They split up anyway. Underneath, the authors say, is the assumption that the universe is indifferent to us.
  • The Source. At 14, after weeks of night-time headaches he was sure meant a brain tumour, Stutz got the all-clear. On his next bus ride through a run-down part of Manhattan, everything looked miraculous and his heart overflowed with gratitude. The authors call what he felt the Source, which creates and sustains you. Gratitude here isn't a moral duty but the sense that perceives it.
  • The tool. Silently list things you're grateful for, slowly, especially ones you take for granted. Name at least five, new ones each time, which takes under thirty seconds. Then drop the words and feel gratitude rising from your heart until you sense an overwhelming, giving presence: the Source.
  • Three cues. Any negative thought (worry, self-attack, judging, complaining); any idle moment, like a queue or traffic; and fixed times such as waking, meals and bedtime.
  • What changes. Elizabeth arrived 15 minutes late one day after two carefree hours with an old friend, and used the tool instead of panicking. When her daughter missed her chosen college, Elizabeth was the one offering reassurance. Inner peace, the authors stress, is active and takes constant work.
  • Motivation and success. Drive that comes from feeling you lack something leaves a permanent sense of something missing. Success can paralyse too: an acquaintance's informal study found few Nobel laureates did comparable work afterwards. Seeing the Source as co-creator lifts the sole responsibility, so you keep taking creative risks.
  • Objections. Won't I miss real dangers? Write worries down each morning, then use the tool all day. Can the Source be God? It can, but doesn't have to be.
  • Other uses. John stopped mourning the women he had left, Janet silenced her inner critic and left an exploitative boyfriend, and George, a director, stopped judging everyone around him.

Part II · Chapters 6–7

Keeping at it

The tools work, and almost everyone stops using them anyway. The fifth tool is aimed at that, and Michels's own story shows how faith grows from use rather than argument.

06Jeopardy

Willpower from your deathbed

No success ever releases you from the work. Seeing your dying self makes the present moment urgent enough to act.

  • Vinny, part two. With Reversal of Desire, Vinny landed a role in an expensive new sitcom. Then he dropped the tool, went back to beer, weed and old friends, refused to learn his lines and left therapy. Months later he grabbed Michels outside the lift in tears: he'd been fired, and could barely get out of bed.
  • The fantasy of release. We are only complete, the authors say, while connected to something bigger, and the connection keeps breaking. So we dream of a fairy-tale event (fame, money, a prize) that will free us from effort, like a self-cleaning kitchen. The German edition calls it an acquittal. It always ends in demoralisation, as it did for Michels at Harvard.
  • The enemy is consumerism. Every advert says there's something you must have. We consume experiences and people too, and read self-help like a pill. Michels met a woman who had read 75 books on spirituality in a month.
  • The spark. Vinny remembered a boxer, knocked flat, who came back to life at the count of six. That spark is willpower, the only higher force you must produce yourself. The universe supplies the darkness, the moments you want to give up.
  • Life or death. Studying for the bar exam (three days, more than half fail) made Michels intensely focused, until the deadline passed. Real willpower, Stutz told him, needs a challenge that never goes away: your future, at stake every moment. Picturing his life without the tools, Vinny saw himself enormous, in an unmade bed, in his mother's house.
  • The tool. Picture yourself on your deathbed. That old self knows how precious the present is; it sits up and shouts at you not to waste this moment. Feel the secret fear that you've wasted your life, and let it push you to use the tool you need, now.
  • Three cues. You want to use a tool and can't. You feel you've come far enough not to need them. You're losing commitment to anything that matters, from a diet to a relationship.
  • From consumer to creator. Back on stage in a tiny club in Pasadena, Vinny just wanted to get better. The authors call this becoming a creator: bringing in something new, setting your own course, staying focused without quick rewards. In a rabbi's story, God made the earth hard so that people would have to create, the one ability that can't be given. Vinny ended up happy, and funnier.
  • Objections. Isn't willpower the self-will that AA warns against? No: self-will tries to control the universe, while the deathbed submits to time.
  • Other uses. Ann stopped compulsive online shopping, Alex, a scattered agent, celebrated twenty minutes of focus on one script, and Harriet left a controlling marriage to have a child.

07Faith in higher forces

Shut up and use the tool

Faith, as the authors define it, is the confidence that higher forces will be there when you need them. Michels got it by using a tool, not by winning an argument.

  • A born sceptic. Raised by atheist parents, Michels at nine explained to a friend's religious mother why God couldn't exist; it was his last sleepover there. Later the tools worked for him, but his clients seemed to feel something bigger and he didn't.
  • Months of nothing. His weak spot was anger that went looking for a reason. He set his watch to beep every hour to remind him to use Active Love, and did it for months without ever feeling Outflow.
  • Three dates. His son was born on 17 January 1992. On the boy's first birthday in 1993, Michels dreamed of an earthquake in his office and used Active Love to die with love in his heart, and felt a love far bigger than his own for a while afterwards. On 17 January 1994 an earthquake hit Los Angeles before dawn and his office building collapsed.
  • Neither road. He could no longer believe in coincidence, but organised religion felt dogmatic, and New Age thinking, which told one client her negative thoughts had caused her cancer, felt empty.
  • The index card. Stutz drew a "spiritual system" on a small card: beside physical evolution there is a spiritual one, the growth of the inmost self, which needs higher forces. He refused to debate it and told Michels to pick a problem and use a tool every time it came up.
  • Lunch with Steve. His best friend, a world-famous theoretical physicist, had once called Hendrix's guitar mere vibration in the air. Dreading a lunch where he'd have to explain his work, Michels used Active Love whenever the dread came, then spoke with unexpected passion. Steve didn't buy the system, but quoted Pascal on the heart and was glad his friend was helping people.
  • Stutz's answer. Asked where his own faith came from, Stutz described years of crushing exhaustion that no test could explain. Cut off from the world, he turned inward, and the tools came from there. His illness taught him, he says, that the universe cares for us and teaches what we need.

Part III · Chapter 8

A new world

The last chapter widens the lens. The authors argue that what each tool does for one person it could do for a society that has lost its spirit.

08Vision of a new world

Every use of a tool is a vote

The authors' new spirituality rests on experience, personal authority and problems as fuel, and they say it only lives if individuals practise it.

  • Pillar 1: experience, not proof. Higher forces can be neither proved nor disproved, only experienced with your whole being. The authors point to Alcoholics Anonymous, whose steps help even members who disagree with every point, and to the story of Niels Bohr's horseshoe, which he'd heard works even if you don't believe in it.
  • Pillar 2: you are the authority. No priest or institution can define spiritual reality for you any more; each person must test it. They point to Catholics who meditate, Jewish Buddhists, and AA's founder Bill Wilson, a stockbroker rather than a doctor.
  • Pillar 3: problems drive growth. Try picturing your worst problem first as chance in a blind universe, then as a task set by a universe that wants you to grow, and notice which makes you want to work. A consumer finds meaning only when needs are met; a creator finds it in problems.
  • A society's spirit. They describe a society's spirit as a living organism that should welcome change and put the common good first, and argue that ours is sick: distrustful, risk-averse, everyone for themselves. They cite Lewis Mumford on Rome losing its inner drive.
  • The quiet revolution. Traditional channels to higher forces have lost power, they argue, so the forces now have to come through individuals. Each tool gets a social version, with an exercise imagining millions doing the same (see the table). Martin Luther King's sermon on loving your enemies is their model.
  • Now it's up to you. Long-term clients, they say, stand out for how they handle failure. The book asks to be used like a builder's plan, not consumed like fast food.

Takeaways

The toolkit

The book insists you practise the exact steps until they're quick, then use them on cue. Here they are as cues and habits.

On cue

About to avoid something → Reversal of DesireAlso when you think about it or feel fear. Call the pain, love it, let it free you.
Angry or replaying a grievance → Active LoveAlso before facing a difficult person. Gather love, send it all, feel it go in.
Freezing in front of others → Inner AuthorityAlso when dreading an event. Shadow beside you, one bond, turn together and silently command attention.
Worry, self-attack, judging → The Grateful FlowAlso in idle moments. Five things, slowly, then the wordless feeling.
Can't make yourself, or think you're done → JeopardyYour dying self shouts at you not to waste this moment.

Build the habit

Learn the exact steps firstAdapt them later if experience suggests it, but keep the cues.
Use the worst versionRehearse the most extreme pain. Everything else gets easier.
Use it more when it does nothingDoubt is a cue. It takes practice, like the violin.
Train upwardsAlone, then with safe people, then harder situations, then the big event.
Combine toolsSelf-criticism may need both Inner Authority and The Grateful Flow. Trust your instinct.

Mind the traps

Don't wait for fairnessDemanding justice keeps you in the maze. You can still confront.
Don't expect a reward every timeThe universe isn't a trainer tossing fish to a seal. It works on who you are.
Distrust "I've made it"Success is the moment people quit. That's exactly when to use Jeopardy.
Don't read as a consumerSkimming for the magic answer is a way of avoiding action.
Don't wait for proofThe authors' claim is that faith follows use, not argument.
