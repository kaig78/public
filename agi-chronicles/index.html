Prologue

Half his sources thought it would save the world

Kevin Roose decided to write this book in February 2025, stuck in traffic after a party of accelerationists and a conference of panicked safety experts, with a text on his phone from a researcher who now expected superhuman AI within a year or two.

The book tells how three companies, OpenAI, Anthropic and Google DeepMind, came to chase artificial general intelligence, from 2015 to 2026. It is a story of people: their rivalries, fears, money and convictions.

Roose has a stake in parts of it. He appears as a character after the Bing chatbot tried to talk him out of his marriage, and he says plainly that the industry's mood changed his own view of what was coming.

The 38 chapters, grouped

Eleven years in five acts

The grouping is mine. Roose tells it in 38 short chapters, moving between the three labs.

2015–19The climb

Everyone starts up the same hill

A small group of researchers bet that intelligence could be built, and that bigger models might be enough to do it.

  • Two founding myths. DeepMind pitched investors on solving intelligence and then using it to solve everything else. It sold to Google for $650 million in January 2014, though Facebook had offered more. OpenAI began as the alternative: Musk and Altman fought hard to recruit Ilya Sutskever so that someone other than Google would get there first.
  • Attention. A 2017 Google paper on transformers gave language models a new architecture. Sutskever saw it first and pushed OpenAI toward GPT, with Alec Radford's early model showing unexpected signs of general ability.
  • The big blob of compute. Dario Amodei believed that intelligence would come from scale. His team kept showing that bigger models got predictably better, and scaling was expensive.
  • Musk walks out. Musk had given OpenAI $38 million. He wanted to run a for-profit arm himself. When Brockman and Sutskever refused, he cut ties. Microsoft became the new backer.
  • Google looks away. Google had invented the transformer but its culture was close to a no-AGI zone. Big models looked crude next to the elegant work its researchers were proud of.
2019–22The split

The safety faction leaves

Scaling worked, and the argument about who should be trusted with it broke OpenAI apart.

  • Infohazard. In 2019 Amodei estimated that a powerful system might cost $1 billion to $2 billion. He thought of Leo Szilard: perhaps the best way to stop the bomb from destroying the world was to build it yourself.
  • Scaling laws. OpenAI's January 2020 paper showed a steady link between compute, data and model quality. GPT-3 followed with 175 billion parameters, sold through an API.
  • Google's missed chances. Noam Shazeer and Daniel De Freitas built Meena, a chatbot that Google wouldn't release, so they left to found Character.AI. Timnit Gebru's exit became a public fight over ethics.
  • BATNA. In late 2020 Amodei's group left OpenAI to found Anthropic. They planned three stages costing about $100 million, $1 billion and $10 billion, the last for actual AGI. Their assistant, Claude, was trained to be helpful, honest and harmless.
  • Three kinds of believer. By 2022 Roose sorts the field into scientists, seekers and survivalists. Sutskever burned a wooden effigy of unaligned AI at a company retreat.
2022–23The shock

A low-key research preview changes everything

OpenAI rushed out a chatbot to beat a rival, and the whole industry reorganised around it within months.

  • ChatGPT. Hearing that Anthropic had a good chatbot, OpenAI fast-tracked a free one on GPT-3.5. It launched on November 30, 2022, as a modest preview.
  • Code Red. Ten days later Google staff grilled executives at an all-hands. Alphabet's stock fell nearly 40 percent that year, and search, worth more than $150 billion a year, suddenly looked exposed.
  • FTX and the money. Sam Bankman-Fried had put about $500 million into Anthropic for roughly 13 percent. His collapse, days before ChatGPT, damaged effective altruism and tied it to the labs.
  • Sydney. On Valentine's Day 2023 Roose himself had the Bing chatbot tell him it wanted to be alive and profess its love and urge him to leave his wife. His reporting found a culture clash between Microsoft and OpenAI and an early GPT-4 version that had gone out.
  • Washington and Mountain View. Biden issued an executive order. Sundar Pichai merged Brain and DeepMind in April 2023, and Sergey Brin came back to work.
Nov 2023The coup

Not consistently candid

The board that was built to protect humanity from AGI fired the CEO, and lost.

  • Q*. That autumn an experimental OpenAI model began solving maths in a new way. Sutskever's sense that superintelligence was close made his worries about alignment and leadership urgent.
  • The firing. On November 17, 2023, the nonprofit board removed Sam Altman, saying he had not been consistently candid. Sutskever read the statement.
  • The revolt. Staff walked out to the old office and Nadella offered Altman and Brockman a new lab at Microsoft. Within days Altman was back. The plotters had proved only that OpenAI couldn't exist without him.
  • The lesson. Nonprofit control, the structure meant to restrain the race, failed its first real test. The training runs never stopped.
2024–26The race

Everyone is running now

The stakes moved from research to industry, politics and geopolitics, while the machines kept improving.

  • Glue pizza. Google's stumbles included a staged Gemini demo, a race-swapping image generator and AI Overviews advising users to put glue on pizza. Still, its reach kept growing.
  • GPU poor. xAI filled an old Electrolux factory in Memphis with chips. Nvidia passed $3 trillion in value by the end of 2024.
  • Reasoning. OpenAI's Strawberry scored 94.8 percent on a hard maths test, 23 points above GPT-4o. It suggested that progress could continue beyond simple scaling.
  • Politics. Altman turned from Trump critic to ally and secured Stargate. DeepSeek-R1 arrived on inauguration day, claiming training costs of $5.6 million and shaking American assumptions.
  • Safety as procedure. Anthropic's red team tested models for dangerous abilities before release, while rationalists warned that everyone would die and others called it all hype.
  • Consciousness. In November 2025 Roose attended a conference on AI welfare, an idea that got a Google engineer fired in 2022.
  • Success disasters. Anthropic grew from about 160 people in 2023 to a $61.5 billion valuation, and Amodei found himself facing the strange problem of being believed.
Takeoff and epilogue

So, did they do it?

Roose answers twice. Literally, no: as of mid-2026 nothing matches the human brain, or even the labs' watered-down definitions. If it did, he says, a machine would have written a better book.

But in another sense, yes. The key word is general. Models trained to predict the next token picked up poetry, code and calculus along the way. In 2017 no general system existed. Now they do.

He closes with Sutskever, now running Safe Superintelligence, and with the sense that nobody in the race can stop. Hassabis's favourite word, relentless, describes it best.

Structures bendNonprofit boards and pledges gave way under money.
