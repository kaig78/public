Introduction

The old stories have collapsed, and engineers won't wait for new ones

Harari's starting point is that humans think in stories, and that the big story of recent decades, the liberal one, is losing credibility at the worst possible moment. Information technology and biotechnology are about to give a few people the power to redesign bodies, minds and life itself.

The book is a set of lessons, many written in reply to questions from readers and journalists, and they don't end in simple answers. Harari deliberately stresses technology's dangers, because its makers already sing its praises.

He also explains why he criticises liberal democracy so much: he thinks it's the most successful and versatile political model yet, and that it can only be repaired if it can be examined. He knows autocrats may hijack such criticism, but he chose open discussion over self-censorship.

His reason for hurry is plain. Philosophers can argue about life's meaning indefinitely. Investors can't, and market forces will supply an answer if nobody else does.

The argument in five moves
I · TechnologyThe merger of infotech and biotech threatens liberty and equality.
II · PoliticsThe fix must be global, but nations and religions divide us.
III · Despair and hopeWe can cope if we control our fears and stay humble.
IV · TruthThe world is too complex for any individual to grasp, so we need to know our limits.
V · ResilienceWith no story left to lean on, learn to observe reality directly.
“In a world deluged by irrelevant information, clarity is power.”
Harari, opening line of the introduction

Part I · Chapters 1–4

The Technological Challenge

People are losing faith in the liberal story just as two linked revolutions pose the hardest test our species has met.

01Disillusionment

From three stories to none

Liberalism has survived three crises by absorbing its rivals' best ideas. This time, Harari argues, it has no answer to the two biggest problems.

3

1938: fascism, communism, liberalism

2

1968: communism, liberalism

1

1998: liberalism alone

0

2018: no story left standing

  • The liberal story says that more freedom in politics, markets and borders brings peace and prosperity. Harari dates the disillusionment to the 2008 financial crisis, reaching the Western core with Brexit and Trump in 2016.
  • Liberalism came through the Franz Ferdinand, Hitler and Che Guevara moments, learning from communism to value equality and welfare. What he calls the Trump moment is different: nihilistic, with no vision for humanity.
  • In his reading, neither Russia's oligarchic model nor global Islam offers an attractive alternative. The vacuum is filling with nostalgia for a local golden past.
  • Liberalism relied on a growing pie to settle conflicts. But growth causes the ecological crisis and depends on ever more disruptive technology.
“Panic is a form of hubris.”
Harari: bewilderment is humbler, and so sees more clearly
02Work

The risk isn't exploitation but irrelevance

Machines used to compete with our muscles. Now they compete with our cognition, and we know of no third field where humans keep a secure edge.

  • AI's rise depends on biology as well as computing. If intuition is pattern recognition, computers can learn to read people too. Hence drivers, bankers and lawyers are exposed.
  • AI also has two inhuman abilities, connectivity and updatability. Compare a network with a collection of individuals, not one robot with one worker.
  • The benefits could be huge. Harari says replacing human drivers could save a million of the roughly 1.25 million road deaths each year. His principle: protect humans, not jobs.
  • Care and creativity will resist longest, but no job is safe forever. Expect a cascade of disruptions, not one shock.
  • Universal basic income or services run into two puzzles. What is "universal", when the victims may be in Dhaka rather than Finland? And what is "basic"?
  • Harari's possible model for a post-work life is Israel, where about half of ultra-Orthodox men never work, and they report unusually high life satisfaction thanks to community and meaning.

Why retraining may stop working

The result could be high unemployment alongside a shortage of skilled labour: what Harari calls a useless class. The years are the book's examples.

03Liberty

When algorithms know you better than you do

Liberalism rests authority on human feelings. Harari argues that once feelings can be hacked, democracy risks becoming an emotional puppet show.

  • He treats feelings as biochemical calculations. They were the best guide available only because no outsider could read them. Biometric sensors and Big Data could change that.
  • Algorithms needn't be perfect, just better on average than people, who often err in big decisions. Life as a drama of decision-making would then fade. Imagine Hamlet consulting an app.
  • Ethics becomes engineering. Humans under stress ignore their principles: most Princeton seminarians, hurrying in 1970 to speak on the Good Samaritan, passed a man in distress. A car will follow its code, so someone must write that code.
  • The danger of robots is that they obey. A robot army would have made Srebrenica worse, and a dictator's soldiers could never defect.
  • Democracies once out-processed dictatorships. AI may reverse this, because it improves with centralised data.
  • Harari warns of individual discrimination: refused by an algorithm that nobody can explain, with no group to protest alongside.
  • Intelligence isn't consciousness. A robot rebellion is unlikely soon. Bots that press our emotional buttons are the likelier threat, so invest a dollar in human consciousness for each one spent on AI.

How authority could drift to algorithms

An illustration of Harari's mechanism, not data. In March 2012, he says, three tourists followed their GPS into the Pacific.

04Equality

Who owns the data owns the future

Industry made the masses valuable, which drove the twentieth century's move toward equality. AI and bioengineering could undo that logic.

  • Harari reports that half of all wealth belongs to the top 1 percent, and that the richest hundred people out-own the poorest four billion.
  • If life extension and upgrades are expensive, economic inequality could become biological: real differences in ability, which would be nearly impossible to close.
  • He describes data giants as attention merchants aiming higher: we are the product, and the data may be worth more than the advertising.
  • Nationalising data risks digital dictatorship, and private ownership is hard to define, since data can be copied endlessly. He calls this perhaps the key political question of the era.

Each age fights over its main asset

AncientLandSociety splits into aristocrats and commoners.
ModernMachinesSociety splits into capitalists and proletarians.
21st centuryDataHumankind could split into different species.
Part II · Chapters 5–9

The Political Challenge

Any solution needs global cooperation. Harari tests each candidate in turn: online community, a clash of civilizations, the nation, religion, and Europe's experience of immigration.

05Community

Humans have bodies

Facebook's plan to rebuild community with AI is a noble test case, but online friends can't bring you soup.

  • In 2017 Mark Zuckerberg pledged to help a billion people join meaningful communities. Harari praises the ambition, while noting that the Cambridge Analytica scandal undercut it.
  • We can really know only about 150 people. Past a point, he argues, intimacy is zero-sum: online friends cost you time with neighbours.
  • The real test is whether a company would adopt a tool that lowers time spent online, and whether it would pay taxes for the roads and hospitals bodies need.
06Civilization

There is just one civilization

The "clash of civilizations" thesis relies on a false analogy with biology. Human groups change, and they merge.

  • Species have fixed identities. Germans ran through six very different systems in under a century. A civilization, in Harari's view, is whatever its members make of it.
  • Species never merge. Tribes do, and over ten thousand years they have fused into ever larger groups.
  • Traditions are less ancient than claimed. He notes that excavated synagogues from Talmudic times show no gender segregation and include images of women.
  • Identity comes more from shared conflicts than shared traits. A European of 1618 was defined by arguing over doctrine, and one of 2018 by arguing over immigration.

Where almost everyone agrees

PoliticsAbout 200 statesSame protocols, from Rio's 11,000 athletes to near-identical anthems.
EconomicsOne dollarThe Islamic State destroyed symbols but kept the banknotes.
MedicineSame hospitalsA thousand years ago treatment depended on where you lived.
PhysicsSame equationsRivals worry about each other's bombs because they share the science.

Harari's thought experiment: try holding the Olympics in 1016. Today's contest between nations is itself a global consensus.

07Nationalism

Global problems need global answers

Nations were built because tribes couldn't tame the Nile. Harari argues that nations are now in the tribes' position.

  • Nationalism isn't natural. Loyalty to millions of strangers is recent and takes immense effort. He still credits mild patriotism as benevolent, and notes that failed states tend to lack it.
  • After 1945, nuclear fear pushed nations toward an internationalist order, and he rates the resulting decline of war as perhaps our era's top political and moral success.
  • Climate change is already under way, with tipping points ahead. No state is ecologically sovereign: Kiribati could cut emissions to zero and still drown.
  • Nations stand to gain or lose differently, so interests diverge. Russia might even benefit. Harari suggests that nationalist denial of climate change partly reflects the lack of any national answer.
  • Technology may help. The first lab-grown burger cost $330,000 in 2013, and four years of work cut that to $11.
  • Globalising politics needn't mean world government. He means that domestic politics should take global problems far more seriously.

Three threats that feed each other

We have a global ecology, economy and science, Harari writes, but only national politics. Failure on one front alone could be catastrophic.

08Religion

God now serves the nation

Harari finds traditional religions largely irrelevant to technical and policy problems. Where they do matter, on identity, he sees them as more problem than remedy.

Three kinds of problem

TechnicalHow should farmers cope with drought? Priests' true skill was explaining why the rain dance failed. Scientists admit failure and try again.

Largely irrelevant

PolicyHow do we prevent warming? Leaders find answers in economists, then justify them from scripture. He sees no "Muslim economics".

Largely irrelevant

IdentityWhose problems should I care about? Rituals draw the line between "us" and "them".

Highly relevant
  • Mass cooperation needs mass identities, and those rest on shared fictions. So religions don't bring rain, but they do help decide whom to cure and whom to bomb.
  • Harari's model case is Japan, which modernised fast while inventing an official state Shinto. He wryly calls kamikaze pilots the first precision-guided missiles.
  • He sees the pattern elsewhere, most extremely in North Korea's Juche.
09Immigration

Some cultures might be better than others

Harari treats the European debate as a deal with three terms, so that people can at least see what they disagree about.

The immigration deal and its four debates

Debate 4 can't be settled until the first three are. Both sides, he adds, count violations far more than compliance.

  • He sets out the pro- and anti-immigration case at each stage and doesn't give a verdict. He says neither camp should be painted as villains, and the choice belongs to democratic procedure.
  • Forty years is short for a society and an eternity for a person. Harari's image is a eucalyptus planted in France: an invader to botanists, French to the tree.
  • He argues racism has lost its scientific basis and a new thing has taken its place, culturism. His invented countries Coldia and Warmland show how different conflict styles can cost immigrants promotions.
  • He gives culturist claims more standing than racist ones, with three common flaws: mistaking local for objective superiority, being too vague, and prejudging individuals from statistics.
Part III · Chapters 10–14

Despair and Hope

The challenges are unprecedented, but Harari thinks we can rise to them if we manage our fears and hold our views more humbly.

10Terrorism

Don't panic

Terrorists can't do much material damage, so they stage theatre and count on us to do the damage ourselves.

The fly in the china shop

1 · The flyA small attackToo weak to move a teacup, it stages a spectacle.
2 · The earFear spreadsMedia and imagination amplify it.
3 · The bullThe state overreactsIt answers with a theatre of security.
4 · The wreckageCards reshuffledThe storm may hand terrorists an ace.
  • Why it works: the modern state promises a public sphere free of political violence. In that silence even a small act is loud, as a lone coin rattles in an empty jar. No medieval king lost his throne to the Black Death, because stopping plagues wasn't his job.
  • Harari's three fronts are clandestine action against networks, a media that keeps perspective, and citizens who free their imagination.
  • Nuclear or biological terrorism would be a different matter. But that hypothetical doesn't justify overreacting to today's attacks.
11War

Never underestimate human stupidity

Major powers seem to have lost the art of winning wars. That protects us, but only up to the limit of human foolishness.

  • In 1914 elites knew what a profitable war looked like. The US war with Mexico, begun in 1846, added 888,000 square miles for about 13,000 American dead.
  • Today's main assets are knowledge and institutions, which can't be looted. His line: Silicon Valley has no silicon mines.
  • Nuclear and cyber weapons are high-damage, low-profit tools. They can destroy countries but can't build profitable empires.
  • Still, Germany and Japan prospered after defeat, so going to war in the 1930s was a miscalculation. Assuming war is inevitable would be self-fulfilling. Assuming it's impossible would be naive.
12Humility

You are not the center of the world

Every group thinks history revolves around it. Harari punctures his own people's version and invites readers to do the same with theirs.

  • Morality, art and creativity are universal human abilities, older than any nation. He gives examples of ethics in chimpanzees: Freddy, an alpha male, adopted an orphan unrelated to him.
  • He argues that biblical morality wasn't original, pointing to Hammurabi, Egyptian texts, Confucius, Buddha and Mahavira. He treats Judaism's role in history as modest, like that of Freud's mother in modern thought.
  • He is blunt about monotheism, calling it arguably one of the worst ideas ethically, because it encouraged intolerance. His contrast is Ashoka's edict of tolerance around 250 BCE with the Theodosian Decrees of 391.
  • He credits Jewish scientists' extraordinary modern contribution (about 20 percent of science Nobel laureates, under 0.2 percent of the world's population) to individuals rather than to the religion.

13God

Don't take the name of God in vain

Harari says the word covers two very different things, and that the believer's argument swaps one for the other.

The cosmic mystery
  • Why is there something, not nothing?
  • We can say nothing concrete about it
  • The God of late-night campfire talk
The worldly lawgiver
  • Rules on food, dress, sex and politics
  • We supposedly know exactly what He wants
  • The God of crusades and inquisitions
  • Holy books supply the missing link, and he finds no evidence for it. The deeper the mystery, he says, the less likely it cares about dress codes.
  • He reads the third commandment as a ban on using God to justify politics or hatred.
  • Religion can inspire compassion. He cites Ted McIlvenna, the Methodist minister behind a pioneering 1964 dialogue with gay and lesbian activists.
  • But morality doesn't need gods. It means reducing suffering, and it is natural to social mammals. Anger harms the angry person first.
14Secularism

Acknowledge your shadow

Secularism is a positive code, not an absence of belief. Its risk is a bar set so high that movements harden into dogma.

The secular code

TruthCompassionEqualityFreedomCourageResponsibility

None of these is exclusive to secular people. In Harari's account, a Muslim feminist or Christian environmentalist is a full member, so long as the code prevails where it clashes with doctrine.

  • Emergencies demand slogans, so inquiry hardens: Stalinism, faith in growth, belief that elections fix failed states.
  • He calls human rights a dogma, and probably the most beneficial one in history. Yet it may not equip us for superhumans and cyborgs.
  • Every creed has a shadow. Science's advantage is that it can in principle admit mistakes, which is why undogmatic movements promise modest change.
  • His test for anyone who wants to lead: name your creed's worst mistake. If you can't, he won't trust you.
Part IV · Chapters 15–18

Truth

If you feel overwhelmed, Harari says, you're on the right track. So how do you avoid being misled?

15Ignorance

You know less than you think

Our power comes from thinking in groups, and the rational individual is a myth.

  • Asked to explain a zipper step by step, most confident people couldn't. Steven Sloman and Philip Fernbach call this the knowledge illusion: we count what others know as our own.
  • Views follow group loyalty, so throwing facts at people tends to backfire. Scientists' faith in facts may itself be groupthink.
  • Power distorts truth like a black hole. Harari recalls a dinner with Benjamin Netanyahu at which about thirty guests jostled for attention and no secrets emerged.
  • Finding truth means wasting time on the mostly nonsensical periphery, which leaders can't afford. The best anyone can do is admit ignorance, as Socrates did.
16Justice

Our sense of justice might be out of date

Morality evolved for a few dozen foragers. It can't trace cause and effect across billions.

  • A forager knew who made her moccasins. Harari can't say where his lunch comes from. Is a passive shareholder in a polluter guilty of stealing a river?
  • In a connected world he makes the imperative to know supreme. Good intentions fall short, as with the upright postmaster serving the Nazi state.

Four ways we escape complexity

DownsizeTwo foragersPicture a civil war as one good person against one bad.
PersonaliseOne childPeople gave more to one sick child than to eight.
ConspireTwenty puppet-mastersHe says nobody understands enough to pull the strings.
DogmatiseAn all-knowing creedComfort and certainty, but is that justice?
17Post-Truth

Some fake news lasts forever

Harari argues that post-truth is nothing new, because shared fictions are how humans cooperate at scale.

  • Propaganda is old. His examples run from Russia's denial of its troops in Crimea in 2014 back through Manchukuo and terra nullius, and to Zionist and Israeli denials of a Palestinian people.
  • He provokes deliberately, saying that what a billion people believe for a thousand years gets called religion. He adds that fictions can be beneficial: large-scale cooperation, and Chartres Cathedral.
  • Power needs a balance. Distort reality too far and bullets don't turn to water, as in the Maji Rebellion of 1905. Stick to bare facts and few will follow.
  • None of this means that everything is fake. Under the fiction, he insists, the suffering is real, and some sources do honestly try.
The story

In 1255 a boy called Hugh was found dead in a well in Lincoln. Rumour blamed the local Jews, and nineteen were executed. England expelled its Jews in 1290. Hugh's tomb drew pilgrims for centuries, and the cathedral repudiated the libel only in 1955.

Rule of thumb 1
  • Pay for reliable information. If news is free, you may be the product.
Rule of thumb 2
  • If an issue really matters to you, read the scientific literature.
18Science Fiction

The movies are misleading us about AI

Science fiction now shapes how most people understand AI, so its errors matter politically.

  • Its worst sin is to confuse intelligence with consciousness. Harari fears a superhuman elite more than a robot war, and thinks Marx a better guide than Spielberg here.
  • The Matrix and The Truman Show assume an authentic self waiting outside the box. He says the brain and society are themselves boxes, and that escape only finds a bigger one.
  • He finds more realism in Pixar's Inside Out (2015), where Riley has no single core self, only interacting mechanisms.
  • He calls Huxley's Brave New World (written in 1931) the most prophetic of all: control through pleasure is more secure than control through fear, and it's hard even to say what's wrong with it.
Part V · Chapters 19–21

Resilience

How do you live in an age of bewilderment, between the old stories and whatever comes next?

19Education

Change is the only constant

Nobody knows what skills 2050 will need. So teach people how to keep reinventing themselves.

  • A parent in China in 1018 could safely teach rice farming or weaving. Harari says today's parents have no such confidence, because even bodies and minds may change.
  • Schools cram information, which is no longer scarce. What's needed is the skill to sift it and build a broad picture.
  • He reports that many educators favour the four Cs over technical skills: critical thinking, communication, collaboration and creativity.
  • The old split between learning and working is breaking down, so mental flexibility and emotional balance become the core skills.

20Meaning

Life is not a story

The book's longest chapter takes apart the stories we use for meaning, then asks the better question: how do we stop suffering?

  • A meaningful story needs only two things: a role for me, and a reach beyond my horizon. It needn't be true or complete. Attention stays inside a safe zone, and nobody asks what the elephant stands on.
  • He runs through the Circle of Life (Arjuna, Simba), linear epics (Islam, Zionism, communism), reincarnation, legacy, kindness and romance. In his view each is incomplete or has little evidence behind it.
  • Stories are held up by the weight of their roof: the identities and institutions built on them.
  • Ritual makes them feel real. "Hocus-pocus, X is Y" turns bread into a body and cloth into a nation. India's 2017 flag at Attari kept tearing and being repaired, at public cost.
  • Most people keep a portfolio of stories. After 1945, he notes, about 80 percent of gauleiters and 90 percent of generals went on living. Fascism, by contrast, demands one identity only, and he warns that it looks beautiful in its own mirror.
  • The liberal story says that I create meaning by free choice. He counters that we can do what we desire but can't choose what to desire. The self is itself a story our minds keep rewriting.
  • Buddhism goes further, denying any meaning at all, but "no story" easily becomes a story too. Burma's king stormed Siam's capital in 1767 and later gilded a pagoda with his weight in gold.

How small is "eternal"?

Harari uses these numbers against the claim that Jerusalem is "eternal", and says other national movements are just as narrow. The scale is logarithmic.

The alchemy of sacrifice

I suffer for it
  • Either the story is true, or I'm a gullible fool.
I hurt others for it
  • Either the story is true, or I'm a cruel villain.

Few people accept either label, so faith deepens. The logic is a fallacy, Harari says, yet it's why suffering makes fictions feel real. He sees the same thing in a costly Ferrari.

The test: can the hero suffer?

A nation has no body, so it can't feel pain. The people in it can. To tell fiction from reality, begin with suffering.

Sound the alarm at four words
sacrificeeternitypurityredemption

Translate them into real terms: a soldier in agony, a child shaking in fear.

21Meditation

Just observe

Harari ends by showing the tint in his own glasses, while warning that what works for him won't work for everyone.

  • Frustrated by philosophy, he took a ten-day Vipassana course in April 2000, at 24, taught by S. N. Goenka. The instruction was simply to notice the breath, with no theory to accept.
  • He couldn't hold focus for more than ten seconds, and learned that he barely controlled his own mind. He wasn't the CEO, just a poor gatekeeper.
  • We react to bodily sensations, not to events. The insult hurts as a burn in the stomach. His main discovery was that the deepest source of his suffering lay in the patterns of his own mind.
  • Since then he has meditated two hours a day, plus a retreat of a month or two each year. He credits the practice for the focus to write Sapiens and Homo Deus.
  • We can still investigate who we are, he closes, but we should do it now, before algorithms make up our minds for us.

Digging from both ends

Scanners record brain activity, not experience. Studying a meditator's brain is like examining a microscope through a magnifying glass, Harari says. And if brain and mind are different things, digging into the mind matters even more.

Takeaways

The toolkit

Harari offers no programme, and he's wary of simple answers. These are habits I'd take from the book.

Thinking

Swap panic for bewildermentTry saying "I don't understand what's going on" before predicting the end.
Audit your favourite sourcesYou can't check everything, so check what you rely on most, and pay for it.
Read the science on what mattersIf you reject a theory, know it first and bring evidence.

Judging

Ask whether the hero can sufferLook past nations and causes to the people who feel pain.
Hear the four alarm wordsTranslate "sacrifice", "eternity", "purity" and "redemption" into real terms.
Ask what a creed got wrongDistrust anyone who can't name a serious mistake, your own side included.

Living

Know what you want from technologyOtherwise it will decide your aims for you.
Keep several identitiesFamily, work, nation and humankind can all claim you. Be wary of anything that demands exclusive loyalty.
Watch your own sensationsNotice what anger feels like in the body, not only what caused it.
