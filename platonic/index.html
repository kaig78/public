Platonic

How attachment science helps you make and keep friends

Psychologist Marisa G. Franco argues that friendship is one of the strongest forces in a healthy life, that adults have to build it on purpose, and that your attachment style explains much of why it feels hard.

8 chapters2 parts6 practices
Introduction

Friends don't just happen to adults

Most of us learned that friendship happens by itself and that romance is what really counts. Franco thinks both ideas are wrong, and that they leave many adults lonelier than they need to be.

Her case has two halves. Looking back explains why friendship matters and how early relationships shaped the way we connect. Looking forward turns attachment research into six practices you can use with real people.

Knowing isn't the goal. Franco keeps asking readers to act: say hello despite the fear of rejection, make room for friends in a full week, and show up differently.

Part I

Looking back

How we became the friends we are.

01Why it matters

Friendship is the underdog of relationships

Our culture ranks romance and family first, yet friends quietly shape our health, our empathy and who we think we are.

  • Loss taught her. Franco opens with a breakup. She had believed that life would really begin once she found romantic love. A weekly wellness group with close friends healed her instead, and she stopped treating friendship as second place.
  • A health intervention. Meta-analyses put the drop in mortality risk at 23 to 30 percent for exercise, up to 24 percent for diet and 45 percent for a large social network.
  • The world looks easier. In one study people judged a hill as less steep when they stood with a friend. In another, men rated a threat as less imposing when friends were present.
  • Friends ask less of us. Unlike parents, they don't need us to live out their hopes. Unlike spouses, they aren't expected to be everything. That freedom is why they can help us find out who we are.
02Attachment

The past walks into every friendship

Early relationships teach us what to expect from people, and we carry those expectations until new experiences change them.

  • Three patterns. Secure people assume others mean well and adjust if the evidence says otherwise. Anxious people expect to be left, so they overgive or watch for signs of rejection. Avoidant people expect to be let down, so they keep their distance and call it independence.
  • Not a life sentence. Studies disagree on how stable attachment style is, with figures from 72 percent down to 26 percent keeping the same style from infancy to adulthood. Change is possible, and Franco builds the rest of the book on it.
  • Security can be primed. People reminded of someone loving handled social exclusion with less physical stress. Insecure people who wrote about such a person then reported being better at reaching out.
  • Why it is worth it. Moving toward security means giving up the armour that once protected you. Franco calls that hard work, and shows that staying stuck has its own price in loneliness.
SecureEveryone's doing their bestStarts friendships easily, trusts contact will resume, gives with boundaries, raises issues calmly.
AnxiousEveryone will leave meFears rejection, worries when friends go quiet, gives until depleted, ignores issues and then blows up.
AvoidantI don't need anyoneStays shallow, forgets friends out of sight, gives little, and sidesteps problems.
Part II

Looking forward

Six practices for making and keeping friends.

03Taking initiative

Friendship doesn't just happen

School gave us friends automatically. Adult life doesn't, so someone has to start, and it can be you.

  • Luck is the wrong model. Adults lose the repeated, unplanned contact and shared vulnerability that made friends easy in school. Franco says to build those conditions on purpose.
  • The liking gap. After a conversation people systematically underestimate how much the other person liked them. Across the settings studied, the fear of rejection was bigger than the reality.
  • The acceptance prophecy. People who assume they will be liked act warmer and open up more, and so they are liked more. It works in reverse too.
  • Sign up for the class, not the workshop. Mere exposure means familiarity builds liking, so join something that repeats: improv, a writing group, a hiking group, a book club. Then follow up with the people you feel chemistry with.
04Vulnerability

Sharing is how trust gets built

We fear it makes us look weak. Research keeps finding that others see it as honesty and courage.

  • Hiding has costs. Students who suppressed their emotions got less social support in their first year of college. Bereaved spouses who talked less about the death were less healthy the following year.
  • Others judge you less than you think. In one study people disclosed insecurities and still underestimated how much their partner liked them, the liking gap again.
  • Go first, but scaffold. Don't wait for a friend to open up. Start with someone you trust, then widen the circle. Don't overshare, and stop being vulnerable with people who have used it against you.
  • Self-compassion makes it possible. Be kind to yourself, notice painful feelings without over- or underreacting, and remember that failing is part of being human.
05Authenticity

Be who you are when you're not under threat

Authenticity isn't saying everything you think. It is the self underneath your defences, and it tends to be kinder than the defences are.

  • Presence, not impulse. Blurting out your reflex is usually a defence mechanism. Franco defines authenticity as staying present and choosing your higher self.
  • Pause and breathe. Notice the trigger, don't indulge it, and turn your attention from defending yourself to the person in front of you. That also eases social anxiety.
  • Don't make rejection mean something about you. People who felt more authentic in one study felt less rejected. Separate what happened from your verdict on yourself.
  • Unequal friendships. Being yourself costs more for people from disadvantaged groups, some of whom code-switch to stay safe. Franco asks people with privilege to practise adjusted mutuality: listen, repeat back, ask, don't argue or play devil's advocate.
06Anger

Conflict can bring friends closer

Silent drift ends more friendships than arguments do. Handled well, a hard talk deepens the bond.

  • Open up or end it? First zoom out. Ask what this friendship gives you that nothing else does and whether it helps or harms overall.
  • The sequence. Calm your feelings. Say how much the friendship matters. Share your world with "I feel". Ask for their view. Soothe your triggers, and de-escalate when needed.
  • Split into two selves. Watch the part of you that wants to counterattack, and let the other part choose. People who are good at this were more popular and less lonely and depressed in one study.
  • When you are the one at fault. Validate their concern and commit to something you will really do. A mistake is normal and doesn't measure your worth. A conflict is over when nobody holds resentment, which can take a few rounds.
07Generosity

Give without losing yourself

Giving bonds us, but giving to earn love burns us out.

  • Check the motive. Give because you love someone, not because you want them to start loving you. Anxious volunteers in one study gave mainly to feel better about themselves.
  • Toxic generosity. Unmitigated givers were more depressed, because they couldn't ask for help or state their needs. Franco separates generosity from fawning.
  • Enlightened self-interest. Look for win-wins. Ask for what you need to keep giving, because boundaries keep a friendship going.
  • Give in proportion. The more contacts we have, the less time each gets. Keep deep mutuality for friends who reciprocate. If someone only calls in a crisis and ignores your limits, that is dependency, not friendship.
08Affection

Say it out loud

We hold back warmth because we expect it to be awkward. It lands far better than we predict.

  • Feeling valued is what people want. In one study being entertaining ranked as the least important quality in a friend. Feeling good about yourself around them ranked highest.
  • The awkwardness is imagined. Writers of gratitude letters misjudged how happy and how awkward the recipients would feel.
  • Warmth makes people feel safe. Affection tells others it is safe to invest in you, and it makes difficult conversations easier.
  • Fit it to the friend, and learn to receive. Ask how they like to feel appreciated. When someone is kind to you, assume they mean it. Rituals such as a yearly Friendsgiving keep affection regular.
Takeaways

The toolkit

Franco ends by asking for one thing: stop waiting for a crisis to show you what friends are worth, and make being a good friend part of who you are.

To make friends

Assume people like youWhen a talk feels bad, ask what they actually did. Without evidence, drop the story.
Join something that repeatsA weekly class beats a one-off event, because familiarity builds liking.
Follow upDon't hide in the corner. Reach out to people near you or those you click with.

To go deeper

Be vulnerable firstStart with someone safe, and let your voice shake if it does.
Pause before you reactBreathe, notice the trigger, and turn your attention to the other person.
Raise it, don't driftSay what the friendship means to you, then "I feel", then ask for their view.

To keep them

Give from loveSet limits so that your giving can last, and look for win-wins.
Say the warm thingTell them what they mean to you. They will mind it less than you fear.
Build ritualsRegular dinners or a yearly Friendsgiving keep contact from depending on memory.
